Most people buy an estate plan the way they buy a smoke detector: they grab the cheapest option, feel protected, and never think about it again. The problem is that the cheapest option often creates the most expensive bill — just not for you. It lands on your family, years later, in probate court. An estate planning attorney is not selling paper. They are selling the difference between a $900 trust package today and a $23,000 probate bill your kids pay later. This guide runs that comparison with real numbers so you can see which side of the math you are on.

## What an estate planning attorney actually charges in 2026

Estate planning is one of the few legal services still sold mostly on flat fees, which is good news: you can know the price before you sit down. Here is what those flat fees look like across the country right now.

**Simple will package: $300–$1,200.** This typically includes the will itself, a durable power of attorney, a healthcare proxy, and a living will — the "big four" documents (more on those below). Solo practitioners in smaller markets cluster around $300–$600; firms in major metros charge $800–$1,200. The range mostly reflects cost of living, not quality: a $450 will from a licensed attorney who drafts fifty of these a month beats a $1,100 will from a generalist who does two a year.

**Revocable living trust package: $1,500–$4,000.** This includes the trust agreement, a "pour-over" will that catches anything you forgot to transfer into the trust, the same power-of-attorney and healthcare documents, and — critically — the funding work: retitling your house, bank accounts, and brokerage accounts into the trust's name. A trust that is never funded is an expensive paperweight, so ask whether funding is included or billed extra. Some firms quote $1,500 for the documents and then bill $150–$300 per asset to retitle things; others wrap it all in one number.

**Complex estates: $4,000–$10,000+.** If your estate is near or above the federal estate-tax exemption (about $14 million per person in 2026), if you own a business, rental properties in multiple states, or have a blended family with children from different marriages, expect customized planning with tax trusts, LLC structuring, or special-needs provisions. That is genuinely complex work, and the fee reflects it.

**Hourly rates: $200–$500 per hour.** Some attorneys bill hourly for unusual situations — contested beneficiary designations, business succession planning, cleaning up a DIY trust that was done wrong. Ask for a written estimate either way.

The headline number everyone quotes is the upfront fee. It is also the least important number. What matters is the lifetime total: what you pay now **plus** what your family pays after you die. That is where wills and trusts split apart.

## Will vs trust: the lifetime cost equation

Here is the math almost no one shows you. A will **guarantees** probate. A funded trust usually **avoids** it. Probate is the court process that validates a will, pays your debts, and distributes your property — and it has its own fee schedule that dwarfs what you paid for the documents.

**The equation:** Lifetime cost = attorney fee now + probate cost later (for a will) or funding maintenance (for a trust).

**Example 1 — Will plan.** You pay $600 for a will package today. You die with a $450,000 estate: a house, retirement accounts, a car. In a state with statutory probate fees like California, the court process alone costs roughly:

- Attorney fees set by state statute: about **$13,000** on a $450,000 estate (California's formula: 4% of the first $100,000, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000)
- Court filing and publication costs: **$1,000–$2,000**
- Appraisal and executor fees: **$2,000–$4,000**
- Total probate cost: roughly **$16,000–$19,000**, paid from the estate over 9–18 months while your family waits

Your $600 will just produced a ~$17,000 bill. The documents were cheap; the process they trigger is not.

**Example 2 — Trust plan.** You pay $2,500 for a revocable living trust package today, and the attorney helps you retitle the house and accounts into the trust. You die with the same $450,000 estate. Your successor trustee — usually your spouse or adult child — distributes everything privately, no court, no public record, typically in weeks. Total cost at death: a few hundred dollars for death certificates and maybe an hour of attorney time to confirm the funding held.

| Plan | You pay now | Family pays later | Timeline for family |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will package | $600 | ~$16,000–$19,000 | 9–18 months in court |
| Funded trust package | $2,500 | ~$300–$800 | Weeks, no court |

The trust costs four times more upfront and saves roughly fifteen thousand dollars on the back end. That is the entire sales pitch for a trust, and it is arithmetic, not opinion.

## But probate costs are not the same everywhere

The example above uses California, which has some of the priciest statutory probate fees in the country. Your state's math is different, and it changes the answer.

**Expensive-probate states** (where a trust usually pays for itself): California, Florida, New York, Illinois, Pennsylvania. Florida probate attorneys can charge up to 3% of the estate for ordinary services by statute; on a $450,000 estate that is another ~$13,500 before court costs. In these states, almost anyone who owns a home should be doing trust math seriously.

**Moderate-probate states**: Texas, Arizona, Colorado. Texas has relatively streamlined independent probate administration — attorney fees of $3,000–$7,000 are common on midsize estates, and the process moves faster. A trust still helps, but the savings gap is smaller.

**Cheap-probate states** (where a will is often fine): many small-estate states let modest estates skip formal probate entirely. Most states have a "small estate affidavit" threshold — commonly $50,000–$100,000 in non-real-estate assets — that lets heirs collect property with paperwork instead of court. If you rent, have no real estate, and your accounts all name beneficiaries, probate may cost your family almost nothing, and a $500 will package is perfectly rational.

This is why the first question an ([estate planning attorney](/estate-planning-attorney)) should ask you is not "will or trust?" but "what do you own, and what state are you in?" If they pitch a $3,500 trust before asking those two questions, get a second opinion. Browse ([attorneys in your state](/attorneys/california)) to compare how firms frame the choice — the ones that lead with the state-specific math are the ones worth calling.

## The four documents that are actually in the package

Whether you buy a will plan or a trust plan, the core package contains the same four documents, and three of them protect **you while you are alive** — which surprises most first-time clients.

1. **The will (or the pour-over will).** Directs where your property goes and names guardians for minor children. With a trust plan, the will is a backup: anything you forgot to put in the trust "pours over" into it at death.

2. **Durable power of attorney.** Names someone to handle your finances — pay bills, manage accounts, sell property — if you become incapacitated. Without it, your family needs a court-appointed guardianship, which costs $3,000–$10,000 and takes months. This single document is arguably more important than the will for anyone under 60, because incapacity is more likely than death.

3. **Healthcare proxy (healthcare power of attorney).** Names someone to make medical decisions if you cannot. Hospitals will not take "I'm his daughter" as authority for big decisions without it.

4. **Living will / advance directive.** States your wishes about life support and end-of-life care, so your proxy is not guessing — or fighting with siblings about what you would have wanted.

When you compare quotes, compare the full package, not the headline document. A $300 "will" that omits the power of attorney and healthcare proxy is not cheaper than a $600 package that includes all four — it is incomplete.

## When the $199 online will is enough (and when it is a trap)

Online will-makers are not scams. For a narrow set of people, they are the correct purchase:

- You are under 40, healthy, rent your home, and your main assets are retirement accounts and life insurance with named beneficiaries (those pass outside probate anyway).
- Your family situation is simple: married, kids together, no ex-spouses, no estranged relatives, no special-needs dependents.
- Your state's small-estate threshold covers everything else you own.

The trap is that online templates cannot tell you which category you are in. The five situations where a template will quietly fail you:

1. **You own a home in an expensive-probate state** — the template never warns you about the $15,000+ probate bill.
2. **Blended families** — "leave everything to my spouse" can accidentally disinherit your children from a first marriage, depending on state law. Attorneys fix this with specific trust provisions; templates do not ask.
3. **Beneficiary designations contradict the will** — your 401(k) still names your ex from 2012? The beneficiary form wins, and the template never checks.
4. **Small business ownership** — who runs the company on Monday morning? A template will not draft a succession clause.
5. **You moved states** — wills are generally portable, but powers of attorney and healthcare documents vary by state, and community-property vs. common-law rules (looking at you, ([Texas](/attorneys/texas)) and ([California](/attorneys/california))) change what "mine" even means.

Rule of thumb: if your net worth including your home is under ~$200,000 and your family tree has no branches, a reputable online will plus correctly named beneficiaries is defensible. Above that — or with any complexity — the attorney's fee is the cheapest insurance in the whole transaction.

## Red flags when you are hiring

Estate planning has more than its share of seminar-driven sales tactics. Watch for these:

- **The free dinner seminar that ends with a $4,000 trust for everyone.** If the "consultation" is a group presentation and the recommendation is identical for every attendee, you are buying a product, not advice.
- **Annual "trust review" subscriptions you did not ask for.** Some firms sell $300–$500/year maintenance plans. Reviews every 3–5 years, or after major life events (marriage, divorce, birth, a move, a big asset change), are what you actually need — not an annual subscription.
- **Refusal to quote a flat fee.** For standard will and trust packages, flat fees are the industry norm. An attorney who will only bill hourly for a routine package is either disorganized or padding.
- **No funding help with a trust.** Ask directly: "Do you retitle my house and accounts into the trust, or is that on me?" An unfunded trust is the most common expensive failure in estate planning — studies of trust audits routinely find a large share of trusts partially or fully unfunded.
- **No questions about your beneficiaries.** If the attorney never asks to review your retirement account and life insurance beneficiary designations, they are drafting in the dark. Those designations override your will.

Good signs: they ask about your assets and your state before recommending anything, they explain probate costs in your state unprompted, they put the fee in writing, and they schedule a funding appointment — not just a signing appointment.

## The decision in one page

If you want the whole guide compressed into a decision tree:

1. **List what you own** — house, accounts, business, insurance. Write down approximate values.
2. **Check your state's probate reality.** Expensive-probate state + homeowner = do the trust math seriously. Small estate + renter = a will package is probably fine.
3. **Check your family complexity.** Blended family, special-needs child, business, property in two states — any one of these pushes you toward an attorney-drafted trust regardless of net worth.
4. **Get two quotes.** Call two firms, ask for the flat fee for the full four-document package, and ask each one: "Given my assets and my state, will or trust — and show me the math." The one that actually shows you the math is the one to hire.
5. **Fund the trust.** If you buy one, finish the job. Sign the deeds. Retitle the accounts. Update the beneficiaries. An unfunded trust is a donation to your attorney. (Retitling the house is itself a small closing, with its own deed and recording charges — the [cost of a real estate lawyer at closing](/blog/real-estate-attorney-fees-closing-cost-math) shows what that side of the paperwork runs.)

Estate planning feels like paperwork about death. It is really paperwork about the living — the people who will be grieving, paying bills, and making decisions in the worst week of their lives. The price of the documents is the smallest number in the whole equation. The price of getting it wrong is the largest. Spend the smaller one deliberately.
