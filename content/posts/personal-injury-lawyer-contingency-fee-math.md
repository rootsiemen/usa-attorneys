You've seen the billboards. "No fee unless we win." It sounds like a free trial for legal help. It is not. A contingency fee is a financing arrangement, and like any financing arrangement, the fine print decides who actually walks away with the money — you or the law firm. This guide runs the real math so you can read a fee agreement the way a lender reads a loan.


![Calculator and forms used to estimate legal costs — personal injury contingency fee math](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1554224155-6726b3ff858f?w=1200&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)

## What a contingency fee actually is

A contingency fee means your lawyer's payment is contingent on a result. No settlement, no verdict, no fee. You pay nothing up front. In exchange, the lawyer takes a percentage of whatever you recover.

Standard personal injury contingency fees in 2026 fall in these bands:

- **33.3% (one-third):** the industry baseline for cases that settle before a lawsuit is filed
- **36–40%:** cases that settle after a lawsuit is filed but before trial
- **40–45%:** cases that go to trial or appeal

Ethics rules require the percentage to be in writing before representation starts. Most state bar associations cap or scrutinize fees above 45% in personal injury matters. If a lawyer quotes 50%, that is a red flag, not a deal.

## The two deductions that change everything

Here is where most clients get blindsided. Your take-home is not "settlement minus percentage." There are two categories of deductions, and the order they are applied in changes your payout by thousands:

1. **The contingency fee itself** — the lawyer's percentage.
2. **Case costs (expenses)** — money the firm advanced on your behalf: filing fees ($200–$400), medical records retrieval ($25–$75 per provider), expert witnesses ($3,000–$15,000+ per expert), deposition transcripts ($500–$1,500 per day), court reporters, accident reconstruction ($5,000–$25,000). Simple soft-tissue cases run $500–$2,000 in costs. Trial-bound cases routinely pass $25,000.

The critical question: are costs deducted **before or after** the percentage is calculated? The two formulas give very different answers. That is the whole game, and we run it in the next two sections.

## Worked example 1: a $100,000 car accident settlement, costs taken off the top

Assumptions: 33.3% fee, $3,500 in case costs (medical records, one deposition, filing fees), no medical liens.

- Settlement: $100,000
- Minus case costs: $100,000 − $3,500 = $96,500
- Attorney fee (33.3% of $96,500): **$32,167**
- Your take-home: $96,500 − $32,167 = **$64,333**

This is the **net fee** structure — the one that favors you. Costs come out first, so the lawyer's percentage is calculated on a smaller base.

## Worked example 2: the same $100,000, fee taken first

Same numbers, different order — the **gross fee** structure, which is still legal in many states and buried in plenty of fee agreements:

- Settlement: $100,000
- Attorney fee (33.3% of $100,000): **$33,333**
- Minus case costs: $100,000 − $33,333 − $3,500 = **$63,167** to you

Difference between the two: **$1,166** in your pocket. On this case it looks modest. Scale it up and it is not modest at all.

## Worked example 3: a $500,000 settlement with a 40% trial fee

Trial was necessary. Costs ballooned to $28,000 (two experts, depositions, reconstruction). Medical liens of $40,000 sit on the settlement (the hospital and your health insurer get paid from the recovery before anyone else).

Gross structure (fee first):

- Attorney fee: 40% × $500,000 = **$200,000**
- Case costs: −$28,000
- Medical liens: −$40,000
- You: $500,000 − $200,000 − $28,000 − $40,000 = **$232,000**

Net structure (costs first):

- After costs: $500,000 − $28,000 = $472,000
- Attorney fee: 40% × $472,000 = **$188,800**
- Medical liens: −$40,000
- You: $500,000 − $188,800 − $28,000 − $40,000 = **$243,200**

**Difference: $11,200.** Same case, same lawyer, same settlement — $11,200 moved from the firm's pocket to yours purely by the order of subtraction. This is the single most valuable line in your fee agreement, and most clients never ask about it.

## The sliding scale: why the percentage changes mid-case

Most contingency agreements use a sliding scale tied to how far the case goes:

| Case stage | Typical fee |
|---|---|
| Settles before lawsuit filed | 33.3% |
| Settles after suit filed, before trial | 36–40% |
| Goes to trial | 40% |
| Appeal | 45% |

The logic: each stage costs the firm more labor and more risk. But notice the perverse incentive baked into this table. A lawyer holding a $60,000 pre-suit offer earns $20,000 at 33.3%. If the case settles six months later for $75,000 at 40%, the lawyer earns $30,000 — a $10,000 raise for one more filing — while you net $45,000 versus $40,000, only $5,000 more for the wait and the added risk of trial.

Ethics rules require the lawyer to follow your instruction on settlement. But when your lawyer's percentage jumps and yours barely moves, ask directly: "Show me the math on both paths." The table above lets you do that math yourself.

One more number most clients never see: the firm's break-even. A firm with $2 million in annual overhead needs roughly 200 pre-suit settlements at $10,000 in fees each just to keep the lights on. That is why firms gravitate toward volume at the 33.3% tier and cherry-pick high-damage cases for trial — the economics push them toward fast settlements on ordinary cases and maximal aggression on catastrophic ones. Your case sits somewhere on that spectrum, and knowing which end tells you how much personal attention you are likely to get.

## Medical liens: the third deduction nobody explains

After the fee and costs come **liens** — claims other parties hold against your settlement:

- **Health insurance subrogation:** if your insurer paid $30,000 for your crash injuries, it can claim reimbursement from your settlement under most plans.
- **Medicare/Medicaid:** federal law gives Medicare a super-lien. It must be resolved before disbursement, and Medicare's recovery can take months to finalize.
- **Hospital liens:** many states let hospitals lien the settlement directly.

A good injury lawyer does not just accept liens — they **negotiate them down**. Reducing a $30,000 hospital lien to $18,000 is worth $12,000 to you, and it costs the lawyer nothing but a few phone calls. Ask during the consultation: "How do you handle lien reduction?" If the answer is vague, that is a signal. Medicare adds its own wrinkle: federal regulations require conditional-payment resolution before disbursement, and Medicare's recovery process routinely takes 60–120 days after settlement — a delay your lawyer should disclose up front so you are not surprised when the check takes three months instead of three weeks. ## When contingency is the wrong deal for you

Contingency fees are not always the cheapest option. Two situations where you should negotiate or walk:

1. **Small, simple cases.** If your damages are $8,000 and the case will settle in one demand letter, a 33.3% fee ($2,667) for perhaps ten hours of work is an effective hourly rate of $267 — fine. But if the same case needs fifty hours, the lawyer is underpaid, and you should expect the firm to deprioritize you. Conversely, if the facts are so clean that settlement is near-certain, negotiate a flat fee or a lower percentage. Some firms will take 25% on clear-liability soft-tissue cases if you ask.
2. **You can afford hourly.** On a $500,000 case that resolves in 100 hours, a 40% fee costs you $200,000 — an effective rate of $2,000/hour. If you can front $25,000–$40,000 at a $250–$400 hourly rate, you keep the difference. Most clients cannot front that, which is exactly why contingency exists — but do the math before assuming contingency is your only option.

## The five lines to read before you sign

Pull out the fee agreement and find these five things. If any are missing or vague, ask:

1. **Exact percentage at each stage** — pre-suit, post-filing, trial, appeal. No ranges, no "standard rates."
2. **Gross or net** — is the fee calculated before or after costs are deducted? (Net favors you.)
3. **What counts as a cost** — get the list. Some firms bill for photocopies and mileage; most do not. You should know.
4. **What happens if you lose** — the fee is zero, but are you on the hook for advanced costs? Many firms eat the costs; some do not. This must be in writing.
5. **Lien handling** — does the firm negotiate liens, and is that included or billed separately?

A lawyer who answers these five crisply in the consultation is a lawyer who has nothing to hide in the agreement. A lawyer who waves them off with "it's all standard" is a lawyer you should not hire.
