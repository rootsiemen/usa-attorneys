Every lawyer you've ever hired was legally working for free until one document was signed. The retainer agreement is the contract that turns a consultation into a representation — and it is also the document almost nobody reads. Clients skim to the fee number, sign, and discover months later that the "reasonable retainer" they approved lets the firm bill in 15-minute minimums, charge 1.5% monthly interest on unpaid balances, and keep a lien on the case file until every invoice is paid.

This is not a document you sign on trust. It is a document you negotiate. Below are the 14 clauses that matter most, what each one costs you in real dollars, and the exact lines that let you push back — before you owe anyone anything.

## 1. The scope of representation (what you are actually buying)

The single most litigated sentence in any retainer is the one that says what the lawyer will do. Vague scope — "handle my business dispute" or "represent me in connection with the above matter" — is how $2,500 consultations metastasize into $18,000 bills. A good scope clause names the matter, the forum, and the limits.

Insist on specifics. "Representation in the breach-of-contract dispute between [Your Company] and [Counterparty] through trial in [County] Superior Court, including discovery and motion practice, excluding appeals" is a scope. "Legal services related to the contract matter" is a blank check. Scope exclusions matter as much as inclusions: if appeals, administrative proceedings, or related counterclaims are excluded, you will be asked to sign — and pay for — a second agreement when they appear. Ask now: "What parts of this matter are NOT covered by this fee?"

## 2. The fee structure (and the math behind the headline number)

Three structures dominate, and the headline number tells you less than you think:

**Flat fee.** Common for defined work like business formation, trademark filings, or uncontested matters. The trap: "flat fee" often covers only the lawyer's labor, not filing fees, court costs, expert charges, or postage. A $1,500 "flat fee" business incorporation that excludes the $200–$800 state filing fee, publication costs in states that require them, and registered-agent service is really a $2,200–$2,600 matter. Ask for the all-in number in writing.

**Hourly.** Business lawyers bill $200 to $600 per hour in most US markets; $300 to $450 is the common middle band for experienced counsel, and big-city specialists clear $800. The clause that matters here is the billing increment (see #3) and which timekeepers are assigned (see #6). A $350/hour partner and a $175/hour associate do not produce the same invoice on the same file.

**Contingency or hybrid.** Rare in business work, standard in personal injury. If your matter offers one — employment disputes, some commercial collections — read the percentage triggers. A common structure is 33% if settled before filing, 40% after filing, and 45% if the case goes to trial. That means settling on day one versus day 200 of litigation changes the fee on a $100,000 recovery from $33,000 to $40,000 or more. The clause should also say whether the percentage comes off the gross recovery or the net after costs — a distinction worth thousands (see our breakdown of [personal injury lawyer contingency fee math](/blog/personal-injury-lawyer-contingency-fee-math) for worked examples).

## 3. The billing increment (the clause that quietly doubles small tasks)

This is the most under-read clause in the entire agreement, and it has the biggest silent effect on your bill. Lawyers bill in increments — usually 6 minutes (0.1 hour), 10 minutes, or 15 minutes (0.25 hour). A 3-minute phone call costs 0.25 hours at a firm with 15-minute minimums. At $400/hour, that call costs $100 instead of $20.

Work the math over a year-long matter: 120 short tasks (emails, quick calls, status checks) at an average of 3 actual minutes each. At 6-minute increments you are billed for 12 hours — $4,800 at $400/hour. At 15-minute increments you are billed for 30 hours — $12,000. The same lawyer, the same actual work, a $7,200 difference created entirely by this one clause.

Push for 6-minute increments. If the firm refuses, cap it: "no charge for communications under 2 minutes" or a monthly ceiling on short-task billing. Some firms will agree to 10-minute increments as a compromise — still better than 15.

## 4. The retainer itself (deposit, evergreen, or non-refundable)

"Retainer" means three different things, and the agreement must say which one:

**Advance deposit (refundable).** You deposit money the firm holds in its trust account and bills against. The lawyer earns it only as work is performed. Whatever is unearned is yours back when the matter ends. This is the standard, client-protective structure — and in most states, ethics rules require the unearned portion to sit in a segregated trust account, not the firm's operating account.

**Evergreen retainer.** The deposit auto-replenishes: when the balance drops below a threshold (say, $2,500), you must top it back up within 10–15 days. On a $5,000-evergreen agreement for active litigation, a client can be asked to wire fresh money every few weeks. Know the threshold and the replenishment deadline before you sign — missing one can give the firm grounds to pause work or withdraw.

**Non-refundable retainer.** Some agreements call the initial payment "earned on receipt" or "non-refundable." Treat this as a red flag unless the scope is genuinely fixed. Ethics rules in many states allow non-refundable fees only if they are reasonable and the client agrees in writing after being told they are non-refundable. If the agreement says the retainer is non-refundable, ask what happens if you terminate the representation in week two. The answer should be a pro-rata refund formula. If there isn't one, do not sign.

Worked example: you sign a $7,500 advance-deposit retainer at $350/hour. Month one, the firm bills 12.4 hours ($4,340). Your remaining balance is $3,160 — not gone, not lost, still yours. Month two bills 9 hours ($3,150). Balance: $10. The firm will ask you to replenish before doing more work. Under an evergreen clause set at $5,000, that replenishment demand arrives in writing the moment the balance crosses below the threshold. None of this is a surprise if the clause is clear.

## 5. Costs and expenses (the second invoice you didn't expect)

Legal fees are one line; costs are another. The agreement should list which expenses are billed to you separately: filing fees, court reporters, process servers, expert witnesses, e-discovery vendors, travel, copying, postage. On a business dispute that goes to trial, out-of-pocket costs commonly run $5,000 to $25,000 — on top of fees.

Three things to negotiate here:

- **Pre-approval thresholds.** "No expense over $500 without prior written approval" is the single best sentence you can add to this clause. Expert witnesses charge $300–$800 per hour; a firm that retains one without asking can add five figures to your matter in a week.
- **Markups.** Some firms mark up vendor services — a 10–15% "administrative fee" on top of the court reporter's bill. The agreement should state whether vendors are billed at cost. If it doesn't, assume markup and ask.
- **Interest on advanced costs.** Firms sometimes advance costs and charge interest on the advance. Read the rate: 1% per month (12% APR) on a $10,000 cost advance running six months adds $600. Not ruinous, but it should be disclosed, not discovered.

## 6. Who actually does the work (the staffing clause)

The partner you met in the consultation is not necessarily the person drafting your motions. The agreement should name the responsible attorney and state whether associates, paralegals, or contract lawyers may be assigned. This matters because rate spreads are enormous: partner $400–$600/hour, senior associate $250–$400, junior associate $175–$300, paralegal $100–$175.

Run the staffing math on a 60-hour matter. All-partner at $500/hour: $30,000. A realistic blend — 15 partner hours ($7,500), 25 associate hours at $275 ($6,875), 20 paralegal hours at $125 ($2,500): $16,875. Nearly half the cost disappears with the same firm, same matter, different staffing. Ask for a staffing plan up front: "Which timekeepers will work on this, at what rates, and what share of the hours will each handle?" Get the rates for every named timekeeper in the agreement, not just the partner's.

Also watch for the rate-increase clause. Many agreements let the firm raise hourly rates annually — often 5–10% — with 30 days' notice. On a multi-year matter, a $400 rate becomes $440 in year two and $484 in year three. Ask for a rate lock for the first 12 months, or at least a cap on increases.

## 7. The communication and billing-report terms

The agreement should say how and how often you are billed — monthly itemized invoices are the standard you should demand — and what detail the invoices carry. "Legal services rendered: $4,200" is not an invoice; it is a demand. Itemized bills list each task, the timekeeper, the time in the agreed increment, and the rate. You cannot audit what you cannot see.

Two more communication terms worth having in writing:

- **Response-time expectations.** "The firm will respond to client communications within two business days" is reasonable and worth stating. Silence during a matter is how small problems become expensive ones.
- **Copies of everything.** You are entitled to your file. The agreement should confirm you receive copies of all filings, correspondence, and substantive documents as they are generated — not at the end when the leverage is gone.

## 8. The termination clause (your exit, and its price)

You can fire your lawyer at any time — that right cannot be contracted away. But the agreement sets the price of exercising it. Read this clause before you need it:

- **Fees on termination.** The standard term: you owe fees earned and costs incurred through the termination date. That is fair. What is not fair: a clause that adds a "termination fee," a minimum-fee guarantee, or converts unbilled time to a flat penalty. Strike those.
- **File transfer.** The agreement should confirm your file is transferred to new counsel promptly on request. Some states allow a retaining lien on the file for unpaid fees (see #9) — know your state's rule before you assume.
- **The firm's right to withdraw.** Firms can withdraw for nonpayment, for client conduct that makes representation unreasonably difficult, or when continued representation would violate ethics rules. The clause should require written notice and, in active litigation, court permission where required. Withdrawal mid-case can cost you weeks and a new retainer elsewhere — the notice terms are your early warning.

Practical note: the cheapest time to switch lawyers is early. By month six of litigation, a new firm needs 10–20 hours just to learn the file — $3,500 to $7,000 at typical rates — before doing anything productive. Read the termination clause as a measure of how locked in you are.

## 9. Liens on your file and your recovery

Two lien clauses appear in retainers, and both have teeth:

**Retaining lien.** In many states, a lawyer who hasn't been paid can hold your file — pleadings, correspondence, work product — until the bill is settled. This is legal in most jurisdictions and it is the firm's quietest collection tool. The counterweight: ethics rules generally require the firm to surrender anything you need to avoid prejudice, like upcoming deadlines. Still, negotiate a carve-out in writing: "the firm shall provide copies of all time-sensitive documents regardless of payment status."

**Charging lien.** In contingency and some hourly matters, the agreement may give the firm a lien on any recovery — settlement, judgment, or award — for unpaid fees and costs. This is standard and usually fair; it is how the firm gets paid from the proceeds. The part to read: does the lien cover only this matter's fees, or any outstanding balance you owe the firm across matters? Cross-matter liens turn an unrelated old invoice into a claim on your new settlement. Limit the lien to the matter at hand.

## 10. Interest and late-payment terms

Unpaid balances accrue interest at the rate stated in the agreement — commonly 1% to 1.5% per month (12–18% APR). On a $15,000 balance carried four months at 1.5% monthly, that's roughly $920 in interest. The agreement should also state when interest starts (usually 30 days after the invoice date) and whether the firm can suspend work for nonpayment — most can, after written notice.

This clause cuts both ways. If you dispute an invoice, say so in writing within the stated window (often 15–30 days) and pay the undisputed portion. Silence plus nonpayment is how clients lose leverage and gain interest charges.

## 11. Fee-dispute resolution (where disagreements go)

Every state bar runs a fee-arbitration or fee-dispute program, and many retainers require you to use it before going to court. Read the clause: does it mandate binding arbitration of fee disputes, or merely offer it? Binding arbitration is faster and cheaper than litigation, but you give up the right to a jury and, usually, the right to appeal.

Note the practical reality: fee arbitration heavily favors clients who kept itemized invoices and questioned them in writing. The clients who lose fee disputes are the ones who paid silently for a year and objected only when the total crossed six figures. Monthly review of invoices — ten minutes with a highlighter — is the cheapest legal insurance you will ever buy.

## 12. Confidentiality and conflicts (the fine print that protects you)

The agreement should restate the obvious — your communications are confidential and privileged — and then handle the non-obvious:

- **Conflicts checks.** The firm represents that it has checked for conflicts with the opposing party and found none. If a conflict emerges later (the firm takes on the other side's affiliate in an unrelated matter), the clause should say how it is handled. Ask: "Are you currently representing anyone adverse to me or my company?"
- **Joint representation.** If the agreement covers more than one client — business partners, spouses, co-defendants — it should state that no privilege exists between the joint clients and what happens if their interests diverge. Joint representation without this clause is a malpractice claim waiting for a disagreement.
- **File retention.** How long does the firm keep your file after the matter closes? Five to seven years is typical. If you may need the file later — tax audits, future litigation — confirm the retention period and the retrieval cost.

## 13. The "no guarantee" clause (and what it really means)

Every retainer includes a sentence like "the firm makes no guarantee regarding the outcome." This is not the firm being pessimistic — ethics rules prohibit guaranteeing results. Read it as a reminder, not a warning.

What matters more is what the agreement says instead of a guarantee: the firm's assessment of likely timelines, cost ranges, and decision points. "We estimate this matter will require 40–60 hours through trial, billed at the rates above" is not a guarantee, but it is accountability. If the agreement contains no estimate at all, ask for one in the engagement letter. A lawyer who won't estimate either doesn't know your matter yet (fine — say so) or doesn't want the number on paper (not fine).

## 14. Signatures, dates, and the engagement letter (the paper trail)

The agreement is not effective until signed by both sides — and the signature date starts the clock on everything: billing, deadlines, the statute of limitations on your own claims. Three paper-trail habits:

- **Get the fully executed copy.** Not the draft, not the version with your signature only — the copy with both signatures and the date. This is the document a fee arbitrator reads.
- **Keep the engagement letter with the retainer.** Many firms send an engagement letter summarizing scope, staffing, rates, and estimates alongside the formal agreement. If the two conflict, the signed agreement usually controls — read both, and ask for conforming edits where they differ.
- **Amend in writing.** "Can you also handle the lease review?" "Sure, we'll add it to the file." That verbal exchange just expanded your scope without expanding your fee cap. Every scope change gets a written amendment with its own fee term. Five minutes of paperwork prevents five figures of surprise.

## The 10-minute pre-signing checklist

Before you sign any retainer agreement, run through this list. It takes ten minutes and it has saved clients more money than any single negotiation tactic:

1. Scope names the matter, forum, and exclusions — nothing vague.
2. Fee structure is clear, with the all-in cost (fees plus costs) estimated in writing.
3. Billing increment is 6 minutes, not 15 — or short tasks are capped.
4. Retainer type is identified: refundable advance, evergreen (with threshold), or fixed — never "non-refundable" without a pro-rata refund formula.
5. Expenses need your written pre-approval above $500, billed at cost with no markup.
6. Every timekeeper who may touch the file is named with their rate; rate increases are capped or locked for 12 months.
7. Invoices are monthly and itemized — task, timekeeper, time, rate.
8. Termination terms are clean: pay what's earned, no termination fee, file transferred promptly.
9. No cross-matter liens; time-sensitive documents released regardless of payment status.
10. Fee disputes go to the state bar's arbitration program, and you know the invoice-objection window.

A retainer agreement is the only contract you sign with someone whose job is to read contracts for a living. That asymmetry is exactly why you read it slowly, negotiate the clauses above, and never sign the first draft the same day you receive it. Sleep on it, mark it up, send it back with your changes. A good lawyer will respect the markup. A lawyer who pressures you to sign unchanged has told you everything you need to know about the representation — before it even starts.
