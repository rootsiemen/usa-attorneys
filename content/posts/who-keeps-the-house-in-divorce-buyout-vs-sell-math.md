For most divorcing couples, the house is the single biggest asset — and the single worst-decided asset. People fight hardest over the house for the least financial reason: one spouse "needs" to keep it for the kids, the other "deserves" their half, and nobody runs the numbers before drawing battle lines. Then a [divorce attorney](/divorce-attorney) walks them through the actual math, and half the time someone says: *"Wait — it's really that much?"*

Yes. It's really that much.

This guide does the math both ways for the same house, so you can see what a buyout actually costs, what selling actually nets, and the three traps that turn "keeping the house" into the most expensive decision of the divorce.

## The setup: one house, two numbers that matter

Every house-buyout decision rests on two numbers:

- **Fair market value** — what the house would sell for today (appraised value, not Zillow's guess, not what you paid in 2019).
- **Equity** — market value minus the mortgage balance.

Worked example we'll use throughout: a house worth **$425,000** with a **$260,000** mortgage balance.

Equity = $425,000 − $260,000 = **$165,000**

In a community-property state (California, Texas, Arizona, and seven others), that equity is typically split 50/50: each spouse's share is **$82,500**. In equitable-distribution states (the other 41), the split is "fair but not necessarily equal" — often 50/50 anyway for a long marriage, but a judge can tilt it. Your share is what a buyout must pay you — or what selling must divide between you.

Notice what's already happened: before a single dollar moves, the number most couples fight about is $165,000 of equity, not a $425,000 house. Keep that framing. It changes everything below.

## Option 1: one spouse buys the other out

A buyout means Spouse A keeps the house and pays Spouse B for their share of the equity. The mechanics:

1. **Get an appraisal** ($400–$600). Both sides need to agree on market value first — this is where most buyout negotiations die before they start.
2. **Pay the other spouse their equity share** — usually 50% of equity in our example: $82,500.
3. **Refinance the mortgage into one name.** The departing spouse must come off the loan, or they stay liable for a mortgage on a house they don't own. Lenders almost always require a refinance to do this; a simple "quitclaim deed" doesn't remove loan liability.

Here's the real cost of the buyout for Spouse A:

| Cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Payment to Spouse B (½ of $165,000 equity) | $82,500 |
| New mortgage: old balance + buyout cash-out ($260,000 + $82,500) | $342,500 |
| Refinance closing costs (2–5% of loan; use 3%) | ~$10,275 |
| Appraisal + misc. | ~$600 |

Spouse A walks away with the house and a **$342,500 mortgage** — about **$82,500 more debt** than before the divorce, plus roughly $11,000 in closing costs. If the new 30-year rate is 6.5%, the monthly payment on $342,500 is about **$2,165** (principal and interest alone, before taxes and insurance). The old payment on $260,000 at a 2021-era 3.25% rate was about $1,132. The monthly cost of "keeping the house" roughly **doubled**.

One more point most people miss: transfers of property between spouses incident to divorce are generally tax-free under IRS Section 1041 — the buyout payment itself isn't taxable income to the spouse receiving it. That's the rare piece of good news in this math.

## Option 2: sell the house and split the proceeds

Selling looks simpler, and the math is cleaner — but it's not free either. Same $425,000 house, $260,000 mortgage:

| Cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sale price | $425,000 |
| Agent commissions (2026 reality: ~5–5.5%, use 5.5%) | −$23,375 |
| Seller closing costs (title, transfer taxes, etc., ~2%) | −$8,500 |
| Mortgage payoff | −$260,000 |
| **Net proceeds** | **~$133,125** |

Split 50/50: each spouse walks away with **~$66,562**.

Compare that to the buyout: Spouse B got $82,500 in the buyout, but only $66,562 from a sale — a **$15,938 difference**. Why? Because selling costs ~$32,000 in commissions and closing costs that a buyout avoids. (The attorney's slice of that closing bill is smaller than most sellers expect — the [cost of a real estate lawyer at closing](/blog/real-estate-attorney-fees-closing-cost-math) is its own line-by-line breakdown.) In other words, the couple collectively *saves* about $32,000 by doing a buyout instead of selling — which is exactly why buyouts are worth negotiating hard for, if either spouse can actually afford the refinance.

Capital gains: if you've owned and lived in the house at least 2 of the last 5 years, the IRS excludes **$250,000 of gain per spouse** ($500,000 for a couple filing jointly). Most divorcing sellers owe zero capital-gains tax on the house. If your gain exceeds the exclusion, the excess is taxed — a [divorce attorney](/divorce-attorney) and a tax pro should run that number before you sign anything.

## The refinance trap: the number that kills most buyouts

Here's where buyouts die: Spouse A wants the house, the math above works on paper — and then the lender says no.

Refinancing means qualifying for a **$342,500 loan on one income**. Lenders generally cap your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) at 43–45% for a qualified mortgage. Run it:

- Spouse A's gross monthly income: $6,500 ($78,000/year)
- New housing payment (PITI — principal, interest, taxes, insurance): ~$2,900
- Car payment + minimum credit card payments: $550
- Total monthly debts: $3,450
- DTI: $3,450 ÷ $6,500 = **53%** — denied.

This is the most common outcome in buyout negotiations: the spouse who wants the house can't carry it alone at today's rates on one salary. Before you spend $5,000 on appraisals and attorney hours negotiating a buyout, get a **pre-approval letter** from a lender for the actual buyout amount. It takes a day and costs nothing. If the answer is no, you just saved yourself months of fighting over a house you couldn't keep.

There's also a second, quieter trap: the departing spouse's credit. Until the refinance closes, both names are on the mortgage. Every late payment during the divorce dings both credit scores — and the spouse leaving the house has zero control over whether the payments get made. Push for a **deadline in the settlement agreement**: refinance within 90–180 days, or the house gets listed for sale. Without that clause, "I'll refinance soon" can drag on for years.

## Community property vs. equitable distribution: your state changes the split

The 50/50 math above assumes a community-property state or an equitable-distribution state where the judge lands near 50/50. But the legal framework matters, especially for short marriages or houses bought before the wedding:

- **Community property (CA, TX, AZ, NV, WA, ID, WI, NM, LA):** assets acquired during the marriage are split 50/50, almost mechanically. Separate property — a house owned before the marriage — generally stays with the original owner, though mortgage payments made during the marriage can create a community interest in the equity growth. In [Texas](/attorneys/texas), for example, paying down a premarital home's mortgage with community funds gives the community a claim on the equity that those payments built.
- **Equitable distribution (41 states):** the court divides property "fairly," considering marriage length, each spouse's income and earning capacity, contributions (including homemaking and child-rearing), and sometimes fault. A 22-year marriage where one spouse stayed home with the kids often still lands near 50/50. A 3-year marriage where one spouse bought the house before the wedding often doesn't.

The practical takeaway: if your state is equitable-distribution and the marriage was short or the house was premarital, don't anchor negotiations on a clean 50/50 split. Ask your attorney what local judges actually do with houses in cases like yours — the county-level pattern matters more than the statute.

## The "keep it for the kids" test: emotion vs. arithmetic

The most common reason anyone gives for keeping the house is the children: stability, same school, same neighborhood. It's a real consideration — moving kids mid-divorce is genuinely hard. But it deserves a number, not just a feeling. Run the comparison:

**Keep the house:** $2,900/month PITI on one income, plus maintenance (budget 1% of home value per year — $4,250, or ~$354/month). True monthly cost: **~$3,254**.

**Sell and rent nearby:** each spouse rents a 3-bedroom in the same school zone. In many markets that's $2,200–$2,600/month — and there's no $11,000 refinance bill, no maintenance risk, and no 53% DTI.

The honest question isn't "is stability worth something?" — it is. The question is "is it worth **$600–$1,000 more per month**, a doubled mortgage, and a DTI that leaves no room for anything to go wrong?" Sometimes the answer is still yes. But make it a priced decision, not a default. And note the middle path many families take: one spouse keeps the house **until the youngest graduates**, with a written agreement to sell then — stability now, clean math later. Courts approve these arrangements routinely when both sides agree.

## Your pre-meeting numbers checklist

Before your first consultation, gather these — an attorney can give you real answers in one meeting instead of three if you walk in with numbers:

1. **Current mortgage statement** — balance, rate, monthly payment, who's on the loan.
2. **A realistic market value** — recent comparable sales in your zip code, not an automated estimate. Your attorney can tell you whether to pay for a formal appraisal now or later.
3. **Both spouses' incomes** — pay stubs or tax returns. The refinance math can't run without this.
4. **Other debts** — car loans, credit cards, student loans. DTI kills buyouts; know yours.
5. **When the house was bought, and with whose money** — premarital purchase changes the split in many states.
6. **Any second mortgage or HELOC** — equity is value minus *all* liens, not just the first mortgage.
7. **Your county's typical timeline** — in some counties a forced sale takes 6+ months to close; see [how long divorce takes, month by month](/blog/how-long-does-divorce-take-timeline-month-by-month).

Bring these to your consultation and ask one question first: *"Can I actually qualify to keep this house on my income?"* If the answer is no, you've just skipped the most expensive argument of the divorce. ## Buyout vs. sell: the final scorecard

Same house ($425,000 value, $260,000 mortgage, $165,000 equity), same 50/50 split:

| | Buyout | Sell |
|---|---|---|
| Spouse B receives | **$82,500** (tax-free transfer) | **~$66,562** |
| Spouse A ends with | House + $342,500 mortgage (~$2,165/mo P&I) | ~$66,562 cash, no house |
| Transaction costs | ~$11,000 (refinance) | ~$32,000 (commissions + closing) |
| Couple's total preserved | **~$154,000** | **~$133,125** |
| Biggest risk | Spouse A can't qualify for the refinance | Market timing; months of carrying costs while listed |

The buyout preserves roughly **$21,000 more** of the couple's wealth than selling — *if* the keeping spouse can qualify for the new loan and carry the payment. If they can't, selling isn't a failure; it's the math telling the truth early. Either way, the decision should come from these numbers, not from who wants the house more. Houses are assets. Treat this one like an asset, decide with a calculator, and save the emotion for the parts of the divorce where it belongs.
