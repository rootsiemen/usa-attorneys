Learning how to choose a bankruptcy attorney in Texas starts with an uncomfortable truth: most of the advice on the internet about picking a bankruptcy lawyer was written for other states, and some of it is actively wrong here. Texas is the most debtor-friendly exemption state in the country, and that single fact changes what a good attorney looks like. A lawyer who files clean cases in Ohio can still fumble a Texas case — by applying the wrong exemption scheme, by missing the homestead acreage rules, or by treating your house like an asset at risk when Texas law says it is not.

This guide gives you a filter, not a pep talk. It is built around one test — the Texas Exemption Test — plus the fee math, the consultation questions, and the red flags that separate a specialist from a generalist who happens to file bankruptcy on the side. If you are comparing attorneys right now, work through it in order. Most bad fits fail at step one.

## Why Texas Changes the Attorney Search

Texas did not adopt the federal bankruptcy exemptions. When you file in Texas, you use the Texas exemption scheme — and it is unusually generous. The headline is the homestead: Texas protects an unlimited dollar value of equity in your primary home, limited instead by acreage (up to 10 acres inside a city, up to 200 acres for a family outside one). Personal property gets up to $50,000 of protection for a single adult and $100,000 for a family, covering vehicles, household goods, tools of the trade, and more. Retirement accounts qualified under federal law are generally fully protected too.

Why does this matter for choosing an attorney? Because the whole strategy of a Chapter 7 case in Texas is different. In many states, the central question is "what will I lose?" In Texas, for most filers, the honest answer is "almost nothing" — and an attorney who cannot explain that confidently, with the acreage and dollar figures, is telling you they do not practice Texas bankruptcy deeply enough. Conversely, the rare Texas cases that do go wrong usually go wrong on exemption details: a homestead claimed on the wrong parcel, a vehicle counted twice, a transfer made in the months before filing that the trustee unwinds. Your attorney's command of these details is the case.

One more Texas wrinkle: the state has four federal bankruptcy districts — Northern, Southern, Eastern, and Western — and local practice varies between them. Trustees, judges, and even the paperwork rhythm differ by district. An attorney who files regularly in your district knows the local trustees by name and knows which documents they ask for first. That local fluency is worth more than a slick website.

## The Texas Exemption Test: Five Questions That Filter Bad Fits

Use your free consultation to run this test. You are not trying to trap anyone — you are checking whether the attorney thinks in Texas rules or generic ones.

1. **"Which exemption scheme will you claim for me, and why?"** The right answer is Texas exemptions, with a brief explanation of why Texas filers use state exemptions. A vague answer — or a mention of federal exemptions as the default — is a fail.
2. **"How does the homestead acreage limit apply to my property?"** A competent Texas attorney asks whether your home sits inside city limits and how much land it sits on, then explains the 10-acre urban / 200-acre rural family limits. If they talk about a dollar cap on your home's value, they are describing another state's law.
3. **"What does the $50,000 / $100,000 personal-property exemption cover in my case?"** They should walk through your vehicles, household goods, and tools of the trade against the cap, and tell you what, if anything, sits above it.
4. **"Have you handled cases before the trustees in my district?"** District fluency matters. A yes with specifics beats a generic yes.
5. **"What transfers or payments in the last year could a trustee question?"** The look-back rules — 90 days for ordinary creditors, one year for insiders — catch more Texas cases than any exemption mistake. A good attorney asks about this before you do.

An attorney who answers all five crisply has passed the test. One who waves them away with "don't worry, we handle all that" has not — they have just told you they do not want to be examined. ## Board Certification: The Credential That Actually Means Something in Texas

Texas is one of the few states with a formal board certification for consumer bankruptcy law, awarded by the Texas Board of Legal Specialization. Earning it requires years of concentrated bankruptcy practice, peer review, and a specialist exam. It is not a marketing badge — it is a credential most Texas attorneys, including many who file bankruptcy cases, do not hold.

That does not mean an uncertified attorney is a bad one. Plenty of excellent Texas bankruptcy lawyers are not board-certified, especially younger specialists building toward it. But certification is the fastest single signal available to you: if an attorney is Board Certified in Consumer Bankruptcy Law by the Texas Board of Legal Specialization, the specialization question is settled, and you can spend the consultation on your case instead of their resume. If they are not certified, ask what percentage of their practice is consumer bankruptcy. The number you want is high — 80% or more. A general practitioner who files five bankruptcies a year next to divorces and wills is not the attorney for a case with real exemption or means-test complexity.

Check standing yourself, too. The State Bar of Texas website lets you confirm any attorney is licensed and in good standing, and whether they carry the bankruptcy specialization. It takes two minutes and it is the one verification step no one should skip.

## The Real Fee Math for a Texas Bankruptcy Case

Texas bankruptcy fees are not mysterious, but the quotes you collect will look inconsistent until you know what each one includes. Here is the honest breakdown for a typical consumer case.

The court filing fee is set federally: $338 for Chapter 7 and $313 for Chapter 13, identical in every Texas district. On top of that come the two required courses — pre-filing credit counseling and post-filing debtor education — usually $10 to $50 each, sometimes waivable for low-income filers. The attorney fee is the variable part. For a straightforward Texas Chapter 7, most attorneys charge a flat fee roughly in the $1,200 to $3,000 range, and they almost always require it paid in full before filing — because once the case is filed, unpaid pre-filing fees become dischargeable debt, so the attorney cannot collect them afterward. Chapter 13 fees run higher, typically $3,500 to $6,000, but most of that is paid through the three-to-five-year repayment plan rather than upfront.

## The Chapter-Fit Question Comes Before the Price Question

The most expensive mistake in Texas bankruptcy is not overpaying a fee. It is filing the wrong chapter, or filing at the wrong time. A Chapter 7 discharges qualifying unsecured debt in about four months; a Chapter 13 restructures debt into a three-to-five-year plan and is often the right tool when you are behind on a mortgage and want to keep the house, or when your income is too high for Chapter 7 under the means test. The means test compares your income against the Texas median for your household size, and the arithmetic — including which deductions count — is where cases are won or lost before filing.

That is why the consultation should feel like an interview of your finances, not a sales call. A good Texas bankruptcy attorney asks about your income over the last six months, your secured debts, recent transfers, and whether you expect income to change soon. Sometimes the right advice is to wait a month, or to fix one transaction before filing, and an attorney who tells you that — even though it delays their fee — is exactly the attorney you want. ## Questions to Ask at the Consultation (Copy This List)

Bring this list. The answers, and how comfortably they are given, tell you almost everything.

- What percentage of your practice is consumer bankruptcy, and how many cases like mine have you filed in the last year?
- Are you Board Certified in Consumer Bankruptcy Law by the Texas Board of Legal Specialization?
- Which district will my case be filed in, and how often do you appear before its trustees?
- What is your total flat fee, what exactly does it include, and what would cost extra?
- Who will actually handle my case — you, an associate, or a paralegal — and who do I call with questions?
- How will you protect my home and vehicles under Texas exemptions, specifically?
- Are there any transfers, payments, or debts in my history that could complicate the case?
- What is your honest assessment: is bankruptcy the right move for me, or should I consider alternatives first?
- What does your timeline look like from filing to discharge for a case like mine?
- What happens if a creditor objects or the trustee asks for more documents — is that inside the fee?

Watch for how the attorney handles the question about alternatives. A lawyer who has never once talked a prospective client out of filing is either very lucky or not listening. The best Texas bankruptcy attorneys regularly tell some people not to file yet, or not to file at all.

## Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

Some signals are worth walking away over, even if the fee looks attractive.

- **A fee far below the Texas range with no explanation.** A $500 all-in Chapter 7 quote usually means a document-prep mill, not a law firm — and a dismissed case costs far more than the fee you saved.
- **Pressure to sign or pay during the first meeting.** A real consultation gives you time to think. High-pressure tactics are a business model, not a service.
- **No questions about your assets, transfers, or income history.** If the attorney does not probe, they are not screening your case — they are processing it.
- **Promises about outcomes.** No attorney can guarantee a discharge or promise the trustee will not ask questions. Guarantees are a sales line, not a legal opinion.
- **No written fee agreement.** Every fee, every included service, and every extra charge should be in writing before you pay anything.

Also be wary of anyone who wants to file immediately without reviewing your last year of financial moves. Speed is sometimes necessary — a foreclosure sale or garnishment can force urgency — but speed without review is how avoidable problems get baked into a case.

## A Practical Shortlist Process for Texas Filers

Here is the process that works, in order. First, build a shortlist of three attorneys who practice consumer bankruptcy in your district — the State Bar directory and our [Texas attorney listings](/attorneys/texas) are both useful starting points. Second, check each one for board certification and good standing. Third, book a consultation with each and run the Exemption Test plus the question list above. Fourth, compare the written fee agreements side by side — not just the headline number, but what each one includes. Fifth, choose the attorney whose answers were the most specific, not the cheapest. In bankruptcy, specificity is the product you are buying.

One final Texas-specific note: if you own a business, have significant non-exempt assets, or face a lawsuit alongside the bankruptcy, say so in the first consultation, not the third. Those facts change the chapter analysis, the exemption planning, and sometimes the district strategy — and an attorney who learns them late cannot plan around them. The right attorney will not be surprised by complexity; they will price it, plan it, and explain it in plain language.

Choosing a bankruptcy attorney in Texas is not about finding the lowest quote or the biggest firm. It is about finding the specialist who knows Texas exemptions cold, files in your district regularly, and treats the consultation as a real examination of your case. Run the Exemption Test, check certification, read the fee agreement, and trust the attorney who answers hard questions with specifics. That is how a Texas bankruptcy case starts right — and how it ends with a discharge instead of a dismissal.
