Choosing an [immigration attorney](/immigration-attorney) is nothing like choosing a plumber or even another kind of lawyer. In most legal areas, a bad hire costs you money. In immigration, a bad hire can cost you your case — a misfiled form, a missed deadline, or advice from someone legally forbidden to give it can end in denial, removal proceedings, or a ban that follows you for years.

That is exactly what makes immigration the most scam-prone corner of American law. In Spanish-speaking communities, "notarios" advertise immigration services out of storefronts — and in Latin America a *notario público* is a powerful legal official, so the title carries trust it does not deserve here. The FTC and state attorneys general have warned about this for years: in the US, a notary public cannot give legal advice, cannot represent you before USCIS or an immigration judge, and in many states it is outright illegal for them to charge for immigration form preparation.

This guide shows you how to tell a real immigration attorney from the lookalikes, what fair fees actually look like in 2026, and the exact questions that expose the fakes — before you sign anything.

## 1. The three kinds of "helpers" — and only two are legal

Before you hire anyone, understand who is allowed to do immigration legal work in the United States:

- **A licensed attorney** admitted to a state bar. Can advise you, prepare and file everything, appear before USCIS and immigration court, and take your appeal. This is the full package.
- **An EOIR-accredited representative** working for a recognized organization (churches, legal aid nonprofits). Authorized by the Executive Office for Immigration Review to represent people in immigration court and before USCIS, usually at low or no cost. Real, legal, and underused — find them on the EOIR recognition and accreditation roster.
- **Everyone else** — notarios, "immigration consultants," travel-agency back offices, tax preparers who "also do immigration." They can type what you dictate on a form, the same as a friend could. They cannot tell you which form to file, which box to check, whether you qualify, or what to do when USCIS pushes back. The moment they do, they are practicing law without a license.

The dividing line is **advice**. Filing papers is a service; deciding *strategy* is law. If your case involves any judgment call — which category, which waiver, whether to disclose something — you need one of the first two categories, full stop.

## 2. The notario playbook: five moves the fakes all use

Notario fraud is not one trick; it is a pattern. The FTC's complaint files show the same moves repeating across states:

1. **The price anchor.** They quote $400–$800 for a "green card package" when real attorneys charge $3,000–$5,000 flat. The low number is the bait. The real cost arrives later: rushed filings, wrong categories, and a cleanup bill from an actual lawyer that dwarfs what doing it right would have cost.
2. **The guarantee.** "One hundred percent approved — I have a contact inside." No one has a contact inside. USCIS does not take bribes from storefronts, and any "guarantee" of an immigration outcome is a lie by definition — the decision belongs to a government officer or judge.
3. **The urgency close.** "The law is changing next month; file now." Congress has not passed major immigration reform in decades. Deadlines are real, but a stranger's panic about them is a sales tactic.
4. **The vanishing file.** They will not give you copies of what they filed, your receipt numbers, or your USCIS online account access. This is deliberate: if you cannot see the file, you cannot discover the mistake until the denial letter arrives.
5. **The disappearing act.** When the RFE (Request for Evidence) or denial comes, the storefront is suddenly closed, the phone is disconnected, and there is no license to report — because there never was one.

If even two of these are present, walk away. Real attorneys do the opposite on every point: written fee agreements, copies of everything, your own case access, and a license number you can look up.

## 3. What immigration attorneys actually charge in 2026 — the fee math

Immigration is mostly a **flat-fee** practice, which is good for you: you know the price before the work starts. Typical flat-fee ranges reported across AILA member practices and consumer guides this year:

- **Family-based green card (I-130 + I-485 package):** $3,000–$5,000
- **Citizenship / naturalization (N-400):** $1,000–$2,500
- **Asylum application and hearing:** $4,000–$8,000
- **Removal (deportation) defense:** $5,000–$12,000 flat, or $200–$400/hour
- **Waivers (I-601 / I-601A):** $3,000–$6,000

Those are attorney fees **on top of** USCIS filing fees, which are separate and go to the government. Since the April 2024 fee rule, the big ones run roughly: I-130 about $675 on paper, I-485 about $1,440 for most adults, N-400 about $760 on paper, and the asylum application (I-589) carries a $100 fee. Fees change — confirm the current number on the USCIS fee calculator before you budget, because a quote built on last year's fees is a quote that will be "revised."

Now the worked example that matters. Say a family green card case quotes you $3,500 from a licensed attorney, versus $600 from a notario:

- **Attorney route:** $3,500 + ~$2,115 in USCIS fees (I-130 + I-485 + biometrics for one adult) = **~$5,615 total**, filed correctly, with RFEs handled in the fee and copies of everything in your hands.
- **Notario route:** $600 + the same $2,115 in government fees = $2,715 — then the form is filed in the wrong category or with a defective I-864 affidavit of support. USCIS denies or issues an RFE the notario cannot answer. Now you hire a real lawyer to fix it: a motion to reopen or refile runs $2,500–$4,000, plus new filing fees if the case must be re-filed, plus 6–12 months lost. **True cost: $7,500–$10,000+ and a year gone.**

The cheap option is the most expensive option with extra steps. The math is not close.

## 4. Ten questions that separate real attorneys from everyone else

Use these at the free or low-cost consultation most immigration firms offer:

1. **"What is your bar number, and in which state?"** Then look it up on that state's bar website before you pay anything. Two minutes, free, non-negotiable.
2. **"What percentage of your practice is immigration — and have you handled my exact case type?"** A lawyer who did two asylum cases in 2021 is not an asylum lawyer. You want someone whose daily work matches your problem: family petitions, removal defense, employment, asylum, waivers.
3. **"Are you a member of AILA?"** The American Immigration Lawyers Association is the field's professional home. Membership is not a license, but its absence in someone claiming to specialize is worth a follow-up question.
4. **"What is my legal strategy — not just which form, but why?"** This is the question notarios cannot answer. A real attorney explains your eligibility theory, the risks, the backup plan, and what could go wrong — in the first meeting.
5. **"What is the flat fee, what is excluded, and do I get that in writing?"** Exclusions matter: appeals, motions, consular processing, and travel are commonly separate. Get the fee agreement in writing, in a language you read fluently, before paying.
6. **"Who does the actual work — you, an associate, a paralegal?"** Paralegals doing supervised work is normal and fine. A "lawyer" who is never available and routes everything through unlicensed staff is not.
7. **"Will I get copies of everything filed and my own USCIS account access?"** The answer must be yes. Anyone who keeps your file hostage is planning for you not to see it.
8. **"What are my realistic chances, and what is the worst case?"** Honest attorneys talk in ranges and name the downside: denial, referral to immigration court, bars on reentry. Guarantees are disqualifying — see section 2.
9. **"How will we communicate, and how fast do you respond?"** Immigration cases live and die on deadlines — an RFE gives you a fixed window, often 87 days or less. If the office cannot tell you its response-time policy, assume you will be chasing them during the one week that matters.
10. **"Have you ever been disciplined by a bar or by EOIR?"** Ask it plainly. Then check: state bar discipline records are public, and EOIR publishes its own disciplinary actions against practitioners.

## 5. When you genuinely need a lawyer — and when you might not

Not every immigration matter requires full representation, and an honest attorney will tell you so:

- **Probably DIY-able:** a straightforward N-400 naturalization with clean history, a simple I-130 for a spouse with no complications. Even then, many people pay for a one-hour review ($150–$300) as insurance.
- **Hire counsel:** asylum, removal proceedings, any waiver (I-601/I-601A), any criminal history, prior denials or deportations, visa overstays, fraud or misrepresentation allegations, employment-based cases with a company involved. These are the cases where one wrong answer on a form creates a permanent bar.

The rule of thumb: if your case has a **fact that makes an officer pause** — an arrest, a prior denial, an overstay, a gap in status — that pause is worth a lawyer. The consultation is usually free or cheap; the mistake is not.

## 6. How to verify credentials yourself in 15 minutes

Do this before you sign or pay:

1. **State bar lookup.** Every state bar has a free online attorney search. Confirm the name, the license status ("active"), and any public discipline. This catches the suspended lawyer practicing anyway and the non-lawyer using a real lawyer's name.
2. **AILA lawyer search.** The association's public directory lists member immigration attorneys by location and focus area — a quick cross-check of specialization claims.
3. **EOIR accreditation roster.** If someone offers low-cost representation through a nonprofit, confirm the organization is recognized and the representative accredited on the EOIR website.
4. **Immigration court records.** If your case is already in court, the EOIR automated hotline and online portal show your hearing dates and whether anyone has actually entered an appearance for you — which catches the "lawyer" who took your money and never filed the E-28.
5. **Google the exact business name plus "complaint," "fraud," or "FTC."** Notario operations get sued and written up constantly. Five minutes of searching surfaces patterns no consultation will reveal.

Total cost: zero dollars. Total value: the difference between a representative and a predator.

## 7. Your first-meeting document checklist

Bring these to the consultation so the attorney can give you a real strategy instead of guesses:

- Passport(s), current and expired, for everyone involved
- Any visa, I-94 record, or entry documents
- All prior USCIS receipt notices, approval/denial letters, and RFEs
- Marriage, birth, and divorce certificates as relevant
- Criminal records: arrest reports, court dispositions — bring everything even if the case was dismissed
- Prior immigration filings, including anything a previous helper prepared
- A written timeline of entries, exits, and status changes with dates

Organize them chronologically in one folder. Attorneys consistently report that the clients with organized files get better strategy in the first meeting — because the lawyer spends the hour analyzing your case instead of assembling it.

Anyone who resists any of them has told you everything you need to know.
