Two men get hurt on the same stretch of Gulf water on the same day. One is a deckhand on a supply boat. The other is a longshoreman unloading that boat at the dock. Both break a leg, both miss eight months of work, both rack up roughly $90,000 in medical bills. The deckhand's case settles for around $650,000. The longshoreman's claim pays roughly $180,000 — and most of it is not a settlement at all, it is scheduled benefits. Same injury, same water, same day: a gap of nearly half a million dollars. The difference is not the injury. It is the law that covers each man — and **Jones Act settlement amounts** are calculated by a completely different formula than LHWCA benefits.

That fork is the single most expensive misunderstanding in offshore injury law. Workers who file under the wrong law leave money on the table; workers who wait to find out which law applies often miss the evidence window that proves it. This article runs the math both ways, side by side, with real numbers, so you can see which track you are on before an adjuster tells you.

## The fork: seaman or harbor worker — and why it decides the money

Maritime injury law splits workers into two tracks, and the split is decided by the job, not the injury.

**The Jones Act (46 U.S.C. § 30104)** covers *seamen* — workers with a substantial connection to a vessel in navigation. The classic test is about 30% of working time spent aboard a vessel. Deckhands, cooks, engineers, captains, roustabouts on floating rigs, and crew on tugboats and supply boats are usually seamen. The Jones Act is a *negligence* law: you prove the employer was negligent, and the burden of proof is famously low — even slight negligence counts. In return, you can recover the full range of tort damages: past and future medical expenses, past and future lost wages and earning capacity, pain and suffering, mental anguish, and physical impairment. You also get **maintenance and cure** — daily living money plus all medical care — regardless of fault, and an unseaworthiness claim against the vessel owner.

**The LHWCA (33 U.S.C. § 901 et seq.)** covers *harbor workers who are not seamen* — longshoremen, shipbuilders, ship repairers, harbor construction workers, and dock workers on land-side facilities. It is a federal *no-fault workers' compensation* system. You do not have to prove anyone was negligent, and benefits start faster, but the payout is a scheduled formula: two-thirds of your average weekly wage while you are out, plus medical care, plus a fixed dollar amount for permanent partial disability based on a body-part schedule. Pain and suffering is **not** paid. The one exception is a separate negligence claim against the vessel owner under Section 905(b) — the so-called "third-party" claim — which is where the big numbers in LHWCA cases actually come from.

The practical consequence is blunt. A Jones Act case is valued like a personal-injury lawsuit, with a jury in the background. An LHWCA case is valued like a workers' comp claim, with a schedule in the foreground. That is why the same broken leg can be worth three times more on one track than the other.

If you work offshore or on the docks and you are unsure which track you are on, start with the [offshore accident lawyer](/offshore-accident-lawyer) page and compare your job duties against the seaman test — do not accept an employer's classification at face value. Employers and their insurers have a financial interest in calling you a harbor worker, because the harbor-worker track is cheaper for them.

## Jones Act settlement amounts: the formula, with real numbers

There is no official Jones Act settlement table. There is a formula, and the formula has five inputs. Understanding the five inputs is how you sanity-check any offer.

**1. Medical expenses — past and future.** Everything the injury has cost and will cost: emergency care, surgery, rehab, medication, and future procedures. A herniated disc that needs a fusion is not a $40,000 injury; the surgery alone runs $60,000–$110,000, plus a year of physical therapy. Future medical costs are usually the largest single line item in a serious case, and they must be proved with a life-care plan, not guessed.

**2. Lost wages and earning capacity.** Jones Act cases pay *full* lost wages — not the two-thirds rate workers' comp pays. Take a deckhand earning $1,400 a week. Eight months out is 35 weeks × $1,400 = **$49,000** in past lost wages. If the injury ends his sea career at 34, the future loss is the bigger number: 30 working years × even a modest $55,000 annual wage is $1.65 million in gross future earnings, reduced to present value by an economist — typically landing in the $700,000–$950,000 range after discounting. This is why career-ending injuries settle in seven figures while the same injury in a worker near retirement settles for far less.

**3. Pain and suffering.** There is no multiplier table in maritime law, but adjusters and juries think in ranges. A fracture that heals cleanly with no permanent limits might carry $50,000–$150,000 in pain and suffering. A crush injury with amputation, chronic pain, or a traumatic brain injury carries multiples of that — and published verdicts show the range plainly.

**4. Maintenance and cure.** Separate from the settlement, a seaman is owed daily maintenance (living expenses, typically $30–$100 a day depending on the union contract or court rate) plus all medical care until reaching maximum medical improvement. An employer that drags its feet on maintenance and cure can be hit with punitive damages — one Louisiana jury awarded $1.5 million in punitive damages alone for willful refusal to pay cure.

**5. Punitive damages (rare, but real).** Only where the employer's conduct was willful or wanton — hiding a known hazard, falsifying inspection records, refusing cure after a doctor ordered it. Punitive damages are what turn a $900,000 case into a $2 million case.

Now put the formula to work on a worked example. A 38-year-old roustabout on a Gulf supply vessel suffers a herniated disc when a crane load swings into him. He earns $1,600 a week.

| Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| Past medical (surgery + rehab) | $118,000 |
| Future medical (second surgery, therapy) | $85,000 |
| Past lost wages (10 months) | $69,000 |
| Future earning capacity (reduced to present value) | $420,000 |
| Pain and suffering | $250,000 |
| **Total case value** | **$942,000** |

That $942,000 is the *value* — the settlement is where the parties land after discounting for litigation risk. With clear employer negligence (the crane's inspection was overdue), this case settles near value, roughly $800,000–$950,000. With disputed negligence, it settles lower, roughly $500,000–$650,000. Published Jones Act data backs the scale: Law.com's VerdictSearch puts the average Jones Act settlement at about $1.39 million, with awards ranging from $3,000 to over $20 million — and published firm results show the same spread, from $425,000 for a crew-boat slip-and-fall to $4 million for a deckhand's burn injuries and $8 million-plus for disabling back injuries.

## LHWCA settlement amounts: the schedule that caps your claim

The LHWCA does not ask a jury what your injury is worth. It reads a schedule. The core benefit is **temporary total disability: two-thirds of your average weekly wage**, capped at 200% of the national average weekly wage (about $1,950 a week in 2026 terms, adjusted annually). Medical care is paid in full. When you reach maximum medical improvement, **permanent partial disability** is paid from a statutory schedule: a fixed number of weeks of compensation per body part, multiplied by two-thirds of your weekly wage. A lost arm, for example, is scheduled at 312 weeks; a lost leg at 288 weeks; a lost finger at a fraction of that.

Run the same broken leg through the LHWCA. A longshoreman earning $1,500 a week gets $1,000 a week while out — 35 weeks is **$35,000** in wage benefits, against the deckhand's $49,000 in full wages for the same time off. His medical bills are paid, same as the Jones Act. His permanent partial disability for a leg with lasting limits might add $60,000–$120,000 depending on the impairment rating. Total: roughly **$150,000–$250,000** — and zero for pain and suffering, zero for lost earning capacity beyond the schedule, zero for the career he cannot return to.

That is the structural gap, and it is why the classification fight matters so much. The LHWCA also has a time limit that surprises people: claims must generally be filed within **one year** of the injury (33 U.S.C. § 913), compared with three years under the Jones Act. A worker who waits, assuming he has three years, can lose the LHWCA track entirely.

But the LHWCA is not the end of the story. Section 905(b) lets a harbor worker sue the *vessel owner* for negligence — a separate claim on top of the scheduled benefits. If a defective crane, an unseaworthy deck, or a negligent crew caused the injury, that third-party claim is valued like a Jones Act case: full damages, including pain and suffering. In practice, the Section 905(b) claim is where most LHWCA cases find their real money. A longshoreman whose scheduled benefits total $180,000 might add a $400,000–$700,000 vessel-negligence recovery on top — if the negligence evidence is preserved. That evidence — the crane's maintenance logs, the vessel's inspection records, the crew's statements — disappears fast, which is why the first 30 days matter as much here as they do under the Jones Act. ## Side by side: Jones Act vs. LHWCA on the same injury

| Factor | Jones Act (seaman) | LHWCA (harbor worker) |
|---|---|---|
| Who is covered | Crew with ~30%+ time aboard a vessel | Longshoremen, shipbuilders, dock workers |
| Fault required | Yes — employer negligence, low burden | No — no-fault benefits |
| Lost wages | 100% of past + future earning capacity | 2/3 of average weekly wage, capped |
| Pain and suffering | Yes, full tort damages | No — scheduled benefits only |
| Medical | Full, past + future | Full, past + future |
| Maintenance and cure | Yes — daily living money + care | No equivalent (wage benefits instead) |
| Third-party claim | Unseaworthiness vs. vessel owner | Section 905(b) vs. vessel owner |
| Filing deadline | 3 years | 1 year |
| Typical serious-injury value | $500,000–$2,000,000+ | $150,000–$400,000 (plus 905(b) claim) |

The table is the whole article in one view. If you are a seaman, you are in a lawsuit system that pays like one. If you are a harbor worker, you are in a benefits system that pays like one — unless you have a vessel-negligence claim, which is the bridge between the two worlds.

## The three mistakes that cut offshore settlements in half

**Mistake one: accepting the employer's classification.** A worker who spends 40% of his time on a jack-up rig is a seaman under the law even if his paycheck says "shore-based technician." Classification is a legal test, not a job title. Get an independent read before you sign anything that locks in the cheaper track.

**Mistake two: signing a release or recorded statement in the first week.** Offshore employers move fast after an injury — a "routine" statement, a light-duty offer, a small check "for your trouble." Each one can be used to cap the claim or to argue the injury was minor. The [workers' comp settlement math](/blog/workers-compensation-settlement-payout-math) shows the same pattern on land: early low offers are priced to close the case before the real costs are known.

**Mistake three: waiting to see if it gets better.** Both tracks have clocks — one year for LHWCA, three for the Jones Act — but the evidence clock is shorter than either. Vessel logs are overwritten, maintenance records are "updated," witnesses rotate off the boat. A back injury that feels manageable in month one can be a fusion surgery in month nine, and by then the crane inspection record that proves negligence may be gone.

## What to do this week if you were hurt offshore

1. **Get the classification in writing.** Ask your employer, in writing, which law they say covers you — Jones Act or LHWCA — and why. Keep the answer.
2. **Preserve your own record.** Photograph the scene, the equipment, and your injuries. Save every pay stub, every medical bill, and every message from a supervisor. Write down, dated, what happened while it is fresh.
3. **Do not sign a release.** Not a settlement, not a "voluntary" statement, not a light-duty agreement — until someone who does not work for the company has read it.
4. **Get a maritime evaluation, not just a company doctor.** The company doctor's job is to get you back to work. Your own doctor's job is to document what the injury actually is.
5. **Talk to a maritime attorney before the deadlines move.** The consultation costs nothing in most maritime practices — they work on contingency — and the classification question alone can be worth hundreds of thousands of dollars. ## The bottom line: find your track before you negotiate

Offshore injury cases are not valued by how bad the injury looks. They are valued by which law applies, what that law pays for, and whether the negligence evidence still exists when the case is filed. A seaman with a provable negligence claim and documented future losses is negotiating from a seven-figure position. A harbor worker on scheduled benefits alone is negotiating from a six-figure position — unless the vessel owner was negligent, in which case the Section 905(b) claim closes much of the gap.

The question to answer first is never "how much is my case worth." It is "which law covers me, and what does it pay for." Get that answer right, preserve the evidence, and the settlement math takes care of itself.
