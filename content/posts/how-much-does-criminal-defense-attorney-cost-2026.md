A criminal charge is one of the few bills where the sticker price means nothing and the fine print means everything. A [criminal defense attorney](/criminal-defense-attorney) might quote you $2,500 for a misdemeanor shoplifting case and $25,000 for a felony drug case — and both numbers can be perfectly reasonable, or both can be wrong for your situation. The fee depends on the charge, the billing structure, how far the case goes, and one factor most people never think to ask about: what happens to the meter when the case goes to trial.

This guide breaks down what criminal defense lawyers actually charge in 2026, the three billing structures they use, the real math of retainer versus flat fee versus hourly, and the comparison that actually matters — the cost of the lawyer against the cost of the conviction.

## The quick answer: what criminal defense lawyers charge

Across the US in 2026, criminal defense fees cluster into predictable bands:

| Charge level | Typical attorney fee |
|---|---|
| Misdemeanor (shoplifting, simple assault, first DUI) | $1,500 – $5,000 |
| Misdemeanor going to trial | $5,000 – $10,000 |
| Felony (drug possession, burglary, fraud) | $5,000 – $15,000 |
| Serious felony going to trial (assault with weapon, white-collar) | $15,000 – $50,000+ |

Roughly 90–95% of criminal cases in the US are resolved by plea bargain, not trial — the US Sentencing Commission and Bureau of Justice Statistics have reported figures in this range for years. That means most people land in the lower end of their band. The trial rows exist because they change everything about how you're billed, which is the next section.

## The three billing structures, decoded

Criminal defense attorneys almost never bill like your accountant or your divorce lawyer. They use three structures, and you will be quoted one of them in your first consultation:

**1. Flat fee.** One price for the whole case (or for one phase of the case, such as "through plea negotiation" with a separate flat fee "if it goes to trial"). This is the most common structure for misdemeanors: $2,500 flat for a first-offense theft case is the industry default in most mid-size cities. The lawyer assumes the risk that the case eats more hours than expected; you get price certainty.

**2. Hourly billing with a retainer.** More common for felonies. You pay a retainer up front — often $5,000 to $15,000 — and the lawyer bills against it at $200–$500+ per hour depending on experience and city. When the retainer runs out, you refill it. This is where surprises happen: a $10,000 retainer at $350/hour covers roughly 28 hours of work. A felony that goes to trial can burn 28 hours in a single week of preparation and courtroom time.

**3. The hybrid.** A flat fee for the "front end" (investigation, negotiation, pretrial motions) plus a per-day trial fee — commonly $2,000–$5,000 per trial day. This is the structure you most want to understand before signing, because it's where the real money hides. A case quoted at "$7,500 plus $3,000 per trial day" sounds manageable until you learn the trial is expected to last 4–5 days. That's $19,500–$22,500, not $7,500.

Ask every lawyer you consult: "What exactly triggers additional fees, and what is the all-in number if this goes to trial?" The lawyers who answer that question crisply are usually the ones worth hiring.

## Flat fee vs. hourly: a worked comparison

Take a realistic scenario: a felony drug possession charge in a mid-size metro, where a lawyer quotes either **$12,000 flat** or **$350/hour with a $10,000 retainer**. Which is better?

It depends entirely on how the case resolves:

- **Pleads out after 20 hours of work:** hourly costs $7,000 — you save $5,000 versus the flat fee. The flat-fee lawyer just made $600/hour and you overpaid.
- **Resolves after 34 hours:** hourly costs $11,900 — basically a wash.
- **Goes to trial, 80 hours total:** hourly costs $28,000. The flat fee just saved you $16,000.

Here's the asymmetry nobody tells you: the lawyer offering the flat fee knows the 90–95% plea rate. They're pricing against the most likely outcome, which is a relatively short case. You, as the client, should pick your structure based on your *risk tolerance and case facts*, not the average. If your lawyer tells you the evidence is strong for trial and you're not inclined to plead, the flat fee is the better hedge. If the case looks like a quick negotiated dismissal, hourly saves you money.

One more wrinkle: some flat-fee agreements exclude specific costs — expert witnesses ($2,000–$10,000 each), private investigators ($75–$200/hour), independent lab testing ($500–$2,000). Always ask "what costs are excluded from this fee?" before comparing two quotes.

## What actually drives the price up (and down)

Five variables move a criminal defense fee more than anything else:

**The charge class.** A misdemeanor theft case and a felony fraud case aren't just different in fee — they're different in *kind*. Misdemeanor discovery is often a police report and a body-cam video. Felony discovery can be thousands of pages of financial records, digital forensics, and witness statements. More discovery means more hours, and hours are the product being sold.

**Whether it goes to trial.** Trial is the great multiplier. A plea-resolved misdemeanor might involve 10–20 lawyer hours; a two-week felony trial can involve 200+. This is why trial fees are almost always priced separately.

**The jurisdiction.** A criminal defense lawyer in rural counties often charges $150–$250/hour; in New York, Los Angeles, or Chicago, $400–$750/hour for experienced counsel is normal. Court filing fees, bail, and expert costs vary by state too. If your case is in a small county, hiring the big-city lawyer may double the fee for identical work.

**The lawyer's track record.** A former prosecutor with 20 years of jury trials charges more than a generalist who does some criminal work on the side — and for good reason. In criminal defense, you're not buying hours, you're buying the 30 seconds when the lawyer spots the illegal search in the police report. Experience has a real price, and it's the one premium that's usually worth paying.

**The evidence timeline.** Get arrested in January, hire a lawyer in January, and the lawyer can preserve surveillance footage, interview witnesses while memories are fresh, and file suppression motions early. Hire one in March after two continuances, and you're paying catch-up hours at full rate. Early hiring is the single cheapest thing you can do in a criminal case.

## The math that matters: lawyer fee vs. conviction cost

People comparison-shop the lawyer's fee and ignore the other side of the ledger. Let's fix that with a worked example.

**Scenario:** a first-offense misdemeanor — say, a theft under $1,000 — in a mid-size city. Lawyer A quotes **$3,500 flat** for defense through plea; Lawyer B quotes $2,000 flat. Tempting to take B. Now price the conviction:

| Cost of a misdemeanor conviction | Typical amount |
|---|---|
| Court fines and fees | $500 – $2,000 |
| Restitution | $0 – $1,000 (theft amount) |
| Lost wages (court dates, possible short jail stay) | $1,000 – $5,000 |
| Future earnings impact (background checks flagging the record for ~7 years) | $10,000+ over time |
| Increased car insurance (if the charge involves driving) | $2,000 – $6,000 over 3–5 years |

A $3,500 defense that negotiates the charge down to a deferred adjudication — dismissed after probation, no conviction on record — versus a $2,000 defense that takes the first plea offered and leaves you with a conviction: the *price difference is $1,500*, but the outcome difference can be $20,000+ in lifetime cost. This is the single most important calculation in criminal defense shopping, and almost nobody does it.

For felonies the asymmetry is even starker. A felony conviction can affect voting rights (in some states), firearm rights, professional licensing, immigration status, and housing applications for life. Against that, even a $15,000–$25,000 defense fee is often the cheapest money you'll ever spend.

## Public defender vs. private attorney: the honest comparison

If you qualify financially, a public defender costs you nothing — and many are excellent trial lawyers. But the comparison isn't free versus expensive; it's *resources* versus resources:

- **Caseload.** Public defenders in some jurisdictions carry 200+ felony cases or 400+ misdemeanor cases at a time — 2–3x the caseload standards recommended by the National Advisory Commission. A private attorney taking your $8,000 fee might carry 20–40 active cases.
- **Time per case.** The math above tells the story: a lawyer with 300 cases has roughly a tenth of the hours per case that a lawyer with 30 cases has. In straightforward cases that barely matters. In cases requiring investigation, expert witnesses, or motion practice, it matters enormously.
- **Who decides.** With a public defender, the office assigns your lawyer — you don't shop for one. With a private attorney, you choose. If you've never met your public defender before the first court date, that's normal; if it worries you, hire privately for the cases where the stakes are high.

The rule of thumb: if the charge is minor and you're guilty-adjacent with a standard plea on the table, a public defender is often fine. If the charge threatens jail time, your license, your immigration status, or your record — hire the private attorney. The $5,000–$10,000 difference buys you *attention*, which is the actual scarce resource in criminal defense.

## How to read a retainer agreement (5-minute checklist)

Before signing anything, verify these seven items in writing:

1. **Scope:** does the fee cover "the entire case" or only "through plea negotiations"? If the latter, what's the trial fee?
2. **Trial trigger:** at what exact point does the trial fee kick in — filing a trial notice, jury selection, day one?
3. **Excluded costs:** experts, investigators, filing fees, transcripts, travel. Get a written list.
4. **Refund terms:** if you fire the lawyer or the case resolves in a week, what portion comes back? Unearned fees are generally refundable, but the agreement should say so.
5. **Payment plan:** most defense attorneys accept installments — typically 30–50% down. Get the schedule in writing.
6. **Communication:** who returns your calls — the lawyer or a paralegal — and how fast? Put a response-time expectation in the agreement.
7. **Second-chair rule:** if the lawyer gets sick or conflicts out before trial, who takes over, and at what cost?

Two red flags worth walking away from: a lawyer who guarantees a specific outcome (dismissal, acquittal) — that's an ethics violation in most states and a lie everywhere — and a fee that's dramatically below every other quote you got. A $900 flat fee for a felony means either corners are being cut or fees will appear later. Before you hire anyone, run through the [questions to ask before hiring a criminal defense attorney](/blog/questions-to-ask-before-hiring-criminal-defense-attorney) — the consultation is free, and it's where the fee structures above get tested against your actual case.

## FAQ

**Can I get a criminal defense lawyer with no money down?**
Some take payment plans with as little as 20–30% down, and a few accept credit cards. For misdemeanors under $3,000, many will work with you. For felonies, expect to pay a substantial portion up front — the lawyer is reserving weeks of future calendar time for you.

**Is a more expensive lawyer always better?**
No — but a *suspiciously cheap* one is almost always worse. The sweet spot is usually the second or third quote: experienced enough to have a track record, hungry enough to price competitively. Ask every lawyer how many jury trials they've done in the last two years.

**Do lawyers charge extra if the case goes to trial?**
Usually, yes — either a separate trial flat fee ($5,000–$25,000+) or a per-day trial rate ($2,000–$5,000/day). This is the number one source of billing disputes in criminal defense, so nail it down in writing before hiring.

**Can I switch lawyers mid-case?**
Yes, with the court's permission in most cases — and you have an absolute right to counsel of your choice. The complications: your new lawyer needs time to get up to speed (billable), and switching right before trial can annoy the judge. Switch early if you're going to switch.

**What if I genuinely can't afford any lawyer?**
Request a public defender at your arraignment — it's a constitutional right (Gideon v. Wainwright, 1963). You can also look for legal aid societies and law school clinics, which handle some criminal matters for free or at sliding-scale fees.
