A work injury sets two clocks running at once. The first clock is medical — get hurt badly and you need a doctor now. The second clock is legal, and it's the one that bankrupts people: **report deadlines as short as a few days**, a claim form your employer is supposed to hand you (but sometimes "forgets"), and a benefit formula that pays less than your paycheck by design.

Workers' compensation exists in all 50 states. The system pays for medical treatment and part of your lost wages when you're injured on the job, no lawsuit required. But "no lawsuit required" doesn't mean "automatic." Claims get denied every day — for late reporting, for gaps in medical treatment, for injuries described the wrong way on the wrong form. This checklist walks you through the first 30 days, day by day, so your claim survives the scrutiny it's going to get.

## Day 0: the first 10 minutes — report it before anything else

**1. Report the injury to your supervisor immediately — in writing.** This is the single most important step on this entire list. Every state has a reporting deadline, and the clock starts when the injury happens (or when you discover it, for gradual injuries like repetitive strain). In many states the deadline to notify your employer is **30 days or less**; a few are tighter, and waiting almost never helps your case.

Verbal reports are real, but paper is stronger. Send a text, an email, or a short note: *"Reporting a work injury on [date/time] — hurt my lower back lifting a pallet on aisle 4."* A timestamped message is evidence that you reported on time. Coworkers who witnessed it should get the same treatment: ask them to save a quick written note of what they saw.

**2. Describe the mechanism of injury precisely.** Insurance adjusters look for a specific gap: the injury you tell the ER doctor about versus the injury you told your supervisor about. If those two stories differ — "hurt my back lifting" at work versus "my back's been hurting for a while" at the ER — the claim gets flagged. One sentence, consistent everywhere: **what you were doing, what happened, what body part hurts.** Stick to it.

**3. If it's an emergency, go to the ER.** Workers' comp covers emergency treatment. Your health comes before paperwork, and there's no rule that says you must see a "company doctor" before an emergency room. Just make sure everyone at the ER knows it's a work injury, so the billing goes to workers' comp rather than your health insurance.

## Days 1–3: medical care and the doctor problem

**4. Understand the authorized-doctor rule — it varies by state.** Workers' comp doesn't always let you pick any doctor. The rules fall into three buckets:

| Rule | States (examples) | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Employer chooses the doctor | Florida, many others | You must see the company-authorized doctor or risk paying out of pocket |
| Employee picks from a panel | Colorado, others | The employer gives you a short list of 3–4 doctors to choose from |
| Employee chooses freely | California, New York | You can see your own doctor (with some limits on switching) |

Going to an unauthorized doctor in an employer-choice state is the classic self-inflicted claim wound. Ask HR or your supervisor, in writing, **which doctor you're authorized to see.** If they won't answer, document that too — it matters later.

**5. Tell every medical provider it was a work injury.** Say it at the ER, at urgent care, at the authorized clinic, and to every specialist. The medical record is the spine of your claim. A note that says "patient reports injury at work on 10/22/26" is what proves the connection between your job and your injury six months later when the insurer starts doubting.

**6. Get copies of everything.** Medical records, imaging orders, work-status notes (the slip that says you can or can't work), and receipts for prescriptions. Keep your own file — don't assume the clinic will forward things correctly. One missing work-status note is often the difference between getting paid for missed weeks and getting nothing.

## Days 3–10: file the actual claim (don't assume your employer did)

**7. Don't assume the report you gave your supervisor = a filed claim.** These are often two different things. In many states your employer is required to file a First Report of Injury with their insurer, but paperwork gets "lost," supervisors go on vacation, and HR departments get busy. A verbal report to your boss is not the same as an open claim number with the insurance company.

Call your employer and ask: **"Has the First Report of Injury been filed, and what is my claim number?"** If there's no claim number within a week, call the insurer directly, or contact your state's workers' comp board — most have a helpline, and many let workers file directly when the employer stalls.

**8. File the employee claim form yourself.** Several states (California's DWC-1, New York's C-3, Texas's DWC-041) have a form the *employee* fills out. If your state has one and your employer hasn't handed it to you, download it from your state workers' comp board's website and file it yourself. This form is what officially starts your claim in the eyes of the state — waiting for your employer to do it for you is optional patience you can't afford.

## The deadlines that kill claims: state reporting windows

**9. Know your state's reporting deadline.** Miss it and the insurer gets a legal reason to deny, no matter how real the injury. The most common windows:

| Reporting deadline | Example states |
|---|---|
| 30 days | Florida, Texas, most states |
| 60–90 days | Colorado (4 days to employer!), Nevada (7 days) — varies widely |
| 1 year+ to file the formal claim | Many states give longer for the *claim form*, but the *employer notice* deadline is much shorter |

The trap: some states require notice to the employer in as little as **4–7 days**. "I was going to see if it got better" is the most expensive sentence in workers' comp. Report early even if you're not sure it's serious — you can always close a claim, but you usually can't reopen a late one.

## The benefit math: what workers' comp actually pays

**10. Understand the 2/3 rule and the caps.** Temporary disability benefits (wages while you can't work) typically pay **two-thirds (66⅔%) of your average weekly wage**, subject to a state maximum. Run the numbers so you're not blindsided:

- Earning **$1,200/week** → ~**$800/week** in wage benefits (below most state caps, so you get the full two-thirds)
- Earning **$2,500/week** → two-thirds is $1,667, but if your state caps at, say, $1,100/week, you get **$1,100** — a 56% pay cut, not 33%
- Earning **$600/week part-time** → ~**$400/week**, and yes, part-time and temporary workers are covered

There's also a **waiting period** (usually 3–7 days) before wage benefits start; if you're out long enough (often 14–21 days), the waiting period gets paid retroactively. Medical treatment itself is generally covered at 100% with no copay — which is why keeping every medical receipt and going to authorized providers matters so much.

**11. Track every lost day and every mile.** Missed shifts, reduced hours, doctor visits, pharmacy runs, mileage to medical appointments (many states reimburse mileage at the IRS rate) — log it all with dates. When the insurer's accounting of your lost time doesn't match reality, your log is the correction. Spreadsheet, notebook, phone notes — the format doesn't matter, the dates do.

## Days 10–30: retaliation, light duty, and the denial letter

**12. Know that firing you for filing is illegal — and still happens.** Nearly every state prohibits retaliating against a worker for filing a workers' comp claim: you can't legally be fired, demoted, or have your hours cut *because* you filed. In practice, employers sometimes find "other reasons." The protection strategy is the same paper trail you've been building: dated injury report, dated claim, dated everything. If you're fired within weeks of filing, that timeline is your evidence. Save every text, email, and performance review from before and after the injury.

**13. Light-duty offers are a fork in the road — treat them carefully.** If the doctor clears you for light duty and your employer offers a modified job, refusing it can cut off your wage benefits. But "light duty" that ignores your doctor's restrictions is a trap — if the restrictions say no lifting over 10 lbs and the assignment has you lifting 30, get the restrictions in writing from the doctor and decline in writing, citing the mismatch. Never refuse verbally; always respond in writing with the doctor's note attached.

**14. Read the denial letter the day it arrives.** Claim denied? The letter has to state the reason — common ones: late reporting, injury not work-related, pre-existing condition, no medical evidence, unauthorized doctor. Each reason has a specific rebuttal:

- **Late reporting** → your timestamped report to the supervisor
- **Not work-related** → consistent medical records saying "injury at work"
- **Pre-existing condition** → medical evidence the work injury *aggravated* it (aggravation of a pre-existing condition is compensable in most states)
- **Unauthorized doctor** → proof you asked who to see, or switched to an authorized one immediately

Most states give you a **limited window to appeal** — often 30 days from the denial. Mark that date the moment you read the letter.

## When to call a [workers' comp lawyer](/workers-compensation-attorney)

**15. These are the five signs you need professional help:**

1. **Your claim was denied** and the appeal deadline is ticking
2. **The insurer's doctor says you're fine; your doctor says you're not** — this dispute decides your benefits, and it needs a counterweight
3. **You're offered a settlement** — lump-sum offers are often far below what the claim is worth; a lawyer who handles [settlement math](/workers-compensation-settlement-payout-math) knows what your case actually values
4. **You're fired or your hours are cut** after filing
5. **Your injury is permanent** — permanent disability ratings involve complex schedules and future medical costs that shouldn't be negotiated by guesswork

Workers' comp lawyers typically work on **contingency — a capped percentage of what they win**, often 10–20% depending on the state (lower than personal injury's typical 33%), and many states require a judge to approve the fee. The consultation is usually free. If your claim is straightforward and paying, you may never need one — but the moment the system pushes back, the cost of not having one is almost always higher.
