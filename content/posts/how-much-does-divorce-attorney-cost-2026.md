Ask ten divorced people what their [divorce attorney](/divorce-attorney) cost and you'll get ten wildly different answers — anywhere from a few hundred dollars to well over $50,000. That's because "divorce" isn't one product. An uncontested divorce where both spouses agree on everything is a paperwork exercise; a contested custody battle is closer to full-scale litigation.

This guide breaks down what divorce lawyers actually charge in 2026, what drives costs up or down, and how to keep your divorce as affordable as possible without getting a bad outcome.

## The quick answer: average divorce attorney costs

Nationally, the average total cost of a divorce with attorneys involved lands around **$11,000–$15,000 per spouse** — but the median is lower, roughly $7,000, because a large number of simple divorces pull the average down while a small number of brutal custody fights pull it way up.

More useful than the average is the range by divorce type:

| Divorce type | Typical attorney cost per spouse |
|---|---|
| Uncontested, no kids, few assets | $500 – $2,500 |
| Uncontested with kids or property | $2,500 – $7,000 |
| Contested, moderate disputes | $7,000 – $20,000 |
| High-conflict / custody battle | $20,000 – $50,000+ |

Hourly rates for divorce attorneys in 2026 typically run **$250–$450 per hour** in most markets, $350–$600+ in expensive metros like New York City, San Francisco, or Washington, D.C. Most require an upfront **retainer** — often $3,000–$10,000 — which the lawyer bills against as work is done.

## Three real divorce bills: what the money actually bought

Averages hide the mechanics. Here's what three different divorces actually cost, line by line — so you can see which one looks like yours.

**The $1,200 divorce.** Married 4 years, no kids, renting an apartment, one shared car, no joint debts beyond a $3,000 credit card. Both spouses agreed on everything before calling a lawyer. Flat fee: $1,000 for document preparation and filing, plus $200 in court filing fees. Total time from first call to final decree: 7 weeks. The lawyer's actual work: about 4 hours.

**The $9,500 divorce.** Married 11 years, two kids (ages 7 and 9), a house with $90,000 in equity, two retirement accounts. The couple agreed on custody (joint) but fought over the house buyout number and holiday schedule. The bill: $5,000 retainer, depleted in 6 weeks; a second $5,000 retainer, of which $4,500 was used. Roughly 27 billable hours at $350/hour, mostly negotiation calls, two mediation sessions, and drafting the settlement agreement. Total time: 5 months.

**The $47,000 divorce.** Married 14 years, three kids, a family business, and genuine hostility. Custody evaluation ($8,500), business valuation ($12,000), 14 months of litigation, one failed mediation, and a 3-day trial. The lawyer billed 110 hours at $425/hour. The kicker: the amount in dispute over the business valuation was about $60,000 — meaning the couple spent nearly that much fighting over it. This is the math that ruins people.

The pattern: cost doesn't track how much you *have*. It tracks how much you *fight*.

## What drives the cost up

**Children.** Custody disputes are the single biggest cost multiplier in divorce. Custody evaluations, guardian ad litem appointments, and repeated court hearings can double or triple the bill. If you and your spouse can agree on a parenting plan, you've saved more money than any other single decision.

**Disagreement itself.** Every disputed issue — who keeps the house, how retirement accounts split, spousal support — means more lawyer hours: more negotiation, more filings, more court appearances. Two reasonable people who compromise spend a fraction of what two angry people spend fighting over the same assets.

**Your state's rules.** Community property states ([California](/attorneys/california), [Texas](/attorneys/texas), [Arizona](/attorneys/arizona), and six others) split marital assets 50/50 by default, which simplifies property division. Equitable-distribution states give judges discretion, which can mean more litigation over what's "fair." Mandatory mediation or parenting classes in states like [Florida](/attorneys/florida) add steps — and costs — but often reduce the total bill by avoiding trial.

**Hidden assets and businesses.** If a spouse owns a business, holds stock options, or might be hiding money, expect forensic accountants and business valuations at $5,000–$15,000+ on top of legal fees.

**Your spouse's lawyer.** You can't control this one, but an aggressive opposing attorney who files everything and concedes nothing will inflate your costs no matter how reasonable you are.

## The retainer trap: how your deposit really disappears

Most people hear "$5,000 retainer" and think that's the price. It isn't — it's a deposit, and it evaporates faster than anyone expects. Here's the math on a typical month:

Your lawyer bills $350/hour in 15-minute increments (0.25 hours minimum per task). In one ordinary month of a moderately contested divorce:

- Two phone calls with you (20 min each, billed as 0.5 hr total): **$175**
- Reviewing your spouse's financial disclosure (1.5 hrs): **$525**
- Drafting a response letter to opposing counsel (1 hr): **$350**
- Three emails back and forth about the house appraisal (0.75 hr): **$262**
- Court filing and scheduling for one hearing (0.5 hr): **$175**
- Preparing for and attending that hearing (3 hrs): **$1,050**
- Follow-up memo and file notes (0.5 hr): **$175**

**One month's total: $2,712.** Your $5,000 retainer is more than half gone, and nothing dramatic even happened — no trial, no emergency motions, just routine divorce work. This is why lawyers ask you to replenish the retainer, and why the final bill shocks people who thought they were "almost done" three times in a row.

The defense: ask for monthly itemized statements from day one, question every entry you don't understand, and batch your communications — one organized weekly email instead of six scattered calls saves you real money.

## How divorce lawyers charge

**Hourly billing** is the most common: you pay for every hour (often billed in 6- or 15-minute increments) including phone calls and emails. Ask for itemized bills and question anything you don't understand.

**Flat fees** are common for uncontested divorces — one price for the whole case, often $1,000–$3,500. Make sure you know exactly what's included and what triggers extra charges.

**Retainers** aren't a fee type so much as a deposit: you pay $3,000–$10,000 upfront, the lawyer draws from it, and you replenish when it runs low. Unused retainer money comes back to you.

**Limited-scope representation** (unbundled services) is the budget option many people don't know about: you hire a lawyer for specific tasks — reviewing your settlement agreement, coaching you for mediation, handling one hearing — instead of the whole case. This can cut costs dramatically if you're organized enough to handle the routine parts yourself.

## 6 ways to keep your divorce affordable

1. **Agree on as much as possible before hiring.** Every issue you and your spouse resolve yourselves is hours you don't pay a lawyer to negotiate. Write down what you agree on and bring it to the first meeting.

2. **Be an organized client.** Lawyers bill for time spent hunting through your shoebox of bank statements. Deliver clean, labeled financial documents and answer questions completely the first time.

3. **Don't use your lawyer as a therapist.** At $300+/hour, venting about your ex is the most expensive conversation you'll ever have. Get a therapist for feelings; use the lawyer for legal strategy.

4. **Pick your battles.** Fighting over a $2,000 couch with $5,000 in legal fees is arithmetic, not principle. Ask your lawyer: "What will it cost to win this point, and what is the point worth?"

5. **Consider mediation first.** A mediator ($200–$400/hour split between you) helps you reach agreement without two lawyers billing against each other. Many courts require mediation before trial anyway.

6. **Respond promptly.** Every "just checking in" email your lawyer has to send because you didn't answer costs you money. Be the client whose file moves.

## How divorce costs vary by state

Geography matters. In major metros — New York City, Los Angeles, San Francisco, Chicago, Washington D.C. — hourly rates of $400–$650 are normal, and even straightforward contested divorces often cross $20,000 per spouse. In smaller markets across the Midwest and South ([Ohio](/attorneys/ohio), [Indiana](/attorneys/indiana), [Mississippi](/attorneys/mississippi)), experienced divorce attorneys charge $175–$300 per hour, and uncontested cases frequently wrap up under $3,000.

Filing fees also differ: roughly $300–$400 in [California](/attorneys/california) and [Florida](/attorneys/florida), under $200 in states like [Wyoming](/attorneys/wyoming) and [North Dakota](/attorneys/north-dakota). Mandatory programs add up too — parenting classes ($50–$150), mediation sessions, and custody evaluations ($2,500–$10,000 where ordered) vary widely by county. When budgeting, ask local attorneys for a realistic total for cases like yours in your specific county, not a national average.

## Can you get divorced without a lawyer?

Yes — and for simple cases, it's often fine. If your marriage was short, you have no kids, no real estate, and minimal shared debt, many states offer simplified or summary dissolution procedures with filing fees of a few hundred dollars. Online divorce services ($150–$500) handle the paperwork.

But get at least a one-hour consultation ($200–$400) before going DIY if any of these apply: you have children, own a home, have retirement accounts to divide, suspect hidden assets, or there's any history of domestic violence. A single hour of advice can prevent mistakes that cost ten times more to fix.

## Can you make your spouse pay your attorney's fees?

Sometimes. In most states, each spouse pays their own lawyer — but courts can order one spouse to contribute to the other's fees when there's a big income gap, or when one spouse's misconduct (hiding assets, dragging out litigation, ignoring court orders) drove costs up. Judges in [New York](/attorneys/new-york), [California](/attorneys/california), and [Illinois](/attorneys/illinois) routinely award interim attorney's fees so the lower-earning spouse can afford representation during the case.

Don't count on it, though. Fee awards are discretionary, they rarely cover the full bill, and they usually come at the end — you still need to fund your case upfront. If affording a lawyer is the barrier, ask about payment plans, limited-scope representation, or legal aid societies in your county, which handle qualifying low-income divorces at reduced cost or free.

## The bottom line

A divorce attorney costs what your situation demands: a few hundred dollars for the simplest cases, tens of thousands for custody wars. The biggest lever on price is in your hands — the more you and your spouse can agree on, the less you pay lawyers to fight about. Hire the right lawyer for your complexity level, stay organized, and treat every disputed dollar like the real money it is.
