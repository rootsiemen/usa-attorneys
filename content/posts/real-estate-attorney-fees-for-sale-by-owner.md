Real estate attorney fees for sale by owner deals are the most misunderstood number in a FSBO transaction — mostly because sellers compare the fee to the wrong thing. They compare it to zero ("I'm selling it myself, why would I pay a lawyer?") when the honest comparison is against the 5% to 6% commission they just decided not to pay, and against the cost of one contract mistake on the largest asset they own.

Here is the market context. In the National Association of Realtors' most recent profile of home buyers and sellers, for-sale-by-owner homes were about 6% of all sales, and the median FSBO home sold for $380,000 against $435,000 for agent-assisted sales. Some of that gap is property mix, not negotiating skill. But a pattern shows up in the data year after year: the sellers who lose money on FSBO rarely lose it on the listing price. They lose it on the paperwork — a botched disclosure, a dead earnest-money clause, a contract the buyer's lender rejects. That paperwork is exactly what the attorney fee buys. This guide prices it out, task by task, with real 2026 numbers.

## Why selling by owner changes the lawyer's job completely

In an agent-assisted sale, three professionals quietly absorb legal risk without ever calling themselves lawyers. The listing agent drafts the purchase contract on state association forms. The agent manages the disclosure packet and its deadlines. The agent holds the transaction together when the appraisal, inspection, or title search surfaces a problem. None of that is legal advice, but all of it is legal work product — and when you sell by owner, every piece of it lands on your kitchen table.

A [real estate attorney](/real-estate-attorney) in a FSBO deal is not a luxury add-on to the closing. The attorney replaces the contract-drafting function entirely. That means the scope — and therefore the fee — is bigger than the same attorney's fee in an agent-assisted deal, where the lawyer only reviews and closes. Expect a FSBO engagement to include some or all of: drafting the purchase and sale agreement, preparing or reviewing the seller's disclosure packet, reviewing the title commitment, handling earnest money instructions, preparing the deed, and attending or conducting the closing. When you see a fee quote, the first question is which of those six jobs it actually covers.

## Real estate attorney fees for sale by owner, task by task

Most FSBO attorneys price in one of three ways: a flat package for the whole sale, flat fees per document or task, or hourly billing (typically $200 to $450 an hour depending on the market). Here is what each piece runs in 2026:

- **Full FSBO package (contract through closing):** $500 to $2,500 flat in most markets; $2,500 to $4,000 in high-cost metros and attorney-closing states. This is the product most sellers should buy.
- **Purchase contract drafting only:** $300 to $750. You handle the rest, the lawyer writes the one document that decides everything.
- **Contract review (buyer's agent sends the offer, lawyer checks it):** $250 to $600, or one to two hours hourly.
- **Seller's disclosure packet preparation:** $150 to $400 — cheap insurance, because disclosure defects are the number one source of post-closing lawsuits against FSBO sellers.
- **Deed preparation:** $150 to $350. In several states only an attorney or a party to the transaction may prepare a deed, and a defective deed clouds title for the next sale.
- **Closing attendance or conducting the settlement:** $500 to $1,500 when not bundled.
- **Hourly troubleshooting (inspection disputes, appraisal gaps, buyer financing delays):** $200 to $450 per hour, billed in tenths.

Two pricing traps to watch. First, an hourly engagement with no cap: a smooth FSBO closing might take four hours of attorney time, a messy one twelve, and you will not know which deal you have until week three. Second, a suspiciously cheap package — $299 "FSBO kits" sold nationally are usually document templates with a paralegal hotline, not a licensed attorney in your state reviewing your contract. Real estate law is state law; the license has to match the property.

## Worked example: a $425,000 FSBO sale, priced two ways

Take a $425,000 single-family sale — close to the national median — sold by owner in a state where attorneys are optional at closing.

**Option A: flat FSBO package at $1,495.** The attorney drafts the contract ($500 value), prepares disclosures ($250), reviews title ($300), prepares the deed ($200), and closes ($500 à la carte — bundled, the package prices the whole thing at $1,495). Total legal cost: **$1,495, or 0.35% of the price.**

**Option B: hourly at $325.** Contract drafting 2.5 hours ($812.50), disclosure consult 0.8 hours ($260), two rounds of buyer-attorney revisions 1.6 hours ($520), title review 1 hour ($325), closing 1.5 hours ($487.50), phone time 0.9 hours ($292.50). Total: **$2,697.50 — and every buyer hiccup adds to it.**

Now the comparison that actually matters. An agent-assisted sale of the same house at a 5.5% total commission costs $23,375, of which the seller's side is roughly half to all of it depending on the agreement. Even if the FSBO seller offers a buyer's agent 2.5% ($10,625) to widen the buyer pool, the seller who pays $1,495 for a legal package keeps roughly **$11,000 to $21,000** of the commission savings. The attorney fee is not the risk in a FSBO sale. The attorney fee is 0.35% spent protecting the other 99.65%. ## States where FSBO sellers legally need an attorney — and where they just think they don't

In a group of states — including Massachusetts, New York, Connecticut, [South Carolina](/attorneys/south-carolina), Georgia, Delaware, Vermont, and West Virginia — an attorney must conduct or oversee the closing itself. A FSBO seller in those states is hiring a lawyer whether the marketing plan included one or not, and the fee is usually a flat closing package of $900 to $2,500. Price it before you list, not after you accept an offer.

Everywhere else the attorney is optional legally and strategic financially. Three state snapshots show how different the landscape is:

- In [Maryland](/attorneys/maryland), settlements are typically run by title companies or settlement attorneys, transfer and recordation taxes are a real closing line item, and sellers in the Baltimore and DC suburbs see attorney settlement fees around $750 to $1,500 when they bring their own counsel.
- In [Kansas](/attorneys/kansas), no attorney is required at any stage, hourly rates run lower than the coasts ($175 to $300 is common), and a full FSBO package in Wichita or Topeka frequently prices under $1,200.
- In [New Mexico](/attorneys/new-mexico), a community-property state, expect one quirk nobody warns FSBO sellers about: legal services carry the state's gross receipts tax, roughly 5% to 8.5% depending on locality, added on top of the quoted fee.

Wherever you sell, the seller's disclosure obligation follows state statute, not your listing platform's template. If your state's disclosure form asks about water intrusion and you "forgot" the basement flood, no listing website indemnifies you. A $250 disclosure consult is the cheapest line in this entire guide relative to what it prevents.

## The three FSBO traps the fee actually prevents

**Trap 1: the homemade contract.** Downloadable FSBO contracts routinely miss the financing contingency deadline mechanics, the title objection period, and the personal-property schedule. The failure mode is not theoretical: the buyer's loan is denied on day 41, the contract's financing clause expired on day 30, and the buyer demands the earnest money back while threatening to sue — with a contract you wrote as the only evidence. Earnest money disputes are the single most common FSBO legal fight, and they are won or lost on clauses a drafting attorney installs by default.

**Trap 2: disclosure defects.** Every state has its own seller disclosure statute, its own exemptions, and its own lookback exposure — claims can surface a year or more after closing. Sellers who used an agent can at least point to the brokerage's process. FSBO sellers stand alone. This is also where family transfers go wrong: if your "sale" is really a house moving between relatives, the analysis overlaps with the [buyout-versus-sell math in a divorce](/blog/who-keeps-the-house-in-divorce-buyout-vs-sell-math), and the deed and tax treatment deserve a lawyer's eyes even more, not less.

**Trap 3: the unmarketable title.** Liens from a prior contractor, an ex-spouse still on the deed, a boundary that does not match the fence line — title problems surface at the worst moment, after your buyer has a rate lock expiring. A title commitment review early in the process, which is part of any real FSBO package, finds these while they are still fixable.

## How to hire: the five questions that separate a package from a quote

1. **Is the fee flat, and what exactly does it include?** Get the six tasks from the list above checked off in the engagement letter.
2. **What happens if the deal dies?** A fair package charges a reduced kill fee ($300 to $600) if the contract terminates; a bad one bills everything hourly from the start.
3. **Who at the firm does the work?** You want a licensed attorney drafting and a paralegal scheduling — priced accordingly — not the reverse.
4. **How many FSBO closings have you handled this year?** Residential FSBO work is a specialty rhythm: contract, disclosure, title, lender documents. Five or more a year keeps an attorney fluent.
5. **Will you talk to the buyer's lender and title company directly?** If the answer is no, you have hired a reviewer, not a closer, and you will be the courier between professionals for six weeks.

Bring those questions, a copy of your survey if you have one, and last year's tax bill to the first meeting, and the attorney can usually quote a hard flat number on the spot. That number — 0.3% to 0.6% of your sale price in most of the country — is what real estate attorney fees for sale by owner deals really cost when they are bought correctly: small, fixed, and known before the first buyer ever walks through the door.
