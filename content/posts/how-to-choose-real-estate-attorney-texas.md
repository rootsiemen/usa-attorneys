Learning how to choose a real estate attorney in Texas is harder than it should be, because Texas is unusual: unlike New York or Georgia, Texas does not require an attorney at closing. Most Texas closings run through a title company, so buyers and sellers often discover they need a lawyer only when something goes wrong — a title defect, a contract dispute, a survey that does not match the fence line. By then, the cheapest option is gone and the clock is ticking. This guide fixes that with a method instead of a generic checklist: a 5-point scorecard you can apply to any attorney in 20 minutes, a 3-quote comparison, and real dollar math on a typical Texas closing so you can see exactly what the fee buys.

## Why Texas Makes This Choice Different

In attorney-closing states, the lawyer is built into the process. In Texas, the lawyer is optional — and that changes the hiring logic completely. A Texas title company handles the escrow, the title search, and the closing paperwork, but it does not represent you. If the title commitment shows an old lien, a missing heir, or a boundary exception, the title company's job is to protect the title policy, not your negotiating position. An attorney's job is the opposite: to protect you, even when that means slowing the deal down.

Three Texas-specific facts shape everything that follows. First, Texas purchase contracts usually run on Texas Real Estate Commission (TREC) forms, which are standardized — so the value of an attorney is less in drafting from scratch and more in spotting the addenda, deadlines, and option-period traps that cost real money. Second, Texas has no state real estate transfer tax, which keeps one line of the closing statement at zero but makes the remaining lines — title policy, lender fees, attorney fees — easier to compare apples to apples. Third, Texas homestead protections are among the strongest in the country, which matters the moment a deal involves a family home, a divorce, or an inherited property. An attorney who cannot explain how homestead rules affect your specific sale is not the right attorney for that sale, no matter how low the quote.

## The 5-Point Scorecard: Score Every Attorney Before You Hire

Most "how to choose" guides hand you a list of questions and stop there. Questions without scoring just produce three friendly conversations and no decision. Use this scorecard instead: rate each attorney 0, 1, or 2 on each point during or right after the first call, for a maximum of 10. Hire the highest score, not the lowest fee — and never hire anyone below a 6.

1. **Texas real estate focus (0–2).** Two points if at least half their practice is Texas residential or commercial real estate closings and disputes. One point if real estate is a real but secondary practice. Zero if they are a generalist who "also does closings." Ask directly: "What percentage of your cases last year were Texas real estate matters, and how many closings did you personally handle?"
2. **Local courthouse and title-plant knowledge (0–2).** Two points if they regularly close in your county and can name the title companies and county filing quirks there. A lawyer who closes weekly in Harris County but never in Travis County is a weaker pick for an Austin purchase, however good their reviews.
3. **Fee transparency in writing (0–2).** Two points only for a written quote that lists exactly what is included — contract review, title commitment review, closing attendance, post-closing recording follow-up — and what triggers extra billing. A verbal "around $1,000" scores zero.
4. **Response speed (0–2).** Time their reply to your first inquiry. Two points for a substantive response within one business day, one point within two days, zero beyond that. In a 7- to 10-day option period, a slow lawyer is an expensive lawyer.
5. **Conflict clarity (0–2).** Two points if they state plainly, before you pay anything, who they represent and whether they have any relationship with the other side, the title company, or the lender. Dual representation in a Texas closing is a red flag unless it is disclosed and you genuinely understand it.

A scorecard does two things a question list cannot: it forces you to compare attorneys on the same dimensions, and it exposes the common trap — the friendly, cheap generalist who scores a 4 against a focused specialist who scores a 9.

## How to Choose a Real Estate Attorney in Texas: The 3-Quote Method

Knowing how to choose a real estate attorney in Texas comes down to one discipline: get three written quotes before you commit, and make them compete on scope, not just price. Here is the method, step by step.

**Step 1: Shortlist from three different sources.** Take one name from the State Bar of Texas lawyer directory, one from a recent buyer or seller you trust, and one from a local title company or agent referral — then verify each independently. A list built from a single source inherits that source's bias.

**Step 2: Send the same brief to all three.** Email each attorney the same one-paragraph brief: property type, county, price range, whether you are buying or selling, and any known complication (estate sale, divorce, survey issue, investment property). Identical briefs produce comparable quotes; vague inquiries produce vague quotes.

**Step 3: Compare scope first, price second.** Lay the three written quotes side by side and check four scope items: contract review, title commitment review, closing attendance, and post-closing follow-up. A $750 quote that excludes closing attendance is not cheaper than a $1,200 quote that includes it — it is a $750 quote plus a surprise $400 later.

**Step 4: Interview the top two, not all three.** A 20-minute call with your top two scorers is enough. Ask who will actually do the work (the attorney or a paralegal), how they handle a title objection found three days before closing, and what their fee looks like if the deal falls apart mid-stream. Then pick by scorecard total, with price as the tiebreaker.

This method takes about two hours total, and it routinely saves more than that in avoided rework. The most common regret among Texas buyers is not hiring too late — it is hiring the first name they found and discovering at the closing table what the quote excluded.

## The Closing-Cost Math: What an Attorney Actually Costs on a $350,000 Texas Home

Fee talk stays abstract until you put it on a closing statement. Take a $350,000 Texas home purchase with 20% down ($70,000) and a $280,000 loan. Texas attorney fees for a standard residential closing typically land between $500 and $2,000, with hourly rates of $150 to $500 when the matter is billed by time rather than flat fee. Here is how three realistic options compare:

| Option | Attorney cost | What you get | Hidden-cost risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Title company only, no attorney | $0 | Escrow, title search, standard closing | High — nobody reviews the contract or title commitment for your interests |
| Flat-fee attorney, full scope | $1,000–$1,500 | Contract + title review, closing attendance, follow-up | Low — scope fixed in writing |
| Hourly attorney, complex deal | $150–$500/hr, often $1,500–$3,000 total | Same as flat fee, plus dispute work if needed | Medium — meter runs if the deal gets contested |

Now the math that matters. On a $350,000 purchase, a $1,250 flat attorney fee is 0.36% of the price — about the cost of a home inspection and a survey combined. Against that, weigh the three most common Texas closing surprises an attorney catches: a title exception that should have been cleared before closing (curing it after closing can cost $2,000–$10,000 in legal work), a missed option-period deadline that forfeits earnest money (typically 1% of price, or $3,500 here), and an incorrect tax proration or HOA payoff figure (commonly $500–$2,000). One caught problem pays for the fee several times over; zero caught problems still buys you a reviewed contract and a professional in the room. The break-even logic is simple: if the probability of even one $1,500 surprise exceeds the fee, the attorney is the cheaper option in expectation — and on Texas deals with older homes, estate sellers, or rural surveys, that probability is not low.

Sellers face a different equation. Texas seller closing costs typically run 6% to 10% of the sale price including agent commission, title, and payoff costs — on $350,000, that is $21,000 to $35,000. A $1,000 attorney fee inside that total is roughly 3–5% of the seller's closing bill, and it buys review of the seller's disclosure obligations and the payoff figures, where seller-side errors most often hide.

## Buyer vs. Seller: You Are Not Hiring for the Same Job

A buyer hires an attorney to find problems before money moves. The buyer's attorney reads the title commitment line by line, checks survey exceptions, confirms the legal description matches the property being walked, and verifies that HOA and tax prorations are computed correctly. A seller hires an attorney to prevent problems from coming back after money moves. The seller's attorney reviews disclosure forms, confirms lien payoffs, and makes sure the deed and any mineral-rights reservation say exactly what the seller intends.

This matters because the "best" attorney for one side can be wrong for the other. A buyer with a new-build home in a master-planned suburb mainly needs contract and title review; a seller disposing of an inherited property with three heirs mainly needs probate and title-clearance experience. State your side and your complication in the first sentence of your inquiry, and judge the attorney's answer against that job — not against a generic reputation score.

## Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

- **No written fee agreement.** If the quote lives only in a phone call, walk away. Every legitimate Texas real estate attorney puts scope and fees in writing.
- **They cannot name a recent closing in your county.** County practice matters in Texas: filing rules, title plants, and even survey customs vary. "I cover all of Texas" is a warning, not a credential.
- **They promise an outcome.** No attorney can guarantee a clean title, a closing date, or a dispute result. Guarantees are marketing, and marketing is not representation.
- **Someone else does all the work, unnamed.** Paralegal help is normal; a quoted attorney you never speak to, with the file handed to a junior you never meet, is not.
- **They discourage an independent title review.** If an attorney tells you the title company's commitment "is fine, don't worry about it," that is the one document they should most want to read.
- **Pressure to sign today.** A real estate closing has deadlines, but the attorney engagement is not one of them. Same-day pressure is a sales tactic, not a legal necessity.

If you hit two or more of these, do not negotiate — move to the next name on your shortlist. Texas has thousands of licensed real estate attorneys; there is no shortage that justifies settling.

## Your 2-Week Hiring Checklist

**Week 1, days 1–2:** Define your deal in one paragraph — side, county, price, complications. Pull three names from three different sources and check each against the State Bar of Texas directory.

**Week 1, days 3–4:** Send the identical brief to all three and score response speed as it happens. Collect written quotes and score each on the 5-point scorecard.

**Week 1, day 5:** Interview the top two scorers for 20 minutes each. Ask who does the work, how a late title objection is handled, and what happens to the fee if the deal dies.

**Week 2, day 1:** Pick by scorecard total, price as tiebreaker. Get the engagement letter and confirm scope in writing before paying any retainer.

**Week 2, onward:** Send the attorney the contract and title commitment the day you receive them, not the day before closing. Early documents are what make the fee worth paying.

Choosing well in Texas is not about finding the most famous firm or the lowest quote. It is about matching a focused, local, transparent attorney to your specific deal — and doing it early enough for the review to matter. Run the scorecard, get the three quotes, check the math against your own price, and you will have done more diligence on your lawyer than most buyers and sellers do on the house itself.
