A truck accident lawsuit takes **6 months to 3 years** in most cases — and the outlier catastrophic files run past 5 years. That is the honest range, and it is wide because the clock is not set by a court calendar alone. It is set by your medical recovery, by how many defendants are in the case, by federal trucking records that have to be preserved before they are overwritten, and by how long the insurance carrier can afford to wait you out.

The question most people actually mean is narrower: *when will I see money?* This guide answers that with a month-by-month timeline, the real duration of every phase, the state deadlines that force the pace, and three worked examples — a fast case, a typical case, and a slow one — so you can place your own case on the map instead of guessing.

## The short answer: how long does a truck accident lawsuit take?

If liability is clear, injuries are moderate, and the carrier wants the file closed, a truck accident case can settle **before a lawsuit is ever filed — in 6 to 12 months**. Once a lawsuit is filed, the realistic range becomes **1 to 3 years**. Cases that go to trial add another year on top of that, and appeals can add more.

Here is the whole timeline at a glance:

| Phase | Typical duration | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| Investigation and evidence preservation | Days to 3 weeks | Black-box data, driver logs, dashcam, scene evidence |
| Medical treatment to maximum improvement | 3 months to 2+ years | You heal; bills and future-care costs get documented |
| Demand and pre-suit negotiation | 1 to 3 months | Insurer reviews the demand package |
| Filing the lawsuit | Before your state's deadline | Complaint filed; clock becomes court-driven |
| Discovery | 6 to 18 months | Records, depositions, expert reports |
| Mediation and settlement talks | 1 to 3 months | Most filed cases settle here |
| Trial | Days to 3 weeks | Only about 4–5% of cases reach this stage |
| Post-trial motions and appeals | 6 months to 2 years | Only if someone appeals |

The national data backs up the shape of this table. The Bureau of Justice Statistics found that **half of tort jury trials went from filing to verdict in 23 months or less** — and only about **4% of tort cases were ever decided by a trial at all**. Most truck cases end in a settlement, usually at mediation after depositions, once both sides have seen the evidence. ## Why truck cases take longer than car cases

A car crash has two drivers, two insurers, and one police report. A truck crash can have five defendants: the driver, the trucking company, the freight broker, the cargo loader, and the maintenance shop — each with its own insurer and its own lawyer. Every extra defendant adds a round of discovery, a round of finger-pointing, and a round of delay.

Then there is the federal layer. Interstate trucking is governed by Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations, and the evidence that proves a violation is electronic and perishable. The truck's electronic logging device (ELD) records hours of service. The engine control module — the "black box" — records speed, braking, and throttle in the seconds before impact. Dashcam footage, dispatch records, and the driver's qualification file all live on company servers. A spoliation letter has to go out in the first days, because some of that data is overwritten in weeks. That is why the first 72 hours after a truck crash matter more than the first 72 hours after a car crash — our [72-hour checklist](/blog/what-to-do-after-truck-accident-72-hour-checklist) walks through exactly what to preserve and in what order.

The third reason is medical. Truck crashes cause worse injuries — the weight gap is 20-to-1 — and a case should not settle until you reach maximum medical improvement (MMI), the point where doctors can say what your future care will cost. Settling before MMI is the single most expensive mistake in truck litigation: once you sign a release, future surgeries come out of your pocket. If treatment takes 14 months, the case takes at least 14 months, and no lawyer can shortcut that without hurting you.

## Month-by-month: the truck accident lawsuit timeline

**Months 0–1: Investigation.** Your attorney sends the spoliation letter, pulls the police report, photographs the scene, and downloads the black-box and ELD data. The carrier's rapid-response team is usually on the scene within hours of the crash — your side is already playing catch-up, which is why this phase starts the day you hire counsel, not the day you feel ready.

**Months 1–6: Treatment and documentation.** You treat. Every appointment, every scan, every missed paycheck gets documented, because the demand package is built from this paper trail. Meanwhile your attorney pulls the carrier's FMCSA safety history, the driver's record, and maintenance logs. If liability is disputed, an accident reconstructionist is hired now.

**Months 6–9: Demand and negotiation.** Once you reach MMI — or once the treatment picture is stable enough to price — a demand package goes to the insurer: medical records, bills, wage loss, and a number. The insurer typically takes 30 to 90 days to respond. Their first offer is almost always low; the [average truck accident settlement](/blog/average-truck-accident-settlement-2026) guide shows how far first offers usually sit below final numbers.

**Months 9–12: Filing.** If negotiation stalls, the lawsuit is filed — always before your state's statute of limitations expires (see the table below). Filing changes the dynamic: the carrier must now produce its records under court supervision, and a trial date gets set, which creates the first real deadline the defense cannot ignore.

**Months 12–24: Discovery.** This is the longest phase and the one that surprises people most. Written questions, document production, depositions of the driver, the safety director, and your treating doctors, plus expert reports on reconstruction, trucking standards, and future medical costs. Discovery in a truck case routinely runs **6 to 18 months** because there are simply more records than in a car case.

**Months 18–30: Mediation.** Most courts order mediation before trial. By now both sides have seen the evidence, and most filed truck cases settle here — often in the weeks before the trial date, when the defense finally prices the risk of a jury. A case that settles at mediation typically resolves **18 to 30 months** after the crash.

**Months 24–36+: Trial and beyond.** If no settlement is reached, trial lasts days to a few weeks. A verdict can be followed by post-trial motions (30 to 60 days) and an appeal (6 months to 2 years). The full arc — crash to final, unappealable judgment — can pass 5 years in a catastrophic case with multiple defendants.

## Three worked examples: fast, typical, and slow

**The fast case — 8 months.** A rear-end collision by a box truck at a red light. Liability is on video. Injuries: a herniated disc, treated conservatively, MMI at 6 months. The carrier's policy is $1 million, the demand is documented, and the case settles pre-suit at month 8. No lawsuit is ever filed. This is the best-case shape, and it requires clear liability *and* a quick medical endpoint.

**The typical case — 22 months.** A jackknife on an interstate in [Texas](/attorneys/texas). Two defendants: driver and carrier. Injuries: fractured pelvis, surgery, MMI at 11 months. Demand at month 12, low offer at month 14, lawsuit filed at month 15. Discovery runs 8 months. Mediation at month 21 settles the case. Total: just under 2 years, with most of the time spent on treatment and discovery — not in a courtroom.

**The slow case — 4+ years.** A fatal underride crash with four defendants: driver, carrier, broker, and the trailer owner. Federal hours-of-service violations are alleged. The case involves wrongful death, a disputed black-box download, and two rounds of expert depositions. Mediation fails at month 30. Trial at month 40 ends in a plaintiff's verdict; the defense appeals, adding another 14 months. The family waits more than 4 years for final payment — which is why the settlement-versus-trial tradeoff deserves honest math before anyone chooses the courtroom.

Notice the pattern: in all three examples, the courtroom itself is a small slice of the timeline. The time goes into medicine, records, and negotiation.

## The deadline that matters most: your state's statute of limitations

The filing deadline is the one date that cannot be moved. Miss it and the claim is gone forever, no matter how strong the facts are. Filing deadlines for truck accident injury claims in the states with the most truck traffic:

| State | Injury filing deadline | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Texas | 2 years | Wrongful death also 2 years |
| California | 2 years | 6 months to file a claim if a government vehicle is involved |
| Florida | 2 years | Shortened from 4 years in 2023 — older cases keep the old rule |
| New York | 3 years | Wrongful death is 2 years |
| Georgia | 2 years | One of the busiest freight corridors in the country |
| Illinois | 2 years | Discovery rule can extend the clock for latent injuries |
| Louisiana | 2 years | Changed from 1 year in July 2024 — the old 1-year rule still applies to pre-change injuries |
| North Carolina | 3 years | Wrongful death is 2 years |
| South Carolina | 3 years | Modified comparative negligence — under 51% fault still recovers |
| Colorado | 3 years | Wrongful death is 2 years |

Two warnings. First, the deadline is measured from the crash date, not from when you finish treatment — a case that needs 18 months of treatment in a 2-year state leaves only 6 months to file, so the lawsuit often has to be filed while treatment is still ongoing. Second, claims against a government entity (a city truck, a state highway contractor) usually require a formal notice of claim in as little as 6 months, long before the lawsuit deadline. If a government vehicle was involved, the clock is much shorter than the table suggests.

A [truck accident attorney](/truck-accident-attorney) in your state can confirm the exact deadline for your facts — and the earlier that conversation happens, the more options you keep.

## What speeds a case up — and what slows it down

**Speeds it up:** clear liability (dashcam, black-box data, a cited driver), a single defendant, injuries that reach MMI quickly, a carrier with adequate policy limits that wants the file closed, and a demand package that is complete the first time — no missing records for the adjuster to use as an excuse for delay.

**Slows it down:** disputed liability, multiple defendants pointing at each other, catastrophic injuries with years of treatment, a policy-limits fight (the damages exceed the insurance, so the carrier has little incentive to settle early), a crowded court docket, and — most commonly — an insurer that treats delay as a strategy. Carriers know that an injured person waiting on a settlement is under financial pressure, and some will stretch negotiations on purpose. That is also why the [personal injury settlement timeline](/blog/personal-injury-settlement-timeline-month-by-month) on this site shows the same pattern across case types: the insurer's clock and your clock run at different speeds.

One factor people underestimate is their own medical timeline. Every month of treatment you genuinely need adds a month to the case — and that is a *good* delay, because it is the difference between a settlement that covers your future care and one that does not. The bad delays are the procedural ones: a defendant that drags out discovery, an expert who needs six months to write a report, a court that sets trial 18 months out.

## Should you settle fast or wait it out?

The tradeoff is real and it is arithmetic. A pre-suit settlement at month 8 might be $150,000. The same case, filed and mediated at month 22, might be $260,000 — but you waited 14 more months, paid more in litigation costs, and carried the risk that a jury could have gone the other way. There is no universal right answer, only a personal one: how much financial pressure are you under, how strong is the liability evidence, and how much more is realistically on the table?

Three rules of thumb help. First, never settle before MMI unless the offer already covers your worst-case future care — a release is permanent. Second, the biggest jumps in value usually come after depositions, when the defense has to price what a jury will hear from its own driver under oath. Third, if the carrier's offer has not moved in two rounds of negotiation, filing the lawsuit is usually what moves it — the data shows most truck cases settle after filing, not before.

If your case is in [Georgia](/attorneys/georgia) or any other high-freight state, the same math applies, but the court backlog in that county can add months to the discovery phase — ask your attorney what the local docket looks like before you assume the national averages apply to you.

## The bottom line

A truck accident lawsuit takes **6 months if everything goes right, 1 to 3 years in the typical filed case, and longer if it goes to trial or appeal**. Very little of that time happens in a courtroom. Most of it goes into treatment, federal trucking records, discovery, and negotiation — and the single biggest driver is how long your injuries take to reach a stable, priceable point.

So the practical takeaway is not a number, it is a sequence: preserve the electronic evidence in the first days, treat until your doctors can price your future, file before your state's deadline, and expect the case to settle at mediation after depositions — which is where most truck cases actually end.
