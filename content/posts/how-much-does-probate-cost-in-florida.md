When a family asks how much does probate cost in Florida, the first number they hear is usually the attorney's fee — and it is usually only half the bill. Florida is one of the few states that writes a presumed-reasonable attorney fee into statute, so the fee looks fixed, official, and easy to quote. What the quote leaves out is that the personal representative — often a son or daughter handling the estate — is entitled to a separate fee on the exact same schedule. On a $500,000 estate, the attorney's $15,000 statutory fee arrives with a matching $15,000 representative fee, and the family that budgeted $15,000 is suddenly looking at $30,000 before a single court filing fee is added.

This guide runs the full Florida probate math, not the headline half of it. You will see the statutory schedule worked out for four estate sizes, the homestead rule that can subtract a whole house from the fee calculation, the $75,000 fork between summary and formal administration, and the negotiation lever Florida law itself hands you: the statutory fee is presumed reasonable, not mandatory, and your attorney must disclose in writing that it can be negotiated.

## How much does probate cost in Florida? The short answer in numbers

For a typical uncontested estate, total probate cost in Florida usually lands between 3% and 7% of the estate's gross value. That range is wide because it stacks four separate bills:

- **Attorney's fee** under Florida Statutes § 733.6171 — the statutory schedule below.
- **Personal representative's fee** under Florida Statutes § 733.617 — calculated on the same schedule.
- **Court costs** — $399 to open a formal administration ($395 plus a $4 supplement) under § 28.2401, or $344 for a summary administration of $1,000 or more, $234 under $1,000.
- **Out-of-pocket costs** — publication of the notice to creditors (roughly $150–$250), certified copies, postage, and any appraisal or accounting fees.

A $200,000 estate therefore costs roughly $12,600 in total at statutory rates, a $500,000 estate roughly $30,600, and a $1 million estate roughly $60,600 — before any complication like a contested will, a creditor claim, or a property sale. The families who get shocked are not the ones with complicated estates. They are the ones who were quoted one fee and met the second one later. ## The double-fee trap: why the real bill is roughly twice the quoted fee

Florida law treats the attorney and the personal representative as two separately compensated jobs, even when the representative is a family member doing the work out of duty. Both fees are presumed reasonable at the same percentages, and both come out of the estate before beneficiaries receive anything.

Consider a $500,000 estate — a paid-off condo and some savings, a completely ordinary Florida inheritance:

| Cost item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Attorney's fee (statutory schedule) | $15,000 |
| Personal representative's fee (same schedule) | $15,000 |
| Court filing fee (formal administration) | $399 |
| Publication and certified copies | ~$200 |
| **Total** | **~$30,600** |

That is 6.1% of the estate, and the attorney's fee alone was only 3%. The trap is not that either fee is hidden — both are in the statutes. It is that only one of them appears in the first conversation. Two questions prevent the surprise: "What is the total of the attorney fee plus the representative fee on this estate?" and "Will the representative actually claim their fee, or waive it?" A family member serving as representative can waive the fee, and on a $500,000 estate that single waiver saves $15,000 — the largest discount available in the entire process, and it costs nothing to ask about.

## The statutory schedule, worked out dollar by dollar

Florida Statutes § 733.6171 sets the presumed-reasonable attorney fee for ordinary services in a formal administration. The schedule is a staircase, not a flat percentage: smaller estates pay flat amounts, and the percentage drops as the estate grows. The fee is calculated on the estate's compensable value — the probate assets plus income the estate earns during administration — not on what beneficiaries ultimately keep.

| Compensable estate value | Presumed-reasonable attorney fee |
|---|---|
| Up to $40,000 | $1,500 |
| $40,001 – $70,000 | $2,250 |
| $70,001 – $100,000 | $3,000 |
| $100,001 – $1 million | $3,000 + 3% of the amount over $100,000 |
| $1 million – $3 million | $30,000 + 2.5% of the amount over $1 million |
| $3 million – $5 million | $80,000 + 2% of the amount over $3 million |
| $5 million – $10 million | $120,000 + 1.5% of the amount over $5 million |
| Over $10 million | $195,000 + 1% of the amount over $10 million |

Worked examples, attorney fee only:

- **$150,000 estate:** $3,000 + 3% of $50,000 = **$4,500**.
- **$200,000 estate:** $3,000 + 3% of $100,000 = **$6,000**.
- **$500,000 estate:** $3,000 + 3% of $400,000 = **$15,000**.
- **$2 million estate:** $30,000 + 2.5% of $1,000,000 = **$55,000**.

Now double each figure for the representative's matching fee, and you have the real starting point for a formal administration. Note the staircase effect: a $100,000 estate pays $3,000, but a $101,000 estate pays $3,030 — the schedule is continuous at the joints, so there is no cliff where one extra dollar costs thousands. The cliffs in Florida probate are elsewhere, and the next section covers the biggest one.

## The homestead subtraction that can cut the fee by thousands

Here is the single most expensive misunderstanding in Florida probate: the fee is calculated on gross value, not equity — but homestead property is treated differently. A primary residence that qualifies as Florida homestead and passes to protected heirs is exempt property, and it does not count toward the compensable value used to calculate attorney fees. A formal homestead determination is part of the case, and attorneys often charge a separate flat fee of roughly $500–$1,000 for it. Compare that with what it saves.

Take an estate with a $400,000 house and $100,000 in bank accounts — $500,000 gross:

- **Without the homestead exclusion:** attorney fee $15,000, representative fee $15,000, combined $30,000.
- **With the house excluded as homestead:** compensable value $100,000, attorney fee $3,000, representative fee $3,000, combined $6,000, plus roughly $500–$1,000 for the homestead determination.

That is a difference of about $23,000 on the same estate, determined by one classification. The reverse trap also matters: a mortgaged house that is not homestead counts at its full gross value, not its equity. A $400,000 house with a $350,000 mortgage still contributes $400,000 to the fee calculation, even though only $50,000 of equity exists. If the estate's main asset is a mortgaged, non-homestead property, ask early how the fee base is being calculated — it is the number everything else multiplies.

## Summary vs formal administration: the $75,000 fork in the road

Florida offers two tracks, and the cheaper one is not available to every estate. Summary administration applies when the value of the non-exempt assets is $75,000 or less, or when the decedent has been dead for more than two years and no formal administration is pending. Formal administration covers everything else.

The cost difference is dramatic:

- **Summary administration:** filing fee $344 (or $234 under $1,000), no personal representative fee in the formal sense, and attorney fees often flat — a simple uncontested summary case commonly totals $1,500–$2,500 all-in.
- **Formal administration:** filing fee $399, both statutory fees apply, publication is required, and the process typically takes 6–12 months for an uncontested estate, longer if creditors or disputes appear.

The $75,000 threshold counts non-exempt assets, which is where the homestead rule reappears: a $400,000 homestead plus $50,000 in accounts can still qualify for summary administration, because the house is exempt and only $50,000 counts. Families routinely assume they are over the threshold because they added up everything, house included. Before accepting a formal-administration quote, ask the attorney to state the non-exempt total in writing. If it is $75,000 or less, the summary track should be on the table. ## The cash problem: who actually pays, and when

Every Florida probate fee is paid from the estate, not from the family's pockets in principle — but estates are often house-rich and cash-poor, and the timing creates a real problem. The attorney and representative fees are typically approved and paid near the end of administration, after creditor claims are resolved, so the estate usually does not need cash on day one. But filing fees, publication, and appraisal costs arrive early, and someone — usually the representative — advances them and is reimbursed later as a priority claim.

Three cash scenarios to plan for:

1. **Cash covers it.** An estate with $30,000 in accounts and a $30,600 bill is fine; the fees simply reduce what beneficiaries receive.
2. **The house must be sold.** If the estate is a $500,000 house and almost no cash, the fees may effectively require a sale — or the beneficiaries advance the costs and are repaid from the proceeds.
3. **A beneficiary advances costs.** This is common and legitimate, but get the arrangement in writing: who advanced what, and that reimbursement comes before distribution.

One more timing point: Florida's creditor claim window is generally three months from publication of the notice to creditors, which is why even a simple formal administration rarely closes in under five or six months. Anyone promising a two-week probate on a formal administration is describing a summary case or skipping steps.

## How to negotiate below the statutory schedule

The most useful sentence in Florida probate law for a family is the one most fee quotes omit: the statutory schedule is presumed reasonable, not mandatory, and the attorney must disclose in writing that the fee is subject to negotiation. For simple, uncontested estates, many Florida firms offer flat fees — commonly $3,500–$5,000 — that come in well below the statutory figure on a mid-size estate. On a $500,000 estate, a $5,000 flat fee instead of the $15,000 statutory fee saves $10,000 before the representative's fee is even discussed.

Use this script in the consultation:

- "What would the statutory fee be on this estate, and what flat fee would you offer for an uncontested case?"
- "Is the representative's fee being claimed or waived, and what is the combined total?"
- "What is the non-exempt value, and does this qualify for summary administration?"
- "Is the house homestead, and has the fee base excluded it?"
- "What costs are billed separately — publication, copies, appraisals — and roughly what do they total?"

Get the answers as a written fee agreement before signing. A firm that will not put the combined total in writing has told you something important. ## Florida probate cost checklist before you hire

Run through this list before you sign a fee agreement, and keep the answers with the estate file:

- [ ] **Statutory fee calculated:** the attorney has stated the § 733.6171 figure for your estate's compensable value.
- [ ] **Representative fee addressed:** claimed or waived, in writing, with the combined total stated.
- [ ] **Homestead classified:** the house's homestead status is determined and the fee base reflects it.
- [ ] **Track chosen for a reason:** summary vs formal is decided on the $75,000 non-exempt figure, not on habit.
- [ ] **Flat-fee quote obtained:** at least one flat-fee alternative is on the table for an uncontested case.
- [ ] **Out-of-pocket costs estimated:** filing, publication, copies, and appraisals are itemized, not left as "plus costs."
- [ ] **Cash plan agreed:** who advances early costs and how reimbursement works is written down.

Florida probate is expensive mainly because two fees run on the same schedule and the fee base is gross value — but both of those facts have lawful counters: a waived representative fee, a homestead exclusion, a summary track, and a negotiated flat fee. A family that walks into the first consultation knowing the schedule, the double fee, and the $75,000 fork will rarely pay the number on the first quote.
