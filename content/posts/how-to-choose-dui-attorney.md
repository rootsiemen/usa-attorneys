A DUI arrest puts two clocks on you at once: a criminal case in court, and a separate license action at the DMV that moves on its own deadline — often just 7 to 30 days, depending on the state. Most people hire the first [DUI attorney](/dui-attorney) whose ad they see, or the cheapest one. Both are mistakes, and both are expensive in ways that don't show up until months later.

Here's the uncomfortable truth about DUI defense: it is the most local practice in criminal law. The same blood-alcohol reading, the same traffic stop, the same prior record can produce a dismissal in one county and a conviction with jail time in the next. The difference is rarely the law on the books. It's the courtroom — the judge's habits, the prosecutor's offer patterns, and whether your attorney has stood in front of both of them a hundred times before.

So forget the billboards. Choose your DUI attorney with the local-court test below.

## 1. Start with the courtroom, not the credential

Every DUI attorney will tell you they "handle DUI cases." What you actually need to know is narrower: how many DUI cases have they handled *in the court where your case will be heard*?

Ask it exactly that way: "How many DUI cases have you handled in [your county's] court in the last year? Which judges do you appear before most?" A strong answer is specific — "About 60 last year, mostly before Judge Alvarez and Judge Chen, and I know both of their standard offer patterns." A weak answer is vague — "We practice throughout the region."

Why this matters so much: DUI outcomes turn on discretionary calls. One prosecutor routinely reduces a first offense to reckless driving when the breath test has a calibration gap; the prosecutor one county over never does. One judge grants suppression hearings generously; another almost never does. An attorney who knows these patterns negotiates from knowledge. An attorney who doesn't negotiates from hope — and bills you the same either way.

If your case is in a small county, this matters even more. In a two-judge courthouse, relationships and reputation carry real weight, and a generalist from the nearest big city may have appeared there twice in their career.

## 2. Ask how many DUI cases they actually take — and how many go to trial

DUI defense is a volume game for some firms, and volume is your enemy. A firm running 300 DUI cases a year with one attorney is not defending cases; it's processing pleas. Ask two numbers:

- "How many DUI cases do you personally handle per year?"
- "How many DUI trials have you done in the last two years, and what were the outcomes?"

A healthy DUI practice is usually in the range of 50 to 150 cases a year for a dedicated attorney, with several trials a year — not zero. Zero trials is a red flag, not a selling point. An attorney who never tries cases has no leverage: the prosecutor knows the case will plead out eventually, so the offer never improves. You don't necessarily want a trial — most DUI cases resolve without one — but you want an attorney the prosecutor believes *will* go to trial if the offer is bad.

Also ask about the split: first offenses versus repeat offenses versus felony DUI. A first-offense case and a third-offense case are different worlds — different penalties, different license consequences, different negotiation levers. If your case involves an accident, an injury, a child passenger, or a commercial license, say so upfront and ask whether they've handled that exact fact pattern.

## 3. Separate the two cases: criminal court and the DMV

This is the single most common — and most costly — thing people miss after a DUI arrest. In most states, your driver's license is handled in a separate administrative proceeding, and it runs on its own short deadline. Miss it and your license can be suspended automatically, even if the criminal case later ends in dismissal.

At your consultation, ask: "Will you handle the DMV or administrative license hearing too, and is that included in your fee?" If the answer is "that's separate" or "you can handle that yourself," be cautious. The DMV hearing is also a discovery opportunity: it's often the first chance to hear the arresting officer's testimony under oath, and a good attorney uses it to test the criminal case.

Check the timeline in our [72-hour checklist after a DUI arrest](/blog/what-to-do-after-dui-arrest-72-hour-checklist) — the DMV deadline is usually the first date on it. If an attorney you're interviewing doesn't bring up the DMV hearing unprompted, that's a signal about how carefully they run DUI cases.

## 4. Do the fee math before you sign anything

Most DUI attorneys charge a flat fee for a first-offense case, and the range is wide — roughly $1,500 to $5,000 for a straightforward first offense in most markets, and $5,000 to $15,000 or more for repeat or felony cases. (Our full breakdown is in [how much a DUI attorney costs](/blog/how-much-does-dui-attorney-cost-2026).) The number on the quote is not the number that matters. What matters is what the flat fee *covers*.

Ask these five questions, and get the answers in writing:

- Does the flat fee include trial, or does the price jump if the case goes to trial — and by how much?
- Does it include the DMV hearing?
- Does it include expert witnesses, like a toxicologist to challenge the breath or blood test?
- Does it include an appeal or a motion to suppress, if one is needed?
- What costs are extra — filing fees, subpoena costs, independent blood-test reanalysis?

A $2,500 flat fee that excludes trial, experts, and motions can become a $7,000 case. A $4,000 fee that includes all of them can be the cheaper option. This is the same discipline we recommend in our guide to [retainer agreement clauses to read before signing](/blog/retainer-agreement-clauses-to-read-before-signing) — never sign a fee agreement you haven't read line by line.

One more math check: payment plans. Many DUI attorneys offer them, and a plan is fine — but check whether the total price is higher on a plan than paid upfront, and whether the attorney stops working if a payment is late. You do not want your defense paused over a missed installment in the week before trial.

## 5. Test their knowledge of your specific defenses

You don't need to become a DUI law expert. You need to know whether the attorney in front of you is one. Ask: "Looking at a case like mine, what defenses would you investigate first?"

A real DUI attorney will reach for specifics without prompting. They'll ask about the reason for the traffic stop, whether the field sobriety tests were administered to standard, whether the breath machine's calibration and maintenance logs check out, whether the blood draw followed proper chain of custody, whether a medical condition or medication could explain the reading, and whether the observation period before the breath test was actually observed.

A generalist will give you generalities: "We'll review the evidence and look for weaknesses." That's not an answer; it's a shrug. Press once more: "What are the three most common suppression issues you see in this court?" If the answer stays vague, keep interviewing.

Also ask about rising blood alcohol — the argument that your BAC was still climbing between the stop and the test — and about the difference between a per se charge (based on the number alone) and an impairment charge (based on observed driving and behavior). These are the standard tools of the trade; a DUI attorney should discuss them fluently.

## 6. Score the consultation itself

The consultation is a preview of the entire relationship. Score it honestly on four things:

- **Did they ask about your deadlines?** A good DUI attorney opens with the DMV date and the first court date, not with their fee.
- **Did they explain the realistic range of outcomes?** Beware anyone who promises a dismissal before seeing the police report. Honest ranges sound like: "First offense, clean record, no accident — in this court, that's usually a reduction or a standard plea with a license suspension of X months, and here's what we can try to improve."
- **Did they explain the collateral consequences?** A DUI touches your insurance rates (often doubling or tripling for three to five years), your employment if you drive for work, your commercial license, your professional license if you're a nurse, teacher, or pilot, and your immigration status if you're not a citizen. If none of that came up, the attorney isn't thinking about your whole life.
- **Did they give you a straight answer about communication?** Ask who returns your calls and how fast. A DUI case stretches over months, with long silences between hearings. You need to know whether silence means "nothing is happening" or "your attorney is unreachable."

Bring a written list of questions — our guide to [questions to ask before hiring a criminal defense attorney](/blog/questions-to-ask-before-hiring-criminal-defense-attorney) is a good starting point — and notice whether the attorney welcomes the list or rushes past it.

## 7. Red flags that should end the interview

Walk away, politely, if you hear any of these:

- **A guaranteed outcome.** No ethical attorney guarantees a dismissal or a specific result. Guarantees are marketing, not law.
- **A price far below every other quote.** A $750 DUI defense usually means a plea entered at the first hearing with no investigation. The "savings" show up later as a conviction that costs you thousands in insurance increases.
- **Pressure to sign today.** The DMV deadline is real, but it is measured in days or weeks, not minutes. You have time for two or three consultations.
- **No local presence.** If the attorney has to look up which court your case is in, they are not your attorney.
- **They won't put the fee scope in writing.** Verbal fee promises are worthless. If it's not in the agreement, it doesn't exist.
- **They badmouth every other attorney in town.** Reputation in a small courthouse is a working asset. An attorney who burns it freely will burn yours too.

## 8. A simple scoring system for your shortlist

After two or three consultations, the candidates blur together. Score each one out of 10, two points each:

1. **Local court experience** — cases in your court this year, familiarity with the judge and prosecutor.
2. **DUI focus** — share of practice devoted to DUI, recent trial experience.
3. **DMV handling** — included in the fee, with a plan for the hearing.
4. **Fee transparency** — written scope, trial and expert costs spelled out.
5. **Communication** — clear answers, realistic outcomes, a named contact.

Anyone below 7 is a no. Between two candidates at 8 or above, pick the one who asked you the better questions — the attorney who probed your case hardest in the consultation is the one who will probe the prosecution hardest in court.

## 9. What to do in the first 48 hours after you hire

Once you've chosen, move fast on three things. First, get the DMV hearing requested and the fee agreement signed the same day — delays here are the ones that can't be undone. Second, preserve everything: the clothes you were wearing, receipts from the evening, names of passengers or witnesses, any video from the stop. Third, stop discussing the case with anyone except your attorney — including social media, where a single post about "the cop had it out for me" can become an exhibit.

Then let your attorney work. The best sign in the weeks that follow is not constant activity; it's a clear plan with dates attached: DMV hearing on this date, discovery review by this date, motions filed by this date, next court appearance on this date. If you have that, you hired well.

Choosing a DUI attorney is not about finding the most famous name or the lowest price. It's about finding the attorney who knows your courtroom, has tried cases like yours, handles both halves of your case, and tells you the truth about the range of outcomes before you sign. Use the local-court test first, the fee math second, and the consultation score third — and you'll make a decision you won't regret six months from now, when the case is over and the consequences are yours to live with.
