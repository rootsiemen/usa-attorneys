A dog bite is over in seconds, but the decisions you make in the next 72 hours can decide whether your medical bills get paid or land on you. Knowing what to do after a dog bite is not just about first aid — it is about evidence, deadlines, and insurance moves that most victims only learn about after they have already made the expensive mistake. This checklist covers all three, in the order that matters: stop the bleeding, lock down proof, get the right medical paper trail, and only then talk to anyone about money.

The core idea is simple. Insurers do not pay for pain they cannot see on paper. Every step below creates a piece of paper — a photo, a report, a bill, a written timeline — that turns a "he said, she said" bite into a documented claim.

## The first hour: safety, bleeding, and photos before anything dries

**1. Get away from the dog and stop the bleeding.** Move to a safe spot, press a clean cloth firmly on the wound, and keep pressure on for several minutes. If bleeding does not slow, if the bite is on the face, neck, or hands, or if the victim is a child, call 911 or go to the emergency room right away. Hand bites deserve special urgency — hand wounds infect fast and can threaten function.

**2. Wash the wound, but do not close the story yet.** Once bleeding slows, wash gently with soap and running water for several minutes. Do not apply hydrogen peroxide or try to seal a deep puncture yourself. Puncture wounds look small on the surface and run deep underneath, which is exactly why insurers later argue "it was only a minor bite" — a same-day medical record defeats that argument before it starts.

**3. Photograph everything before it changes.** Bite wounds change hourly: swelling rises, bruising spreads, and torn clothing gets thrown away. Before you leave the scene, photograph the wound from two distances, the torn clothing, the location (fence, gate, sidewalk, yard), and the dog if it is safe to do so. A photo taken today is worth ten descriptions given a month from now.

**4. Get the owner's name, address, and insurance — in writing.** Ask for the dog owner's full name, phone number, address, and homeowner's or renter's insurance information, plus the dog's vaccination history, especially rabies. If the owner refuses, note the address and any witness names, and let animal control or police collect the rest. Never accept a verbal promise of "I'll pay the bills" with no name attached to it.

**5. Say little about fault.** Do not apologize, do not say you "startled" the dog, and do not agree that the dog "never does this." Those sentences get repeated back to you by adjusters. A neutral line — "I need medical care, and I'll need your contact and insurance details" — protects you without starting a confrontation.

## The 72-hour evidence clock: what to do after a dog bite, day by day

Most dog bite claims are won or lost in the first three days, not in a courtroom a year later. Here is the clock that matters:

- **Hour 0–1:** Safety, bleeding control, photos, owner information, witnesses.
- **Hour 1–24:** Emergency or urgent care visit, animal control report, written timeline of exactly what happened while memory is fresh.
- **Day 2–3:** Follow-up visit if the wound worsens, daily wound photos showing progression, all bills and receipts saved in one folder, and no recorded statement given to any insurer.
- **Week 1–2:** Tetanus status confirmed, rabies risk assessed with the treating provider, missed-work note from your employer, and a written log of pain, sleep disruption, and activities you could not do.
- **Before any settlement:** A complete picture of the total cost — not just the first ER bill.

Why the rush? Animal control records get archived, witnesses forget details, surveillance footage from a nearby home or shop is overwritten within days, and the dog's vaccination records become harder to obtain once the owner talks to their own insurer. A claim filed six months later with a single blurry photo is a different claim from one filed with a dated, day-by-day record.

If you were bitten while working — a delivery driver, postal worker, or home-care aide, for example — report it to your employer in writing the same day as well. That written report can matter for a separate workplace claim, and our guide to [what to do after a workplace injury](/blog/what-to-do-after-workplace-injury-legal-checklist) covers that parallel track.

## Your state's dog bite rule changes everything

There is no single national dog bite law. Your state follows one of three approaches, and the approach decides how hard your claim is to prove:

| State approach | What you generally must show | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strict liability | The owner is liable when their dog bites, even without proof the owner knew the dog was dangerous | California, Florida |
| One-bite / negligence | You generally must show the owner knew (or should have known) the dog was dangerous, or was negligent — for example, violating a leash law | Texas, Georgia (with important exceptions) |
| Mixed / comparative fault | Recovery can be reduced by your share of fault, and in some states barred above a threshold | Georgia: recovery generally allowed if you were less than 50% at fault |

A few specifics worth knowing. California's strict-liability rule (Civil Code Section 3342) applies to bites in public places and places you were lawfully present — it generally does not require proving the owner knew the dog would bite. Georgia's dog bite statute (O.C.G.A. § 51-2-7) ties liability to the owner's failure to control the animal, knowledge of dangerous tendencies, or a leash-law violation, and Georgia claims generally carry a two-year filing deadline (O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33). Florida, for most negligence claims from injuries on or after March 24, 2023, also uses a two-year deadline — a sharp cut from the older four-year rule that still confuses many victims.

The practical lesson: never assume the deadline you heard from a friend in another state applies to you. Search your state's bar association site or a local [personal injury lawyer](/personal-injury-lawyer) directory page for your state's rule on day one, and calendar the deadline immediately. Two years sounds long; evidence does not last two years.

## Medical care: the paper trail that pays the claim

Dog bites carry infection risks that victims routinely underestimate — puncture wounds, hand bites, and bites to children or older adults escalate fast. That medical reality is also a claims reality: gaps in treatment are the insurer's favorite argument. "You waited nine days before seeing a doctor" becomes "the injury must not have been serious."

Build the trail this way:

- **Same-day visit, even for a "minor" bite.** Urgent care or the ER creates the anchor record: date, wound description, treatment, tetanus and rabies assessment.
- **Follow every instruction in writing.** If the provider says return in 48 hours, return in 48 hours. Missed follow-ups read as recovery, even when they are just scheduling problems.
- **Photograph the wound's progression daily.** One photo on day one and one on day seven showing swelling, drainage, or scarring tells a story no bill can.
- **Keep a one-page pain and activity log.** Note sleep lost, work missed, childcare you could not do, and activities avoided. Two sentences a day, dated, beats a perfect memory claimed a year later.
- **Track every dollar, including the small ones.** Bandages, prescriptions, parking at the clinic, the urgent-care copay, and the babysitter you hired because you could not lift your child — all of it belongs in the folder.

One cost surprise to plan for: rabies post-exposure treatment, when a provider orders it because the dog's vaccination status cannot be confirmed, can run into thousands of dollars across multiple visits. That single fact is why "the owner will cover it" is never a substitute for an insurance claim — an unvaccinated or unknown-status dog turns a $300 urgent-care visit into a multi-thousand-dollar course of treatment.

## The insurance math: whose policy pays, and what first offers hide

Most dog bite claims are paid by the dog owner's homeowner's or renter's insurance, not out of the owner's pocket. That changes the negotiation completely — you are dealing with a professional adjuster whose job is to close your file cheaply.

Three numbers on the owner's declarations page decide the shape of your claim:

- **Medical payments coverage (med-pay):** Often a small, no-fault pot — commonly in the low thousands — that can pay initial bills quickly without proving liability. Useful, but it is not the whole claim.
- **Personal liability limit:** Commonly $100,000 to $300,000 on a homeowner's policy. This is the real ceiling for most settlements, and it is why documenting the full cost matters more than arguing about pain.
- **Your own health insurance:** Your health plan may pay first and then seek reimbursement from the settlement (a lien). A settlement that ignores a $6,000 lien is not a $6,000 win.

Now the trap: the first offer. Adjusters often call within days with a fast, friendly number — enough to cover the ER bill plus a little extra. The offer arrives before scarring is assessed, before any scar-revision or therapy cost is known, and before missed work is tallied. Accepting it usually means signing a release that ends the claim forever.

Worked comparison, using round illustrative numbers: a $7,500 first offer that covers a $2,800 ER bill leaves you $4,700 ahead — until a $4,000 scar-revision consultation, $1,800 in follow-up visits, and two weeks of missed shifts worth $2,400 arrive later. The same claim, documented to $28,000 in total costs and losses, is a different negotiation. The difference was never the injury. It was the paperwork.

## Worked example: what a documented claim is actually worth

No article can price your case, but an illustrative example shows how the pieces fit. Take a hypothetical adult bitten on the hand by a neighbor's dog, with an ER visit, a follow-up, a short course of antibiotics, and ten days of missed shifts:

- Emergency visit and wound care: $2,800
- Follow-up visits and prescriptions: $900
- Rabies risk assessment and shots (status unconfirmed): $3,500
- Missed work, ten shifts: $2,400
- Scar and hand-function follow-up: $1,400
- **Total documented costs and losses: $11,000**

If that claim settles for $45,000 — a figure that reflects pain, scarring risk, and the documented costs above, not a promise — the contingency math on a standard one-third fee looks like this:

- Gross settlement: $45,000
- Attorney fee at 33.3%: $15,000
- Case costs (records, filing, expert review): $1,500
- Medical liens repaid: $8,000
- **Net to the victim: $20,500**

Change one input and the outcome swings. Skip the follow-up visits and the documented costs fall to $6,300; the insurer's "generous" $12,000 offer suddenly looks reasonable, and the net after fees and liens shrinks to a few thousand. Documentation is not bureaucracy here — it is the settlement.

Note what this example leaves out on purpose: permanent scarring, nerve damage, and psychological effects (fear of dogs, especially in children) can raise a claim's value substantially, but only when a provider documents them. "I still flinch around dogs" in a medical note is evidence; the same sentence told only to friends is not.

## Hire a lawyer or handle it yourself? A decision matrix

Not every dog bite needs a lawyer. Use this matrix honestly:

**You can often handle it yourself when:** the bite needed one visit, healed fully within two weeks, left no scar, cost under roughly $1,000, and the owner's insurer has already paid the bills in full without asking for a release. Even then, read any release word by word before signing.

**Get a consultation — usually free — when:** the bite needed multiple visits, involved the face, hands, or a child, may scar, required rabies treatment, kept you off work, or the insurer is asking for a recorded statement or pushing a quick release. Most dog bite lawyers work on contingency, so the consultation costs nothing and the fee comes only from a recovery. The [how to choose a personal injury lawyer](/blog/how-to-choose-personal-injury-lawyer) guide covers the vetting questions in full.

**Do not wait when:** the insurer has gone silent, liability is disputed ("you provoked the dog"), the owner is uninsured, or your state's deadline is within six months. Delay helps only the other side.

## Questions to ask before you hire a dog bite attorney

If you take a consultation, these five questions separate experienced dog bite handling from generic intake:

1. **How many dog bite cases have you handled in my state, and under which liability rule?** A lawyer who knows your state's rule — strict liability versus one-bite — frames the claim correctly from day one.
2. **Who pays the medical liens, and when?** Liens can eat a settlement after the fee. Get the lien strategy before you sign, not after.
3. **What happens if the owner's policy limit is low?** Ask about underinsured options, med-pay stacking, and whether your own policies fill gaps.
4. **Who actually handles my file day to day?** The lawyer you meet should not vanish behind a case manager you never chose.
5. **What is your fee if the case settles before a lawsuit versus after filing?** Some agreements raise the percentage once a lawsuit is filed. Know both numbers up front, and read the retainer clauses with the same care as our [retainer agreement guide](/blog/retainer-agreement-clauses-to-read-before-signing) recommends.

## The full checklist, in one place

Print this or save it to your phone — it is the whole article on one screen:

- [ ] Stop bleeding, wash with soap and water, seek same-day medical care
- [ ] Photograph the wound, clothing, location, and dog
- [ ] Get the owner's name, address, insurance, and the dog's vaccination records in writing
- [ ] Collect witness names and phone numbers
- [ ] Report to animal control (and police, if the bite was severe or the owner is unknown)
- [ ] Write a dated timeline of what happened, while memory is fresh
- [ ] Follow all medical instructions; photograph wound progression daily
- [ ] Save every bill, receipt, and missed-work note in one folder
- [ ] Do not give a recorded statement or sign a release without review
- [ ] Calendar your state's filing deadline on day one
- [ ] Get a free consultation if the bite was serious, scarring, or disputed

A dog bite claim is not about being aggressive — it is about being organized faster than the insurer. Safety first, evidence second, silence third, and money last, in that order. If you follow the checklist above, you give yourself the one thing most victims lose in the first week: options.
