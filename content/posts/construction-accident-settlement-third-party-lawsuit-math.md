A construction accident settlement is usually two claims wearing one name — and most injured workers only ever file the first one. The first claim is workers' compensation: fast, no-fault, and capped. It pays your medical bills and roughly two-thirds of your wages, and that is where it stops. The second claim is the third-party lawsuit — against the general contractor, a subcontractor, the property owner, or the company that made the defective equipment — and it is the one that pays for pain and suffering, the full value of your lost earning capacity, and, in the worst cases, punitive damages. The same injury can be worth $85,000 on the comp track alone and $650,000 once the third-party claim is added. That gap is not a rounding error. It is the whole case.

Construction remains the most dangerous work in America. In 2023, 1,075 construction workers died on the job — the highest number since 2011 and nearly one in five workplace deaths nationwide, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. OSHA's "Fatal Four" — falls, struck-by, caught-in/between, and electrocution — cause nearly 60% of those deaths. Non-fatal injuries are far more common: BLS data puts the construction injury rate at roughly 2.4 recordable cases per 100 full-time workers, and the average employer-paid cost of a single construction injury runs about $42,000. Yet those averages hide the real story, because a construction accident settlement is decided less by the injury itself than by *who* caused it and *which* legal track you are on. This guide works through the side-by-side math, the five parties who can be sued beyond your employer, and the three deadlines that quietly kill third-party claims.

## What a construction accident settlement actually pays — by injury

Settlement ranges for construction injuries run wide because the injuries themselves do. Published settlement data and law-firm case results cluster around these bands, and they are the starting point for any honest estimate:

- **Minor injuries** (sprains, lacerations, short recovery): **$15,000–$50,000**
- **Moderate injuries** (a fracture, surgery, months off work): **$50,000–$250,000**
- **Serious injuries** (multiple surgeries, lasting limitations, return to a different trade): **$150,000–$500,000**
- **Severe injuries** (amputation, serious burns, career-ending damage): **$500,000–$2,000,000+**
- **Catastrophic and wrongful death** (spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury, paralysis, fatality): **$1,000,000–$10,000,000+**

By injury type, the numbers sharpen further. A traumatic brain injury case commonly lands between $500,000 and $5,000,000. A spinal injury with permanent disability can exceed $2,000,000 once lifetime care and lost earnings are counted. A trench collapse that buries a worker has produced settlements of $6,500,000 in New York. And a 2,500-pound steel I-beam that toppled onto a delivery driver at a Los Angeles site — crushing his foot and triggering cascading medical complications — settled for $15,000,000 after the case survived a failed mediation and a key court ruling on the eve of trial. The pattern is consistent: the bigger the permanent consequence, the more the third-party track matters, because workers' comp has no mechanism to pay for a life that is permanently smaller.

## Workers' comp vs. third-party lawsuit: the side-by-side math

Here is the comparison most injured workers never see, worked through on one realistic case. Take a 38-year-old union carpenter earning $1,400 a week who falls 18 feet from an unguarded scaffold edge. He fractures his pelvis and two vertebrae, spends three weeks in hospital, has two surgeries, and is out of work for 14 months. His medical bills total $210,000. He returns to light duty at $700 a week — half his old pay — and will never climb scaffolding again.

**On the workers' comp track alone:**

- Medical bills: $210,000 — paid in full, but only for authorized treatment
- Lost wages: roughly two-thirds of $1,400 a week for 14 months — about **$56,000**
- Permanent partial disability: a scheduled award, often **$40,000–$80,000** depending on the state
- Pain and suffering: **$0** — workers' comp does not pay it, ever
- Future lost earning capacity: **$0** — comp pays what you lost, not what you will never earn again
- **Comp-track total: roughly $306,000–$346,000**

**Add the third-party lawsuit** — the scaffold was owned and erected by a subcontractor who skipped the guardrails, and the general contractor never inspected it:

- The same $210,000 in medical bills, but now recoverable in full, including future care
- Lost wages in full: $1,400 a week for 14 months — **$84,000**, not two-thirds
- Future lost earnings: 20 years of working at half capacity, $36,400 a year short — discounted to present value, roughly **$450,000**
- Pain and suffering: for a permanently altered life, a multiplier of 3–5 times medical costs is common in negotiation — **$630,000–$1,050,000**
- Loss of enjoyment of life and household services: **$50,000–$100,000**
- **Combined total: roughly $1.4 million–$1.9 million**

Same injury. Same worker. Same accident. The difference between the two columns is the third-party claim — and it is the reason the first question after any serious construction injury is not "how much is comp paying" but "who else was on that site." If your case involves a [construction accident lawyer](/construction-accident-lawyer), that question is the first thing they investigate, because the answer decides whether your case is a five-figure claim or a seven-figure one. Compare the fee math too: on a contingency basis, the [workers' comp attorney](/workers-compensation-attorney) track and the third-party track are usually handled together, so adding the second claim rarely adds a second fee — it adds recovery.

## The five parties you can sue beyond your employer

Workers' comp's grand bargain is simple: you give up the right to sue your employer, and in exchange you get benefits without proving fault. But that bargain covers your employer only. Everyone else on the site is fair game, and a modern construction site has many someones:

1. **The general contractor.** The GC controls the site, sets the safety plan, and coordinates the trades. When the GC fails to enforce fall protection, skips daily inspections, or rushes a schedule that makes safe work impossible, the GC can be liable for the injuries that follow. On multi-employer sites, OSHA can cite the GC as the "controlling employer" — and that citation becomes powerful evidence in the lawsuit.

2. **A subcontractor.** The crew that erected the scaffold, ran the crane, or poured the concrete. If their work created the hazard — an unguarded edge, an overloaded lift, a live wire left exposed — the subcontractor is a defendant. Scaffold cases are the classic example: the sub that built it wrong is liable even though the injured worker's own employer did nothing wrong.

3. **The property owner or developer.** Owners who retain control over the work, or who hire a contractor they know is unsafe, can be liable. In states with strict scaffold laws — New York's Labor Law § 240 is the famous one — the owner and GC face near-automatic liability for gravity-related falls, which is why New York construction settlements run so high. A New York case is worth checking against the [attorneys in New York](/attorneys/new-york) directory if that is where the injury happened.

4. **The equipment manufacturer.** A crane with a defective brake, a saw missing its guard, a harness that failed under load. Product-liability claims against manufacturers and rental companies carry a different proof standard — you do not need to show the manufacturer was careless, only that the product was defective — and they open a second insurance policy that is often much larger than the contractor's.

5. **The architect or engineer.** When a design defect — a load miscalculation, a missing shoring specification, a structural detail that could not be built safely — causes the collapse, the design professional can be liable. These are the rarest third-party defendants, but they appear in the biggest cases: collapses, crane failures, and structural failures.

The practical rule: if more than one company touched the thing that hurt you, there is probably more than one claim. The investigation that finds them — site photos, contracts, daily logs, OSHA citations, equipment maintenance records — is time-sensitive, which is why the deadlines in the next section matter so much.

## The three deadlines that quietly kill third-party claims

Workers' comp claims have their own short deadlines — often 30 days to report the injury, sometimes less. Third-party lawsuits have a different clock, and missing it ends the claim permanently, no matter how strong the facts are:

- **The statute of limitations: usually 2–3 years.** Most states give two or three years from the date of injury to file a personal injury lawsuit. Georgia gives two years. New York gives three for most construction injuries. A few states give only one. The clock starts on the day of the accident, not the day you realize a third party was at fault — and discovery of the defect is not always enough to restart it.

- **Claims against a government entity: 6 months or less.** If the project is public — a school, a highway, a municipal building — the defendant may be a government agency, and those claims require a formal notice of claim, often within 90 days to 6 months, before any lawsuit can be filed. Miss the notice and the lawsuit is barred, even if the statute of limitations has years left.

- **The evidence deadline: days, not years.** This one is not written in any statute, and it is the one that actually decides cases. Scaffold is dismantled. The crane is repaired. The site is paved over. The subcontractor's daily logs are "routinely deleted" after 90 days. The black box on the equipment is overwritten. A spoliation letter — a formal demand that the GC and subs preserve everything — must go out in the first days after the injury, and it only works if someone sends it before the cleanup does its work. Every week you wait, a piece of your case disappears.

## What actually moves the number up or down

Five factors decide where your construction accident settlement lands inside those wide ranges, and three of them are within your control in the first month:

- **Liability clarity.** A clear OSHA violation — missing guardrails, no fall protection, an uninspected scaffold — is worth more than a disputed one. OSHA citations are admissible in most states and they change the negotiation instantly: an insurer settles differently when the violation is in writing. Document the violation yourself if you can: photos of the unguarded edge, the missing harness anchor, the overloaded lift.

- **The multiplier on medical costs.** Insurers still use a rough multiplier method in negotiation: total medical costs multiplied by 1.5 to 5, depending on severity. A $210,000 medical bill with a multiplier of 3 is $630,000 in pain and suffering alone. The multiplier rises with permanent injury, visible scarring, and any injury that changes how you live day to day — and it falls when treatment gaps appear in the records. Consistent treatment is not just medical advice; it is valuation advice.

- **Lost earning capacity, not just lost wages.** The difference between "I missed 14 months" and "I will earn half as much for the next 20 years" is often the largest single line in a construction settlement. A vocational expert's report — what trades you can still work, at what pay — routinely adds six figures that a comp claim never sees. Get the report early, before the insurer's own expert frames your future as a desk job you never wanted.

- **Comparative fault.** In most states, your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault — and barred entirely if you are 50% or 51% at fault, depending on the state. "He wasn't wearing his harness" is the defense's favorite sentence, and it is why the question of whether a harness was *provided*, *fitting*, and *anchored* matters more than whether one was worn. Fault is argued, not assumed; an experienced negotiator reframes a 20% fault claim into a 5% one, and that swing alone can be worth $100,000.

- **Policy limits and collectability.** A settlement cannot exceed what the defendants can pay. A small subcontractor with a $1 million policy and no assets caps the case — unless the GC's larger policy or the equipment manufacturer's policy is also in play. This is the hidden reason the five-party investigation matters: each additional defendant is another policy, and another policy is another ceiling raised. The [workers' comp settlement payout math](/blog/workers-compensation-settlement-payout-math) shows the same principle on the comp side — the ceiling is set by the system, and the only way past it is a second claim.

## The first-offer trap, and how the counter-offer works

The insurer's first offer on a construction case is almost always low — often 30% to 50% of the claim's real value — and it usually arrives while you are still in treatment, when the future cost is unknowable and the pressure to take cash is highest. The pattern is so consistent it has a shape: a quick call, a sympathetic tone, a number that covers the bills you have *so far*, and a release form that ends everything else. Sign it, and the third-party claim dies with it, even if the GC's negligence is discovered a month later.

The counter-offer that moves adjusters has three parts, and each one answers a question the first offer ignored:

1. **A complete damages packet.** Not just the bills, but the future-care estimate from your surgeon, the vocational report on lost earning capacity, and the daily-life impact statement — the things that turn a $210,000 medical bill into a $1.4 million case. Adjusters price what they can see; make them see all of it.

2. **A liability package.** The OSHA citation, the photos, the contract showing who controlled the scaffold, the sub's own safety manual that its crew violated. Liability evidence does not just prove fault — it raises the adjuster's reserve, the internal number that caps what they can offer without supervisor approval.

3. **A credible trial threat.** The cases that settle highest are the ones the insurer believes will go to a jury. A filed lawsuit, an expert retained, a trial date on the calendar — each one moves the number, because a jury that hears "1,075 workers died last year" and "the guardrail was never installed" does not think in multipliers. It thinks in verdicts.

## When comp alone is enough — and when it is not

Not every construction injury needs a third-party lawsuit. A minor injury with a full recovery, a clear comp claim, and no other party at fault is a comp case, and adding a lawsuit would cost more in time than it returns in money. But three signs mean the comp track alone is leaving money on the table, and they are worth checking before any release is signed:

- **Someone other than your employer was on the site.** If a GC, a sub, or an equipment supplier had any role in the work that hurt you, there is a claim to investigate. Most construction sites have five or six employers on them at once.
- **The injury is permanent or career-changing.** Pain and suffering and lost earning capacity are the two largest lines in a serious case, and comp pays neither. If you cannot return to your trade, the comp award is a fraction of the loss.
- **The comp carrier is already talking about a settlement.** A lump-sum comp settlement almost always includes a release — and that release can be written to extinguish third-party rights if you are not careful. Never sign a comp settlement before the third-party investigation is complete; the order of the two settlements matters, because the comp carrier also has a lien on the third-party recovery, and a good negotiator reduces that lien as part of the deal.

## Your 30-day checklist after a construction injury

1. **Report the injury in writing, same day.** Tell your supervisor and file the written report. Verbal notice fades; a dated paper trail does not.
2. **Get medical care and keep every record.** Emergency care, follow-ups, physical therapy, prescriptions — and no gaps in treatment. A six-week gap is the insurer's favorite argument.
3. **Photograph the scene before it changes.** The unguarded edge, the missing harness anchor, the damaged equipment, the posted safety plan. Take them yourself; the site will look different tomorrow.
4. **Do not sign anything from any insurer.** Not the comp carrier's settlement, not the GC's "incident report" that asks you to describe fault, and never a release. A signature is the cheapest way to lose a six-figure claim.
5. **Send a spoliation letter through a lawyer.** It costs little and it freezes the evidence — contracts, logs, maintenance records, inspection reports — before the cleanup crew and the document-retention schedule do their work.
6. **Identify every company on the site.** Get the names of the GC, every sub, the equipment rental company, and the property owner. Each one is a potential defendant and a potential policy.
7. **Track every dollar.** Medical bills, mileage to appointments, lost wages, help you had to hire at home. The damages packet is built from this ledger, and the ledger starts the day of the injury, not the day someone asks for it.

A construction accident settlement is not a lottery ticket and it is not a single number pulled from a table. It is two claims, priced on different rules, and the difference between them is usually the largest sum of money an injured worker will ever see in one case. Workers' comp gets you treated and keeps the lights on. The third-party lawsuit is what pays for the life the injury took. File the first, investigate the second, and never sign away the second while you are still deciding about the first.
