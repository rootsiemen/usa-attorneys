Ask three families how long probate took and you will hear three different answers: "four months," "fourteen months," "we are still waiting after two years." All three are telling the truth. Probate timelines are not set by one national rule. They are set by your state's creditor-claim window, whether the will is contested, how many assets need valuing, and how backlogged your county's probate court is.

The national picture: a simple, uncontested probate with a valid will and cooperative heirs typically closes in **6 to 9 months**. An average estate with real estate and a few creditor claims takes **9 to 18 months**. A contested estate, or one with a business, out-of-state property, or missing heirs, can run **18 to 36 months** — and every extra month costs real money in mortgage payments, taxes, insurance, and attorney time. The same court system produces all three outcomes. The difference is the facts of the estate, not the law.

This guide walks through probate month by month, with the state deadlines that set a hard floor on how fast you can finish, the math on what delays really cost, and the shortcuts that let some estates skip probate entirely. ## The 30-Second Version

Every probate passes through the same sequence: **filing and executor appointment (months 0–1), inventory and appraisal (months 1–4), the creditor-claim window (months 2–6, depending on the state), paying debts and taxes (months 4–9), and distribution and closing (months 9–18)**. Roughly **90% of probates close without a trial** — but "closing" in month 16 costs far more than "closing" in month 7. Every state also imposes a minimum creditor-claim period — 4 months in California and Texas, 3 months in Florida, 7 months in New York, up to a year in Pennsylvania. No distribution to heirs can safely happen before that window closes, because a late creditor claim could claw the money back. The single biggest lever on your timeline is whether anyone contests the will or a creditor's claim: each contested issue adds roughly **3 to 12 months** to the calendar.

## Months 0–1: Filing the Petition and Getting Appointed

Probate does not start automatically when someone dies. It starts when someone — usually the person named as executor in the will, or the closest relative if there is no will — files a **petition for probate** with the county court and asks to be appointed as the estate's personal representative.

Filing itself is fast: an attorney can usually prepare and file the petition within **1 to 3 weeks** of being retained. Filing fees run **$200 to $500** in most counties. The court then schedules a hearing, typically **4 to 8 weeks** after filing, and issues **letters testamentary** (or letters of administration when there is no will). Those letters are the executor's legal authority — without them, no bank will release funds, no property can be sold, and no creditor can be paid from estate money.

Two things commonly slow this phase down:

- **No original will.** Courts generally require the signed original, not a photocopy. A lost original can add **2 to 6 months** while the court decides whether a copy is admissible.
- **Out-of-state heirs or missing heirs.** Every heir entitled to notice must be formally notified, usually by mail and sometimes by publication. Tracking down a missing heir can stall the entire case before it starts.

**Worked example:** A parent dies in Ohio on November 1. The executor hires an attorney November 10, files November 20, and gets a hearing December 18. Letters issue the same day. Total time from death to legal authority: **about 7 weeks**. If the original will cannot be found, that same estate might not get letters until March — a **3-month delay before a single bill is paid**.

## Months 1–4: Inventory, Appraisal, and the Creditor Clock

Once appointed, the executor's first real job is the **inventory**: a complete list of everything the deceased owned — bank accounts, real estate, vehicles, investments, business interests, personal property — plus everything owed. Most states require the inventory to be filed with the court within **60 to 90 days** of appointment.

The expensive sub-projects that live in this phase:

- **Real estate appraisals.** Straightforward: **$300 to $600** per property, **1 to 3 weeks**. A court-appointed probate referee in California sets the value for most assets, which adds formality but also a queue.
- **Business valuation.** If the deceased owned a business, the court needs a valuation. A certified valuation costs **$5,000 to $20,000** and takes **2 to 4 months** — and it can delay everything else, because the business's value affects every heir's share.
- **Finding hidden accounts.** Old 401(k)s, forgotten bank accounts, and life insurance policies with no named beneficiary all surface slowly. Executors who skip a thorough search risk personal liability for missed assets.

At the same time, the **creditor clock** starts running. The executor publishes a notice to creditors in a local newspaper and mails notice to known creditors. Creditors then have a fixed window to file claims — and that window is the single most important number in the whole probate timeline:

- **California:** 4 months from the date letters issue (or 60 days after direct notice to a known creditor, whichever is later).
- **Texas:** 4 months from the date notice is published.
- **Florida:** 3 months from first publication for unknown creditors; 30 days after service for known creditors.
- **New York:** 7 months from the date letters issue.
- **Illinois:** 6 months from publication.
- **Ohio:** 6 months from the decedent's death.
- **Pennsylvania:** 1 year from the date notice is published — the longest common window in the US.

**Worked example:** Two identical estates, one in Florida and one in Pennsylvania, both with letters issued November 1. The Florida estate's creditor window closes around **February 1**. The Pennsylvania estate's window does not close until **November 1 of the following year** — a full 9 months later — before the executor can safely distribute anything. Same assets, same family, **9 months difference purely from the state**.

## Months 4–9: Paying Debts, Taxes, and the Fights That Start Here

Once the creditor window closes, the executor pays valid claims from estate funds, in the priority order the state sets: secured debts and mortgages first, then funeral expenses, then taxes, then unsecured creditors. Only what remains goes to heirs.

This is also the phase where disputes usually erupt, and each one has a price tag in months:

- **A contested claim.** A creditor whose claim is rejected can sue the estate. Even a small dispute adds **3 to 6 months** while the claim is litigated or settled.
- **A will contest.** A disinherited heir challenging the will — typically on grounds of lack of capacity or undue influence — is the most expensive delay in probate. Contested wills routinely add **12 to 24 months**, and the estate pays both sides' litigation costs in many cases.
- **Final income taxes and estate taxes.** The deceased's final income tax return is due April 15 of the following year, and estates above the federal exemption (over **$13 million** per person in recent years) owe federal estate tax 9 months after death. Tax preparation alone can hold distribution until the return is filed and accepted.
- **Selling real estate.** A house that needs repairs, sits in a slow market, or has multiple heirs who disagree on price can take **3 to 9 months** to sell — and the estate keeps paying the mortgage, taxes, and insurance the whole time.

**The delay math nobody does until it is too late:** a $350,000 house with a $1,900 monthly mortgage payment, $400 a month in property taxes, and $150 a month in insurance costs the estate **$2,450 per month** just to sit empty. A 12-month delay in selling costs **$29,400** — money that comes directly out of the heirs' inheritance. A 6-month will contest on top of that pushes the carrying cost past **$44,000**. Speed is not just convenience in probate. It is money.

## Months 9–18: Distribution, the Final Accounting, and Closing

When debts and taxes are paid, the executor prepares a **final accounting** — a report showing every dollar that came in and every dollar that went out — and asks the court to approve it and authorize distribution. Heirs sign receipts, the money and property go out, and the court closes the estate.

In a clean case, this phase takes **1 to 3 months**. It stretches when:

- An heir refuses to sign a receipt or waiver, forcing a formal court hearing instead of an informal closing (**+2 to 4 months**).
- The estate holds an asset that cannot be divided — a family cabin, a business — and heirs must agree on a buyout price (**+3 to 12 months**).
- A tax audit or an amended return is filed after distribution, which can reopen the estate.

**The attorney-fee math, using California's statutory schedule as the worked example:** California sets probate attorney fees by statute — 4% of the first $100,000, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000, and 1% above $1 million. On a **$500,000 estate**, that is $4,000 + $3,000 + $6,000 = **$13,000** in statutory attorney fees, plus a similar amount for the executor in many cases. On a **$1 million estate**, it is $4,000 + $3,000 + $16,000 = **$23,000**. These fees are set by law in California regardless of how simple the estate is — which is one reason California probates feel expensive even when nothing goes wrong. Most other states use "reasonable fee" standards instead, which usually land in a similar range for a comparable estate.

## The State-by-State Cheat Sheet

| State | Creditor-claim window | Typical total timeline | Small-estate shortcut threshold* |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | 4 months | 12–18 months | $184,500 |
| Texas | 4 months | 6–12 months | $75,000 |
| Florida | 3 months | 9–12 months | $75,000 |
| New York | 7 months | 12–24 months | $50,000 |
| Illinois | 6 months | 9–15 months | $100,000 |
| Ohio | 6 months from death | 9–12 months | $35,000 |
| Pennsylvania | 1 year | 12–24 months | $50,000 |

\*Small-estate thresholds change by legislation and usually exclude real estate or cap it separately — check the current statute for your state before relying on a shortcut. The pattern in the table is the point: states with short creditor windows and generous small-estate thresholds (Texas, Florida) close faster on average than states with long windows (New York, Pennsylvania), even for identical estates.

## When You Can Skip Probate Entirely

Not every estate needs this process at all. The fastest probate timeline is no probate, and these are the routes that get families there:

- **A revocable living trust.** Assets titled in the trust's name pass to beneficiaries without court involvement, usually in **1 to 3 months** — which is why a trust is the single biggest timeline decision a person makes while alive.
- **Joint ownership and beneficiary designations.** Bank accounts with a payable-on-death beneficiary, retirement accounts with named beneficiaries, and jointly owned property with right of survivorship all pass outside probate automatically.
- **Small-estate affidavits.** Estates under the thresholds in the table above can often be settled with a sworn affidavit and no court case at all, in **30 to 60 days**.
- **Transfer-on-death deeds.** Available in a growing number of states, these let a homeowner name a beneficiary for real estate directly on the deed.

The comparison that decides most families' planning: a **$400,000 estate in California** through full probate takes roughly **12 to 18 months** and about **$13,000+ in statutory fees**; the same estate held in a living trust typically distributes in **2 to 3 months** for a fraction of that cost. That gap is why estate planning attorneys ask about trusts before they ask about wills.

## How Executors Actually Speed Probate Up

Five moves that consistently shave months off a timeline, in order of impact:

1. **File within 30 days of death.** Every week of delay at the start is a week added at the end, because the creditor window cannot start until letters issue.
2. **Publish creditor notice immediately after appointment.** The clock is the floor — starting it late is the most common self-inflicted delay.
3. **Get appraisals ordered in week one.** Appraisers and business valuators have queues; ordering late means waiting in them.
4. **Settle, do not litigate, small disputes.** A $5,000 creditor claim fought for 6 months costs more in attorney time than the claim is worth.
5. **Communicate with heirs monthly.** Most will contests and receipt refusals start as silence and suspicion, not as legal strategy. A one-page monthly update prevents most of them.

The bottom line: probate takes as long as your state's creditor window plus the time your estate's complications demand. A simple estate in a fast state closes in about half a year; a contested estate in a slow state takes two. The executor who files fast, starts the creditor clock immediately, and keeps heirs informed is the one whose estate lands on the short end of that range.
