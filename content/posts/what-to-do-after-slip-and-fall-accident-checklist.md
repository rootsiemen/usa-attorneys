Most people get the first ten minutes after a fall wrong, and that mistake costs them later. They apologize, they brush themselves off, they walk out of the store without a single photo — and then, three weeks later, when the back pain turns out to be a herniated disc, the wet floor has been mopped, the security footage has been overwritten, and the store's insurer is asking why there is no record that anything happened at all. Knowing what to do after a slip and fall accident is not about being dramatic in the moment. It is about building a paper trail while the evidence still exists, because in a fall case the evidence has a shorter shelf life than your injuries do.

This guide works on a clock, not a vague list. The first hour is about safety and proof. The first 72 hours are about medical documentation. The first 30 days are about deadlines and decisions. Follow it in order, and you will walk into any conversation with an insurer — or a [personal injury lawyer](/personal-injury-lawyer) — with a file that speaks for itself.

## What to Do After a Slip and Fall Accident in the First Hour

The first hour decides what your claim looks like six months from now. Do these five things before you leave the property, in this order.

**1. Stay put and assess, then get help if you need it.** Adrenaline masks injuries. A fall that feels like a bruised hip at 2 p.m. can be a fractured wrist or a concussion by dinner. If you hit your head, lost consciousness even briefly, cannot put weight on a limb, or feel numbness, call 911 and do not drive yourself. A same-day emergency record is the single strongest piece of evidence that your injuries came from this fall and not from something later.

**2. Report the fall and get the report in writing.** Tell the manager, the property owner, or the employee on duty, and ask for a written incident report. Get the name of the person you spoke to, the time, and a copy or a photo of the report itself. If they refuse to give you a copy, photograph the report number or email yourself a note describing the conversation while you are still standing there. A fall with no incident report is a fall the property owner can later claim never happened.

**3. Photograph everything before it changes.** Take wide shots of the whole area, close-ups of the hazard (the puddle, the broken tile, the icy patch, the missing warning sign), and photos of your visible injuries and your clothing. Capture the absence of things too: no wet-floor sign, no cone, no mat. Hazards get cleaned up within minutes of a report, so your phone camera is the only witness that cannot be mopped away.

**4. Collect witnesses while they are still there.** Ask anyone who saw the fall for a name and a phone number. Shoppers leave, employees change shifts, and a witness you could have reached in two minutes becomes unreachable in two days. One independent witness statement is worth more than ten pages of argument about what the floor looked like.

**5. Say little about fault.** Do not apologize, do not say "I should have watched where I was going," and do not tell the manager "I'm fine." Those sentences end up in the incident report and in the insurer's file. You can be polite without narrating blame: "I fell on the wet floor near aisle four, I need an incident report, and I am going to get checked out."

## The First 72 Hours: Build the Medical Paper Trail

Insurers do not pay for pain. They pay for documented treatment, and the gap between the fall and your first doctor visit is the first thing they measure. A 72-hour gap is explainable. A three-week gap is an argument that you were not really hurt.

- **See a doctor within 24 to 72 hours, even if you feel "mostly okay."** Soft-tissue injuries, concussions, and back injuries routinely appear a day or two after the impact. Tell the doctor exactly how you fell and where, so the visit note links the injury to the fall. That link — mechanism of injury, written in a medical record — is what turns a complaint into a claim.
- **Follow the treatment plan and keep every appointment.** Missed physical therapy sessions and skipped follow-ups get read as recovery, not as a busy schedule. If you cannot make an appointment, reschedule it rather than dropping it.
- **Keep the shoes and clothes you were wearing.** Do not wash them. A worn sole, a torn seam, or a wet stain on a shirt is physical evidence, and defense lawyers love to argue that a claimant's footwear caused the fall. Preserving the shoes removes that argument before it starts.
- **Start a simple daily log.** One line a day: pain level, what you could not do, what it cost you. "Day 4: could not lift my toddler, missed a shift, $180 urgent-care copay." Six months later, you will not remember day 4. The log will.
- **Do not give a recorded statement to the property owner's insurer yet.** Their adjuster will call fast, sound friendly, and ask you to describe what happened "for the file." That recording can be used against you. You are not obligated to give one on their timetable.

## The First 30 Days: Build the File, Not Just the Story

By day 30, most fall claims are won or lost on organization. Here is the file to assemble:

1. **The incident report** and the name of every employee you spoke to.
2. **All medical records and bills** so far, including the ER or urgent-care visit, imaging, prescriptions, and therapy.
3. **Proof of lost income** — pay stubs before and after, a letter from your employer about missed shifts, or invoices if you are self-employed.
4. **Receipts for every fall-related expense** — medication, braces, rides to appointments, help you had to hire at home.
5. **Your photo set and witness contacts**, backed up somewhere other than your phone.
6. **Any communication from the insurer**, saved in full, including envelopes with postmarks.

One more step matters in this window: ask, in writing, that the property preserve its security camera footage. Video systems typically overwrite footage on a 14-to-30-day cycle, and a short preservation letter — sent by email so you have a timestamp — can stop a routine deletion from erasing your best evidence. If you later work with an attorney, this is often the first letter they send, and sending it yourself in week one costs nothing.

## The Evidence Clock: What Disappears and When

Not all evidence decays at the same speed, and that is why the order of this checklist matters. Think of it as four clocks running at once:

| Evidence | Typical lifespan | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The hazard itself (puddle, ice, broken step) | Minutes to hours | Photograph it before you leave |
| Witness memory and availability | Days | Get names and numbers on day one |
| Security camera footage | 14–30 days | Send a written preservation request in week one |
| Medical proof of the injury's cause | 72 hours for the strongest link | See a doctor within 72 hours |
| The legal deadline to file | 1–6 years by state | Know your state's number (see below) |

The pattern is simple: the evidence that proves the fall happened disappears fastest, while the evidence that proves how badly you were hurt takes weeks to build. People who wait reverse the order — they focus on treatment and forget the scene — and by the time they are ready to file, the scene is gone.

## Deadlines by State: The Number That Ends Claims

Every state sets a statute of limitations for personal injury claims, and missing it ends the case no matter how strong the facts are. The most common window is two years, but it varies, and falls on government property (a city sidewalk, a public building, a transit station) usually carry a much shorter notice deadline — sometimes as little as 90 days in New York or six months in California — before you can even file.

| State | Typical personal injury deadline | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| California | 2 years | 6-month claim deadline for government property |
| Texas | 2 years | Modified comparative fault: recovery barred at 51% fault |
| New York | 3 years | 90-day notice of claim for city/county property |
| Florida | 2 years | Shortened from 4 years in 2023 for negligence claims |
| Illinois | 2 years | 1-year notice for claims against local government |
| Pennsylvania | 2 years | Strict 2-year limit with few exceptions |
| Ohio | 2 years | Discovery rule rarely helps in fall cases |
| New Jersey | 2 years | 90-day notice for public-entity claims |

Treat this table as a starting point, not a guarantee: the exact deadline can shift with the type of defendant, the victim's age, and when the injury was discovered. The practical rule is to assume the shortest deadline that could apply to you and act inside it. If your fall happened on government property, treat the first 90 days as the real deadline. ## What a Slip and Fall Claim Is Actually Worth: A Worked Example

Nobody can tell you what your claim is worth from a chart, but the math behind every valuation is the same, and understanding it keeps you from accepting a low first offer. Consider a hypothetical grocery-store fall with a torn meniscus that needs arthroscopic surgery:

- Emergency room and imaging: $4,800
- Surgery and follow-up: $11,600
- Physical therapy (12 sessions): $2,000
- **Total medical expenses: $18,400**
- Missed work (4 weeks at $1,550 per week): $6,200
- **Total economic losses: $24,600**

Non-economic losses — pain, limitation, and disruption to daily life — are then weighed against those economic losses. In a straightforward case with clear liability and documented treatment, insurers often value the total case in the range of 1.5 to 3 times the economic losses, which would put this example roughly between $37,000 and $74,000 before any fault reduction. If the injured person were found 20% at fault — say, for looking at a phone while walking — a $50,000 valuation would drop to $40,000 in a comparative-fault state. Run your own figures through our free [slip and fall settlement calculator](/tools/slip-and-fall-settlement-calculator) — it weighs how clear the owner's responsibility is, not just the bills.

Three things move that number more than anything else: a same-week medical record tying the injury to the fall, a photo set proving the hazard existed, and a complete treatment history with no unexplained gaps. A case with all three settles faster and higher than a case with a bigger injury but a thinner file. That is the whole logic of this checklist.

## The 5 Settlement Traps After a Fall

1. **The quick check.** An adjuster offers $2,500 within a week, before you know whether you need surgery. Once you sign a release, later treatment is on you, even if the diagnosis changes.
2. **The recorded statement.** A casual "I feel fine" on day two becomes exhibit A against a day-twenty MRI.
3. **The medical authorization.** A blanket release lets the insurer dig through years of unrelated medical history looking for a pre-existing condition to blame.
4. **The social media post.** A photo of you at a family barbecue, posted while you claim limited mobility, will be found. Assume everything public is read.
5. **The delay trap.** Every month without treatment or documentation is a month the insurer uses to argue the injury came from something else. Silence is not neutral in a claim; it is evidence for the other side.

If an offer arrives before your treatment is finished, the safest response is no response at all until you know the full cost of the injury. A claim is worth what the complete medical picture shows, not what the first phone call suggests.

## When to Talk to a Personal Injury Lawyer

Most fall cases do not need a lawyer on day one, but some need one early, and waiting too long is the expensive mistake. Consider a consultation — most personal injury lawyers offer one free — if any of these apply: you needed surgery or hospitalization, you missed more than a week of work, the property owner disputes that the fall happened, the insurer is pressing you for a recorded statement or a quick settlement, or the fall happened on government property with its short notice clock. Our breakdown of [contingency fees](/blog/personal-injury-lawyer-contingency-fee-math) explains how the typical no-win-no-fee arrangement works, so you can judge whether representation makes financial sense for your case.

Bring your file to that first conversation: the incident report, your photos, your medical records, your expense receipts, and your daily log. A lawyer can evaluate a complete file in one meeting; a thin file takes weeks of back-and-forth. And if you decide to handle a minor claim yourself, the same file is what makes the insurer take your demand letter seriously.

The checklist, in one line: photograph the scene, report it in writing, see a doctor within 72 hours, preserve the video before it is overwritten, and know your state's deadline before the insurer's clock becomes yours. Falls happen in seconds. Claims are built in the thirty days after — and the people who build them early are the ones who get paid fairly for them.
