Ask five people how long their divorce took and you will get five answers: "six weeks," "nine months," "three years." They are all telling the truth — because divorce timelines are not set by a single rule. They are set by your state's mandatory waiting period, whether you and your spouse can agree on anything, how many assets and custody questions need valuing, and how busy your county's family court docket is.

The national picture: an uncontested divorce with no children and no property disputes typically finalizes in **1 to 3 months**. A contested divorce with custody or asset fights averages **9 to 14 months**. A high-conflict divorce that goes to trial can run **18 to 36 months** — and cost ten to twenty times what the uncontested version costs. The same legal system produces all three outcomes. The difference is the facts of the case, not the law.

This guide walks through the divorce process month by month, with the timeframes attorneys actually see, the state waiting periods that set a hard floor on how fast you can finish, and the math on what delays really cost. ## The 30-Second Version

Every divorce passes through the same sequence: **filing and service (month 1), response and temporary orders (months 1–3), discovery and valuations (months 3–6), mediation and negotiation (months 6–12), and trial prep/trial (months 12–24+)**. Roughly **95% of divorces settle before trial** — but "settling" in month 10 costs far more than "settling" in month 2. Every state also imposes a **minimum waiting period** between filing and final judgment — 6 months in California, 60 days in Texas, and in some states no minimum at all. The single biggest lever on your timeline is how many issues you and your spouse contest: each contested issue adds roughly **2 to 6 months** to the calendar.

## Months 0–1: Filing, Service, and the Waiting-Period Clock

The divorce clock does not start when you decide to divorce. It starts when the **petition is filed with the court and served on your spouse** — service is the event that triggers the waiting period in most states and the response deadline (typically **20 to 30 days**, depending on the state).

Filing itself is fast: most attorneys can draft and file a petition within **1 to 2 weeks** of being retained. Filing fees run **$200 to $400** in most counties (up to $435 in California). Service of process — having a process server hand the papers to your spouse — usually takes **3 to 14 days** and costs **$50 to $100**.

This is where the state waiting period enters. The waiting period is the minimum time between service (or filing) and the earliest date the court can enter a final judgment:

- **California:** 6 months from service — the longest common waiting period in the US. Even a perfectly amicable, one-issue divorce cannot legally finalize faster than six months.
- **Texas:** 60 days from filing — the minimum is short, but the average Texas divorce still takes **6 to 12 months** because agreement on custody and property takes longer than the statute requires.
- **New York:** no mandatory waiting period — but New York's congested court dockets mean an uncontested divorce still averages **6 to 9 months** to finalize.
- **Pennsylvania:** 90 days for mutual-consent no-fault divorces; contested divorces require a **2-year separation** before filing in many cases.

**Worked example:** You file in California on November 1 and your spouse is served November 5. The earliest your divorce can legally become final is **May 5** — six months after service. If you file in Texas on November 1, the earliest legal final date is **December 31**. Same marriage, same facts, **4+ months difference** purely from the state you file in. This is why the first question in any divorce timeline conversation is always: *which state has jurisdiction?*

## Months 1–3: The Response, Temporary Orders, and Discovery's Opening Moves

Once served, your spouse has **20 to 30 days** to file a response. A response that agrees to everything — rare, but it happens — can put an uncontested divorce on a fast track to final judgment. A response that contests custody, support, or property division moves the case into the standard contested track.

If there are children or one spouse depends on the other financially, the first contested fight is usually over **temporary orders** — court orders for temporary child custody, child support, and spousal support while the divorce is pending. Temporary-order hearings are typically scheduled **30 to 60 days** after filing. These hearings matter more than most people expect: whatever the temporary custody arrangement looks like often becomes the **de facto status quo** that the final order resembles. Attorneys treat the temporary-orders hearing as a preview of the trial.

Discovery also opens in this phase. Each side must exchange a **preliminary declaration of disclosure** — a sworn inventory of all assets, debts, income, and expenses — usually due within **60 days** of the petition. In California this is mandatory and must be exchanged before a judgment can be entered. The typical form set takes **10 to 20 hours** of attorney time to prepare when there are real assets involved, which at $300/hour is **$3,000 to $6,000** of legal cost before anyone has argued about a single dollar.

Two numbers that decide this phase: **20–30 days** for the response deadline, and **$1,500 to $5,000** in attorney fees spent before the case has any momentum at all. Cases that settle in this window — usually cases with no children and simple finances — are the ones that finish in 2 to 4 months total.

## Months 3–6: Discovery, Appraisals, and the Custody Question

Months 3 through 6 are where contested divorces get expensive. This is the **discovery phase**: interrogatories (written questions, typically **30 to 35 per side**), requests for production of documents (bank statements, tax returns, business records), and depositions (sworn in-person testimony, **$1,500 to $5,000** per deposition when you add court reporter and attorney time).

The expensive sub-projects that live in this phase:

- **Business valuation.** If either spouse owns a business, the court needs a neutral valuation. A certified valuation costs **$5,000 to $20,000** and takes **2 to 4 months** — and each side often hires its own expert, so the real cost doubles.
- **Real estate appraisals.** Straightforward: **$300 to $600** per property, **1 to 3 weeks**. Becomes complicated when the property is commercial or when the spouses disagree on who appraises it.
- **Custody evaluations.** When parents contest custody, courts often order an evaluation by a neutral mental-health professional. These take **3 to 6 months** and cost **$2,500 to $10,000** — and they add that entire waiting period to the timeline because courts rarely set final custody orders without the report.
- **Forensic accounting.** Suspected hidden assets or disputed income (common in high-earner divorces) trigger forensic accountants at **$300 to $600 per hour**, with typical engagements of **$10,000 to $50,000**.

**Worked example:** A mid-complexity divorce — one house, two retirement accounts, one small business, contested custody of two children. Discovery costs: business valuation $12,000, custody evaluation $6,000, two depositions $7,000, attorney time for document review and motions $18,000. Total just to reach the negotiation table: **~$43,000**, over **6 to 9 months**. The same divorce with no business and agreed custody: **~$8,000**, over **4 to 6 months**. The business and the custody fight are the entire difference.

## Months 6–12: Mediation and Negotiation — Where 95% of Divorces End

Most courts now **require mediation** before a case can go to trial, and for good reason: mediation settles the large majority of divorces that reach it. Private mediation typically costs **$200 to $500 per hour per side**, with a full day running **$2,000 to $5,000** per spouse. Court-ordered mediation (mandatory in states like Florida and North Carolina) is often free or low-cost but slower to schedule — **2 to 4 months** for a court date versus **2 to 4 weeks** for a private mediator.

A mediation session runs **4 to 8 hours**. Roughly **70 to 80%** of mediated divorces reach a full settlement in one session; most of the rest settle in a second session or in attorney-led negotiations within **30 to 60 days** after. This is the phase where the math of early settlement becomes stark:

- Settle at mediation in month 9: total legal cost around **$15,000 to $30,000** for a mid-complexity case.
- Fail mediation and proceed to trial prep: add **$25,000 to $75,000** more.

Once a settlement is reached, the attorneys draft a **marital settlement agreement** — typically **2 to 4 weeks** of back-and-forth on the fine print — and submit it to the court with a proposed judgment. The court reviews it (checking that support orders meet state guidelines and the disclosures were complete) and signs the judgment. In uncontested-friendly counties this takes **2 to 6 weeks**; in backlogged counties, **2 to 4 months**.

## Months 12–18+: Trial Prep and Trial — The Expensive Long Tail

The roughly **5% of divorces** that do not settle enter trial prep. This phase is measured in months and priced in tens of thousands:

- **Pre-trial motions and settlement conferences:** **2 to 4 months**, **$5,000 to $15,000** in attorney time.
- **Trial preparation:** exhibit binders, witness prep, expert witness fees (**$300 to $800 per hour** for financial experts, **$400 to $1,000 per hour** for custody experts), mock arguments. Typical trial prep: **$20,000 to $50,000**.
- **The trial itself:** most divorce trials run **1 to 5 days**. A 3-day trial at $350/hour with two attorneys: **$25,000 to $35,000** in attorney time alone, before expert fees.
- **The judgment:** judges typically take **30 to 90 days** after trial to issue a written decision in complex cases.

A full trial divorce — from filing to judge's decision — therefore runs **18 to 36 months** and **$50,000 to $150,000+** per side in high-conflict cases. The per-day cost of trial is the single most expensive unit in all of family law, which is why attorneys say: *the trial is the failure mode; the settlement is the product.*

## The State Math: Five Jurisdictions Compared

Waiting periods and docket speed vary enough that the same divorce on the same facts finishes in different years depending on the state:

| State | Min. waiting period | Typical uncontested | Typical contested |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | 6 months from service | 6–8 months | 12–18 months |
| Texas | 60 days from filing | 3–6 months | 9–14 months |
| New York | None (docket-driven) | 6–9 months | 12–24 months |
| Florida | 20 days (uncontested) | 1–3 months | 9–12 months |
| Nevada | None (residency: 6 weeks) | 1–3 months | 6–12 months |

Florida and Nevada illustrate the floor: with a 20-day waiting period (Florida) or no waiting period at all (Nevada), an uncontested divorce can legally finalize in **6 to 10 weeks**. California illustrates the ceiling: no matter how agreeable both spouses are, six months is the legal minimum. Note the second column for Texas — the 60-day waiting period is short, but the *typical* contested Texas divorce still takes **9 to 14 months**, because agreement takes longer than statutes allow.

The jurisdiction rule behind all of this: **one spouse must meet the state's residency requirement** before filing — commonly **6 months in the state and 3 months in the county** (California), **6 months** (Texas), or as little as **6 weeks** (Nevada, the historical reason for "quickie" Reno divorces). Filing in the wrong state gets the case dismissed and the clock restarts at zero.

## What Speeds Up or Slows Down a Divorce

**Speeds it up:**

- **Agreeing on the parenting plan early.** Custody is the single biggest timeline driver. Parents who agree on a schedule in month 2 skip the 3-to-6-month custody evaluation entirely.
- **Complete financial disclosure up front.** The #1 cause of discovery fights is one spouse stonewalling on documents. Voluntary full disclosure in month 1 can cut **2 to 4 months** off discovery.
- **Choosing mediation over motion practice.** Every contested motion (a hearing asking the judge to decide something before trial) costs **$2,000 to $5,000** and **30 to 60 days** of calendar. Mediated agreements skip them.
- **Filing in a fast county.** Docket speed varies by county, not just state. Some counties set trial dates **6 months** out; others **18 months** out. A local attorney knows which county you're in.

**Slows it down:**

- **Hidden assets or suspected hiding.** Triggers forensic accounting (**$10,000 to $50,000**) and adds **3 to 6 months**.
- **High-conflict custody.** Adds the evaluation (**3 to 6 months, $2,500 to $10,000**) plus trial prep if it fails.
- **One spouse refusing to respond.** Courts can enter a default judgment, but only after the waiting period plus proof of service — typically adding **1 to 3 months** of procedural delay.
- **Appeals.** A losing spouse can appeal the judgment, adding **12 to 24 months**. Appeals are rare (under 5% of trials) but devastating to the timeline.

**The cost math of delay:** At a blended rate of $300/hour, each additional month of an actively contested divorce costs roughly **$3,000 to $8,000** in attorney fees. A divorce that drags from month 9 to month 18 therefore adds **$27,000 to $72,000** — for a delay that usually produces a settlement within a few percentage points of what was on the table at month 9. This is the number to remember in every negotiation: *the delay is the tax.*

## Two Worked Scenarios, End to End

**Scenario A: Uncontested, no children, one house.** Filing ($350) + service ($75). Both spouses agree on the split in week 3. Attorney drafts the settlement agreement: **6 hours at $300 = $1,800**. Submitted to the court in month 2; judgment signed in month 3 (in a state with a short waiting period). **Total: ~3 months, ~$2,225.** This is the floor.

**Scenario B: Contested, two children, a house, one small business.** Months 1–3: temporary orders ($4,000). Months 3–8: discovery, business valuation ($12,000), custody evaluation ($6,000), depositions ($7,000), attorney time ($22,000). Month 10: private mediation — settles ($4,000). Settlement agreement drafted month 11; judgment month 12. **Total: ~12 months, ~$55,000 per side.** The business valuation and custody evaluation account for **$18,000 and 5 months** — the entire difference between this and a 6-month, $20,000 divorce.

**Scenario C: Trial.** Same as B, but mediation fails. Months 12–18: trial prep ($30,000), expert fees ($12,000). 4-day trial ($32,000). Judge's decision at month 20. **Total: ~20 months, ~$130,000 per side.** The trial added **8 months and $75,000** over the mediated outcome — for a result the judge decided, not the spouses.
