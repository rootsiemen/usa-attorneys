Learning how to choose a workers compensation attorney in Texas starts with a question no generic "how to choose a lawyer" guide asks, because in 49 states it does not exist: does your employer even carry workers compensation insurance? Texas is the only state in the country where private employers are not required to carry it. That single fact splits every Texas workplace injury into two completely different legal worlds, and hiring the right lawyer means first finding out which world your case lives in. Pick the wrong kind of lawyer and you can end up with a DWC specialist handling a civil negligence lawsuit, or a personal injury trial lawyer fumbling through a Division of Workers' Compensation (DWC) dispute process they have never used.

This guide gives you the fork first, then the lawyer for each branch: how to find out your employer's status in one phone call, what each track pays and costs, the fee math on both sides, and the exact questions that separate a Texas DWC regular from a lawyer who is learning your system on your claim. If you only remember one thing, remember this: in Texas, you do not hire "a workers comp lawyer." You hire a DWC lawyer or a non-subscriber trial lawyer, and those are different hires.

## 1. The Fork: Subscriber or Non-Subscriber? Find Out Before You Call a Lawyer

In Texas, an employer that carries workers compensation insurance is called a "subscriber." An employer that does not is a "non-subscriber." Roughly speaking, most large Texas employers subscribe, while a meaningful share of smaller employers, contractors, and staffing operations do not — and you cannot tell by looking at the building, the uniform, or the size of the paycheck. You find out by asking, and the answer rewrites your case:

| Question | Subscriber (DWC track) | Non-subscriber (civil court track) |
|---|---|---|
| Where is your case heard? | Texas DWC: benefit review conferences and contested case hearings | District court, before a judge and potentially a jury |
| What can you recover? | Medical benefits and income benefits set by statute (no pain and suffering) | Full negligence damages, including pain and suffering and, in some cases, punitive damages |
| Who proves what? | No need to prove the employer was at fault | You must prove the employer was negligent, but the employer cannot use the usual defenses of contributory negligence or assumption of risk |
| Who pays your medical bills? | The workers comp carrier, through the DWC system | Your health insurance, out of pocket, or a letter of protection while the lawsuit runs |
| Typical timeline | Months for a disputed claim; benefits can start quickly if undisputed | Often 1 to 3 years to trial, though settlements can come sooner |

Here is the one-call test: ask your employer's human resources department, in writing, "Does the company carry workers compensation insurance, and who is the carrier?" Then verify it independently by calling the DWC or checking the employer's posted notice — Texas employers are required to post notice of whether they carry coverage. Do not accept a verbal "we take care of our people" as an answer. And do not let a non-subscriber employer steer you into its own "injury plan": many non-subscribers run private occupational-injury benefit plans with their own doctors, deadlines, and arbitration clauses. Signing up for that plan, or signing a waiver after an injury, can affect your right to sue. Talk to a lawyer before you sign anything from a non-subscriber employer after an injury.

## 2. How to Choose a Workers Compensation Attorney in Texas for a Subscriber (DWC) Case

If your employer subscribes, your case lives inside the DWC system, and the lawyer you want is a DWC regular — someone who appears at benefit review conferences (BRCs) and contested case hearings (CCHs) every week, not once a year. The DWC system has its own vocabulary, its own doctors, and its own deadlines, and fluency in it is the whole job.

Start with three filters. First, ask what percentage of the lawyer's active cases are Texas DWC claims. You want a number above 50%, ideally above 80%. A lawyer who handles DWC cases as a sideline between car accidents will not know which hearing officers are strict on impairment disputes or which designated doctors the carriers routinely challenge. Second, ask how many BRCs and CCHs the lawyer handled in the last twelve months. "I have twenty years of experience" is not an answer; "I handled 40 contested hearings last year" is. Third, ask who actually handles your file. DWC cases involve constant paper: medical records, work-status reports, designated doctor scheduling, and responses to carrier denials. If a senior partner sells you the case and a case manager you never met runs it, you will feel that gap the first time a deadline arrives unannounced.

Then test DWC fluency with specifics. A real Texas DWC lawyer should be able to explain, in plain language, the difference between temporary income benefits (TIBs, generally 70% of your average weekly wage while you cannot work), impairment income benefits (IIBs, paid after maximum medical improvement based on your impairment rating), and supplemental income benefits (SIBs, for longer-term wage loss). They should know that your impairment rating — a percentage assigned by a doctor — directly drives how many weeks of IIBs you receive, which is why challenging a low rating is often the highest-value move in a DWC case. If a lawyer waves past the impairment rating as a detail, keep looking.

## 3. The Non-Subscriber Track: A Different Lawyer Entirely

If your employer does not subscribe, you do not have a DWC case at all. You have a negligence lawsuit against your employer in civil court, and the lawyer you need is a personal injury trial lawyer with non-subscriber experience specifically. This is the branch where generic advice does the most damage: a DWC specialist has no particular advantage in district court, and a general car-accident lawyer who has never handled a non-subscriber case may not know the employer's defenses are stripped away, or how to build the safety-violation evidence that wins these cases.

In a non-subscriber case, the questions change. Ask how many non-subscriber cases the lawyer has taken to verdict or settlement in the last three years — not how many personal injury cases in general, but non-subscriber cases specifically. Ask how they investigate: do they send a preservation letter for safety records, training logs, and surveillance video within days of being hired, before the employer "loses" them? Do they work with safety experts who can testify about OSHA standards and industry practice? Ask how medical bills are handled while the case runs, because there is no workers comp carrier paying them: the answer should be a concrete plan involving health insurance, provider agreements, or letters of protection, not a shrug.

Also ask about arbitration. Many non-subscriber employers require workers to sign arbitration agreements as a condition of employment, sometimes buried in onboarding paperwork. An experienced non-subscriber lawyer reads that agreement first, before promising you a jury trial, and tells you honestly whether your case will be heard by an arbitrator instead of a jury. A lawyer who never asks to see your onboarding paperwork is not ready for a non-subscriber case.

## 4. Fee Math: The 25% DWC Cap vs. Civil Contingency — Worked Examples

Fees are where the two tracks diverge hardest, and where comparing lawyers on their percentage alone misleads you. On the DWC track, Texas law caps attorney fees: a DWC attorney's fee must be approved by the DWC and cannot exceed 25% of the benefits recovered. On the civil track, there is no statutory cap, and a typical contingency fee runs 33% to 40% of the recovery, rising if the case goes to trial. But the recoveries themselves are different sizes, so the net to you is what matters. Run both examples before you judge a fee as high or low.

**Worked example A — subscriber (DWC) case.** Suppose a disputed DWC claim settles for $80,000 in income benefits and a lump-sum payment. At the 25% DWC cap, the approved attorney fee is $20,000, leaving $60,000 before case costs. If the lawyer also secured benefits you were already receiving without a fight, ask whether the fee applies to those — a good DWC lawyer does not take a cut of benefits the carrier was already paying voluntarily, and the DWC approval process exists partly to police exactly that.

**Worked example B — non-subscriber (civil) case.** Suppose a jury awards $200,000 for a back injury caused by an employer's failure to maintain equipment: medical bills, lost wages, and pain and suffering. At a 33% pre-trial contingency fee, the fee is $66,000, leaving $134,000 before costs and medical liens — still more than double the DWC example's net, because the damages available in civil court are broader. But if the same case settles for only $60,000 because the negligence evidence is thin, the 33% fee leaves $40,200 before costs, and the years-long timeline means that money arrives far later than DWC benefits would have.

The lesson is not that one track is always better. The lesson is that your employer's status decides your track, and your lawyer's fee should be judged against what that track can actually deliver, after costs, after liens, and after the wait. Ask every lawyer you consult to put their fee in writing as a net-to-you estimate on two scenarios: a settlement and a hearing or trial. The lawyer who refuses to do that math is the lawyer to avoid.

## 5. The Texas DWC Process in Plain English: Designated Doctors, BRCs, and Impairment Ratings

If you are on the DWC track, your case will turn on a handful of Texas-specific steps that no out-of-state guide explains well. Knowing them lets you judge whether your lawyer is steering or drifting.

First, the treating doctor. In the DWC system, your treating doctor certifies whether you can work and, eventually, assigns your impairment rating when you reach maximum medical improvement (MMI) — the point where your condition is not expected to improve materially with further treatment. That rating is not a medical opinion you can ignore; it is a number that converts directly into weeks of income benefits. A rating of 10% and a rating of 20% are not two opinions about the same injury — they are two different paychecks.

Second, the designated doctor. If the carrier disputes your treating doctor's certification or rating, the DWC appoints a designated doctor — an independent physician whose opinion carries presumptive weight at a hearing. This is the single most consequential appointment in many DWC cases, and your lawyer's job is to prepare you for it: what to bring, what to say about your limitations in concrete terms (not "it hurts" but "I cannot lift more than 10 pounds or stand longer than 20 minutes"), and what records the doctor must see. A lawyer who treats the designated doctor exam as a routine appointment is leaving money on the table.

Third, the dispute ladder. Most DWC disputes start at a benefit review conference, an informal mediation-style meeting. If that fails, the case moves to a contested case hearing before a DWC hearing officer, whose decision can be appealed to the DWC Appeals Panel and then, in limited circumstances, to court. Each step has its own deadlines, measured in weeks rather than months, and missing one can end a dispute you were winning. Ask your lawyer, at the first consultation, to write down the next three deadlines in your case and who is responsible for each. That one request separates organized DWC practices from chaotic ones.

## 6. Deadlines and Paperwork: The Texas Checklist That Protects Your Claim

Texas deadlines punish delay more than most states, because the DWC system runs on short clocks and the civil track runs on a two-year statute of limitations for personal injury. Keep this checklist from the day of the injury:

- **Report the injury to your employer within 30 days.** This is a hard Texas rule: late notice can cost you the claim, and "I told my supervisor verbally" is weaker than a written report with a date. Send it by email or text so you have proof, and keep a copy.
- **File your DWC claim within one year of the injury.** Even if your employer reported it, file your own claim (DWC Form-041) and keep the stamped copy. Do not assume the employer's paperwork covers you.
- **Keep every medical record, work-status report, and pay stub.** Your average weekly wage calculation — the basis for your income benefits — depends on wage records, and carriers routinely miscalculate it in their favor. Bring 13 weeks of pay records to your first lawyer meeting.
- **Never miss a designated doctor appointment or a hearing.** A missed exam can be used to suspend benefits; a missed hearing can end a dispute. Put every DWC date in your phone the day you receive it.
- **On the civil track, preserve evidence immediately.** Photographs of the hazard, names of witnesses, and the employer's safety records degrade fast. A non-subscriber lawyer should send a preservation letter within days, not months.

If you are already past one of these deadlines, do not assume your case is dead — talk to a lawyer before concluding anything. Some deadlines have exceptions, and the exception analysis is exactly what a consultation is for. But do not wait another week to find out.

## 7. Red Flags and the 10-Question Consultation Script

Take this script to every consultation, DWC or civil, and write down the answers. Lawyers who welcome specific questions are almost always better than lawyers who answer with slogans.

1. "Is my employer a subscriber or a non-subscriber, and how will you verify it?"
2. "What percentage of your cases are Texas DWC claims — or non-subscriber cases, if that is my track?"
3. "How many contested hearings or trials did you handle in the last twelve months?"
4. "Who will handle my case day to day, and how many active files does that person carry?"
5. "What is my impairment rating risk, and how do you prepare clients for a designated doctor exam?" (DWC track)
6. "Have you handled a case against an employer with an arbitration clause, and what happened?" (Non-subscriber track)
7. "What fee applies, who approves it, and what is my estimated net on a settlement versus a hearing or trial?"
8. "What are the next three deadlines in my case, and who owns each one?"
9. "How will my medical bills be paid while the case is pending?"
10. "What is the weakest part of my case, in your honest assessment?"

Red flags that should end the meeting: a lawyer who guarantees an outcome ("I will get you $100,000"), a lawyer who asks you to sign a fee agreement before explaining which track you are on, a lawyer who has never heard of the DWC Appeals Panel or, on the civil side, never asks about your onboarding paperwork, and any firm that pressures you to sign in the hospital or at home within days of the injury. Texas has real deadlines, but no legitimate deadline requires you to hire the first lawyer who calls.

## 8. Your Decision Worksheet: Picking Between Two Good Lawyers

If two lawyers both pass the script, decide on fit, not fame. Score each on five factors, and weight the first two heaviest: track fluency (do they live in your system every week?), honest fee math (did they show you the net, not just the percentage?), communication (did they return your first call within one business day, and explain things without jargon?), caseload (will your file be one of 40 or one of 300?), and local knowledge (do they know the DWC field office, the hearing officers, or the district court where your case will be heard?). A lawyer who scores high on all five but charges the standard fee beats a famous lawyer who scores low on communication and caseload every time, because workers comp cases are won in the months of follow-through, not in the first meeting.

Start today with the one-call test: confirm your employer's subscriber status in writing. Then use this guide's script in two consultations — one DWC regular if you are a subscriber, one non-subscriber trial lawyer if you are not — and compare their answers side by side. The right Texas lawyer is not the one with the biggest advertisement. It is the one who knows which system you are in, what it pays, what it costs, and what happens next.
