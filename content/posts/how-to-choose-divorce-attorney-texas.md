Learning how to choose a divorce attorney in Texas starts with an uncomfortable fact: most of the advice online is written for "divorce in general," and Texas is not general. Texas runs on community property, a mandatory 60-day waiting period, county-level family courts with their own local habits, and a board-certification system that fewer than 1% of Texas family lawyers hold. A lawyer who is excellent in another state, or even excellent in the wrong Texas county, can still be the wrong hire for your case. This guide gives you the Texas-specific filters that actually change outcomes: residency and county, property rules, certification, custody language, and retainer math you can run before you sign anything.

## 1. First, Confirm You Can Even File in Texas — and Where

Before you interview a single lawyer, check the two residency numbers that control everything else. Under the Texas Family Code, at least one spouse must have lived in Texas for six months, and in the county where you file for 90 days. File in the wrong county and your case can be delayed, transferred, or dismissed — and you will have paid a retainer to learn that.

This matters for choosing a lawyer because Texas family courts are county courts in practice. A Dallas County judge's scheduling habits, mediation requirements, and standing orders are not Harris County's, and neither matches a smaller county where one judge hears everything. Ask every candidate: "How many cases have you handled in the county where I will file in the last year?" A lawyer who files weekly in your county knows which judges push mediation early, which ones want detailed parenting plans before temporary orders, and how long a contested hearing realistically takes to get on the docket. A lawyer who has to look your county up is learning on your retainer.

- **Residency check:** 6 months in Texas, 90 days in the filing county, for at least one spouse.
- **Waiting period:** Texas requires a 60-day wait from filing to final decree — no lawyer can waive it, and any lawyer who promises a faster final divorce is selling something Texas law does not allow.
- **County question to ask:** "Who are the family judges in my county, and how does each handle temporary orders?" A specific answer beats a confident general one.

## 2. The Texas Property Filter: Community Property Changes the Whole Case

Texas is a community property state, and that single fact shapes more divorce outcomes than almost anything your lawyer says in consultation. Property acquired during the marriage is presumed community property, divided in a manner the court finds "just and right" — which does not automatically mean 50/50. Separate property (owned before marriage, or received by gift or inheritance) stays separate, but only if you can prove it with records. Commingled accounts, a house refinanced during the marriage, or a business that grew while married can all blur that line.

This is where lawyer selection becomes concrete. Ask: "Walk me through how you would trace separate property in a case like mine." A strong answer names documents — original purchase records, inheritance paperwork, bank statements showing the money trail — and explains when a forensic accountant or business valuation is worth paying for. A weak answer waves at "we'll sort it out in discovery." Also ask how the lawyer handles the house, because it is usually the largest asset and the most emotional one. Our companion guide on [who keeps the house in divorce — buyout vs. sell math](/blog/who-keeps-the-house-in-divorce-buyout-vs-sell-math) shows why the buyout number, not the sentiment, should drive that decision.

Worked example: a couple bought a house for $280,000 before marriage, refinanced during the marriage, and now it appraises at $460,000. The community estate may have a reimbursement claim for mortgage principal paid down with community funds, while the original separate-property character of the down payment may still be traceable. Get that analysis wrong and you can misprice the single biggest asset in the case by tens of thousands of dollars. The lawyer who spots this in consultation is worth more than the lawyer with the nicer office.

## 3. Board Certification: The Filter Fewer Than 1% Pass

Texas offers board certification in Family Law through the Texas Board of Legal Specialization. It requires substantial family-law experience, peer review, references, and a written exam. Fewer than 1% of Texas family lawyers hold it. That does not mean a non-certified lawyer is bad — many excellent divorce lawyers never sit for the exam — but it does mean certification is the fastest credible shortcut when you are comparing strangers.

Use it as a tier, not a religion:

- **Complex case** (business, contested custody, high-asset property, out-of-state issues): strongly prefer board-certified family law specialists, and ask how many contested trials they have handled in the last two years.
- **Straightforward uncontested case** (agreed property split, no minor children, no disputes): certification matters less than clear flat-fee pricing and speed — do not pay a specialist premium for paperwork.
- **Red flag:** a lawyer who claims a "specialization" Texas does not recognize, or who lists family law tenth among ten practice areas. Check certification and licensing through the State Bar of Texas directory, not the firm's own website.

## 4. Custody in Texas Means Conservatorship — Test the Lawyer's Language

Texas does not use the word "custody" the way most states do. It uses conservatorship (who makes decisions) and possession and access (the schedule). The guiding standard is the best interest of the child under Texas Family Code §153.002. A lawyer who talks fluently about joint managing conservatorship, standard possession orders, geographic restrictions, and how your county's judges actually apply the standard possession schedule is speaking Texas. A lawyer who only says "we'll fight for full custody" is translating from another state's playbook.

![A couple signing divorce paperwork with a pen, reviewing the terms of their agreement](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1450101499163-c8848c66ca85?w=1200&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)

Ask these three questions, and listen for specifics:

1. "What does a standard possession order look like in my county, and when would you argue for something different?"
2. "How do judges here treat a geographic restriction when one parent wants to move?"
3. "What evidence actually moves a judge on conservatorship — and what evidence just wastes money?"

If the answers are generic ("we'll present your side strongly"), you have learned something important: this lawyer has a pitch, not a plan. Also bring your documents to the first meeting — our [divorce attorney consultation document checklist](/blog/divorce-attorney-consultation-document-checklist) lists exactly what to carry, from tax returns and pay stubs to existing court orders involving children.

## 5. Run the Retainer Math Before You Sign

Texas divorce fees vary wildly, and the retainer is not the price — it is the deposit. Family lawyers in Texas commonly charge $250 to $450 per hour, with contested-case retainers often starting at $5,000 to $15,000. An uncontested divorce with no disputes can cost a fraction of that, sometimes on a flat fee. The difference between a $7,500 retainer and a $25,000 total bill is how the case is fought, and you should model that before signing.

Here is the comparison most people never run. Lawyer A quotes a $5,000 retainer at $300 per hour. Lawyer B quotes $10,000 at $350 per hour. If your case settles after 30 hours of work, Lawyer A costs $9,000 and Lawyer B costs $10,500 — close. If it goes to trial and takes 100 hours, Lawyer A costs $30,000 and Lawyer B costs $35,000, but the real question is which lawyer settles sooner and cheaper. Ask each candidate: "For a case like mine, what is your realistic total range — settled, mediated, and tried?" Then ask what is excluded: expert witnesses, appraisals, deposition transcripts, and mediation fees are often billed on top. Compare totals, not retainers. For the full cost picture, see [how much a divorce attorney costs in 2026](/blog/how-much-does-divorce-attorney-cost-2026) and the [divorce mediation vs. litigation cost math](/blog/divorce-mediation-vs-litigation-cost-math) — mediation usually resolves Texas cases far cheaper than trial, and a lawyer who defaults to litigation should explain why your case is the exception.

Also read the fee agreement the way you would read a contract, because it is one. Who bills for paralegal time, and at what rate? How often are invoices sent? What happens when the retainer runs out — does work stop, or does billing continue? ## 6. The Consultation Scorecard: Score Every Lawyer the Same Way

Do not hire on gut feeling after one meeting. Interview two or three lawyers and score each on the same five points, 0 to 2 each, for a 10-point total:

- **County experience (0–2):** Recent cases in your filing county, names judges and local procedure without hesitation.
- **Property clarity (0–2):** Names your community vs. separate property issues unprompted, with a tracing plan.
- **Custody fluency (0–2):** Uses conservatorship and possession language correctly and explains the local standard schedule.
- **Fee transparency (0–2):** Gives settled, mediated, and tried total ranges in writing, and names exclusions.
- **Communication system (0–2):** Tells you who your point of contact is, how often you get updates, and how fast emails are returned — and the answer is a system, not "call me anytime."

A lawyer scoring 8 or above is a strong hire. A lawyer scoring 5 or below with a low price is not a bargain; in divorce, the cheapest lawyer who mishandles property characterization or misses a deadline is the most expensive one. Also ask the question most people skip: "What are the three biggest weaknesses in my case?" A lawyer who answers honestly is telling you what the other side will argue. A lawyer who says your case has no weaknesses is either not listening or not being straight with you. ## 7. Red Flags That Should End the Interview

![Two people shaking hands after reaching a divorce settlement agreement](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1521791136064-7986c2920216?w=1200&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)

Some answers should stop the conversation, no matter how impressive the office is:

- **Guaranteed outcomes.** No Texas lawyer can guarantee a property split, a custody result, or a timeline. Guarantees are a marketing tactic, not a legal assessment.
- **A 60-day promise.** The waiting period is mandatory. A lawyer promising a final decree sooner is either confused or dishonest.
- **No written fee agreement.** Texas fee disputes are miserable without one. Walk away from any lawyer who wants to start on a handshake.
- **Pressure to file today.** Urgency is sometimes real — safety issues, asset dissipation — but a lawyer who pressures you to sign before you have compared options is selling, not advising.
- **No plan for mediation.** Most Texas courts order mediation before trial. A lawyer with no mediation strategy is planning for the most expensive path by default.
- **You never meet the lawyer.** If consultations are handled entirely by sales staff and your case will be passed to an associate you never meet, ask to meet that associate before signing.

## 8. Your Texas Hiring Timeline: What to Do This Week

You do not need to rush, but you do need a sequence. This week: confirm your filing county and residency, pull the documents from the consultation checklist, and book two or three consultations. In each one, run the scorecard, ask for the settled/mediated/tried fee ranges in writing, and ask for the property-tracing and custody answers in plain language. Between meetings, compare totals — not retainers — and check each lawyer's standing and certification with the State Bar of Texas.

Then decide on fit, not fame. The right divorce attorney in Texas is the one who knows your county's courts, can explain your property and custody issues without jargon, prices the case honestly across all three outcomes, and communicates on a schedule you can rely on. That lawyer may not have the biggest billboard on the highway. But when the 60-day clock is running, the property spreadsheet is on the table, and a parenting plan has to hold up for years, county knowledge and honest math beat advertising every time.
