Of every type of lawyer in America, the workers' compensation attorney is the oddest one to price — because you don't really price them at all. Your state legislature already did. A [workers' compensation attorney](/workers-compensation-attorney) in San Diego and one in San Antonio do roughly the same job, but the California lawyer can take 9 to 15 percent of your award while the Texas lawyer can take 25 percent, and neither number was ever up for negotiation. A workers' compensation judge reviews the fee before it gets paid. If you're trying to figure out what this lawyer costs you, the answer is a math problem with your state's statute as the first input — and a second, sneakier question hiding behind it: will hiring one leave you with more money than going it alone? That's the part most fee guides skip. This one won't.

## The one rule that makes workers' comp fees different from every other lawyer's

Here's the setup that governs nearly every injured worker's case: there is no hourly bill, no retainer check, no invoice that lands on your kitchen table while your claim is pending. Workers' comp attorneys work on contingency — they get paid only as a percentage of the benefits they recover for you. Lose, and the fee is zero.

That alone isn't special; personal injury lawyers work on contingency too. What makes workers' comp different is that the percentage is **set or capped by state law** and **approved by a workers' compensation judge or board** before anyone gets paid. In personal injury, an attorney can quote 33% or 40% and you can haggle or walk. In workers' comp, haggling is mostly theater — the cap is the cap, and a judge rejects fee petitions that exceed it. One result: workers keep far more of their recovery than personal injury clients do. Injured workers surveyed by Nolo reported their lawyers took about **15% of settlements and awards on average** — less than half what a typical car-accident contingency takes.

That 15% average is your baseline expectation. Everything below is what moves your number off that baseline.

## The state fee map: where the same injury costs twice as much to litigate

Because each state writes its own workers' comp statute, the fee ceiling is a patchwork. The same $80,000 award can cost you $7,200 in attorney fees in one state and $20,000 in another. Here's the 2026 map that matters:

- **California** — the Workers' Compensation Appeals Board typically approves **9 to 15 percent**, depending on case complexity. This is among the lowest fee structures in the country; California's official injured-worker guidebook puts the fee at roughly 9–15% of a permanent-disability settlement or award.
- **Texas** — capped at **25 percent** under Division of Workers' Compensation rules.
- **Georgia** — capped at **25 percent**.
- **Illinois** — capped at **20 percent**.
- **Alabama** — fixed at **15 percent**, one of the lowest in the country.
- **Pennsylvania** — presumptive **20 percent** cap, subject to judge approval.
- **Florida** — a statutory sliding scale under section 440.34: **20% of the first $5,000, 15% of the next $5,000, and 10% of the remainder**. A judge of compensation claims must approve the fee.
- **New York** — no fixed percentage; the Workers' Compensation Board approves every fee case by case and deducts it from the award.
- **Minnesota** — spelled out in statute (Minnesota Statute §176.081): **20% of the first $130,000** of compensation awarded, which works out to a hard ceiling of **$26,000**.
- **Massachusetts** — the standout exception, covered below: when the worker prevails, the **insurer** pays the attorney fee, not the worker.

## The worked example: one $80,000 award, five different bills

Take the same outcome — an $80,000 settlement on a back-injury claim — and run the fee math state by state. Assume the attorney takes the maximum the state allows and a judge approves it:

| State | Fee rule | Attorney fee | Worker keeps |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | 15% | $12,000 | $68,000 |
| Texas | 25% | $20,000 | $60,000 |
| Illinois | 20% | $16,000 | $64,000 |
| Alabama | 15% | $12,000 | $68,000 |
| Pennsylvania | 20% | $16,000 | $64,000 |

Now do the Florida sliding-scale math, which rewards careful arithmetic. On a $50,000 award: 20% of the first $5,000 ($1,000) + 15% of the next $5,000 ($750) + 10% of the remaining $40,000 ($4,000) = **$5,750 total**. That's an effective rate of 11.5% — well below what most injured workers assume "a lawyer costs." On a larger $80,000 Florida award: $1,000 + $750 + $7,000 = **$8,750** (10.9% effective). Florida's brackets are one of the most worker-friendly fee structures in the country, and almost nobody knows it until an attorney explains it at the free consultation.

The comparison makes the point the caps are designed to make: this is not a market where lawyers extract 33%. The legislature decided injured workers needed protection from their own counsel, and the numbers show it.

## The fee is not the whole cost: case expenses and who eats them

The percentage fee covers the lawyer's time. It does not cover **case expenses** — and this is where injured workers get their one genuinely unpleasant surprise. The fee agreement must spell out, in writing, how expenses are handled. Typical expenses:

- Medical records and imaging copies: a few hundred dollars
- Deposition transcripts: several hundred to over a thousand per deposition
- Independent medical examination (IME) or expert reports: often $1,000 to $5,000+

Two questions decide whether expenses sting you, and both belong in the fee agreement before you sign:

1. **Do expenses come out of your share or does the firm front them and get reimbursed from the recovery?** Either is normal; what matters is that it's stated.
2. **Do you owe the expenses if the case loses?** This varies. Some firms absorb them; some agreements say you reimburse costs win or lose. A legitimate agreement is one page, uses plain language, states the exact percentage, lists what costs you'll reimburse — and is signed by both sides. Never sign a fee agreement with a blank percentage or language like "reasonable fee to be determined later."

Add expenses to the Florida example above: a $5,750 fee plus, say, $2,200 in records and one IME is **$7,950** off the $50,000 award. Still a bargain if the attorney created the award — which brings us to the real question.

## The net-payout test: does hiring one pay for itself?

Forget "how much does it cost." The question that matters is **how much of the recovery exists because the attorney was there**. There are exactly two scenarios, and a reputable attorney will tell you which one you're in at the free consultation:

**Scenario A — the undisputed claim.** The employer reported the injury, the insurer accepted the claim, TTD checks are arriving, medical is authorized, and nobody is fighting about the impairment rating. Here, a lawyer adds cost without adding value. A 15% fee on a $30,000 accepted-claim payout is $4,500 you handed over for administrative work you could have done yourself. Honest attorneys decline these cases or tell you to call back if something goes sideways.

**Scenario B — the disputed claim.** The insurer denied the claim, lowballed the settlement, disputed the impairment rating, or offered a compromise-and-release that smells wrong. Now run the net-payout test with real numbers. Suppose the insurer's standing offer is $25,000 and your attorney negotiates it to $60,000 at a 15% fee ($9,000) plus $2,000 in expenses:

- Without the attorney: **$25,000**
- With the attorney: $60,000 − $9,000 − $2,000 = **$49,000**

The attorney "cost" $11,000 and made you $24,000. That is the entire economics of workers' comp representation in one calculation — and it's why the standard guidance is: accepted, undisputed claim, probably fine alone; denied claim, disputed rating, or settlement offer on the table, get the free consultation. (For the mechanics of what the settlement itself should be, see our guide on [how workers' comp payouts are actually calculated](/blog/workers-compensation-settlement-payout-math).)

## The Massachusetts exception: when the insurer pays your lawyer

One state flips the whole model. Under Massachusetts General Laws chapter 152, section 13A, when an employee prevails at a conference or hearing, the **insurer — not the worker — pays a fixed attorney fee**. The fee comes out of the insurer's pocket as a statutory cost of losing. Other states have milder versions of the same idea: fees assessed against insurers that unreasonably delay or deny benefits, paid on top of the award rather than out of it.

If you're in Massachusetts and your case is heading to a hearing you expect to win, your fee math is close to zero — which also means you should be skeptical of any attorney quoting a contingency percentage against your benefits without explaining the 13A route first.

## What to check in the fee agreement before you sign

Every state requires the fee agreement to be reasonable and most require judge approval, so the document is short by design. Still, check these five lines before signing — they separate the one-page plain-language agreements from the problematic ones:

1. **The exact percentage**, not a range and not "to be determined."
2. **Whether the fee applies to all benefits or only to disputed/additional amounts.** Some agreements take the percentage of everything, including TTD checks that were already flowing before the attorney appeared. Ask.
3. **The expenses clause**: what's reimbursable, whether there's a cap, and whether you owe expenses if the case loses.
4. **No retroactive fee increases.** Some agreements let the attorney raise the percentage if litigation gets more complex. Don't sign that; the state cap is the ceiling anyway.
5. **Who handles medical liens.** Unpaid medical bills attached to the claim need to be resolved at settlement — the agreement should say whether that's the attorney's job or yours.

And one thing to check that isn't in the agreement: confirm the fee will be **submitted for judge or board approval**, which is required in most states. If an attorney tells you approval isn't needed and your state requires it, that's a red flag about the attorney, not the fee.

## The bottom line

A workers' comp attorney costs whatever your state says — roughly 9 to 25 percent of what they recover, averaging around 15% nationally, approved by a judge, with nothing due if you lose. The fee is not the risk; the risk is paying it on a claim that needed no lawyer, or going without one on a claim the insurer was undervaluing. Run the net-payout test, read the fee agreement's expense clause twice, and remember that the consultation is free at nearly every firm — which means the most expensive mistake available to you is never asking.
