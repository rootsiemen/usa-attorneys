Most people shopping for a [bankruptcy attorney](/bankruptcy-attorney) do it backwards. They collect three quotes, pick the cheapest one, and only discover the mistake months later — when a case gets dismissed, a car they thought was protected gets flagged by the trustee, or a "low fee" turns out not to include the work the case actually needed.

Bankruptcy is not a commodity purchase. Two attorneys can file the same chapter for the same person and produce very different outcomes, because the outcome depends on judgment calls made before anything is filed: which chapter fits, which exemptions protect what you own, and how your income, debts, and recent transactions are presented. Get those calls right and a case moves quietly from filing to discharge. Get them wrong and the case stalls, converts, or gets thrown out — and you have paid for the privilege.

The test below is how to tell the difference before you sign anything.

## 1. Start with chapter fit, not the fee quote

The first question a good bankruptcy attorney asks is not "how much do you owe?" It is "what does your money look like?" — income, household size, assets, and what kind of debt is crushing you. That is because the chapter decision drives everything else: the fee, the timeline, and what you keep.

Chapter 7, the liquidation chapter, typically wraps up in 4 to 6 months. Chapter 13, the repayment-plan chapter, runs 3 to 5 years. Filing the wrong one is the most expensive mistake in bankruptcy, and it is also one of the most common: a filer pushed into Chapter 13 when they qualified for Chapter 7 pays thousands more and spends years in a plan they never needed, while a filer pushed into Chapter 7 with non-exempt equity in a home or business can lose property a Chapter 13 plan would have protected.

At your consultation, the attorney should walk through the means test with you — the income comparison against your state's median for your household size — and explain, in plain numbers, why one chapter fits and the other does not. If the attorney recommends a chapter without asking about your income, your mortgage balance, your car equity, and any property you transferred in the last few years, that is not a consultation. That is a sales call.

Our full comparison is in [Chapter 7 vs Chapter 13: the math that decides](/blog/chapter-7-vs-chapter-13-the-math-that-decides). Read it before your first consultation, so you can tell whether the attorney's recommendation matches the math or just matches their fee schedule.

## 2. Do the fee math before you sign anything

Bankruptcy fees are more transparent than most legal fees, but the headline number still hides the real total. Typical attorney fees for a straightforward Chapter 7 case run roughly $1,000 to $2,000 in most markets, and Chapter 13 fees commonly run $3,000 to $6,000, often paid through the repayment plan itself. Court filing fees add $338 for Chapter 7 and $313 for Chapter 13. Credit counseling and debtor education courses — both required — add roughly $25 to $100 combined.

Here is the math that trips people up. Take two Chapter 7 quotes: $1,200 and $1,800. The cheaper one looks like a $600 saving. But if the $1,200 quote excludes adversary proceedings, motions to avoid liens, and reaffirmation agreements — work a case with a car loan or a judgment lien will need — and those add $900 later, the "cheap" case costs $2,100 while the $1,800 all-in case costs $1,800. Always ask what is included and what is billed extra, and get the answer in writing.

The other trap is the "no money down" Chapter 13 pitch. In Chapter 13, attorney fees can often be paid through the plan, so filing with little upfront cash is genuinely possible. But it is also a sales tool: an attorney who only gets paid if you file Chapter 13 has a financial reason to recommend Chapter 13 even when Chapter 7 would have been faster and cheaper for you. If an attorney recommends Chapter 13, ask them to show you, side by side, what Chapter 7 would have cost and what you would have lost in it. If they cannot or will not, get a second opinion. (Our detailed breakdown is in [how much a bankruptcy attorney costs in 2026](/blog/how-much-does-bankruptcy-attorney-cost-2026).)

One more fee check: payment timing. Most Chapter 7 attorneys require full payment before filing, because debts — including unpaid attorney fees — can be discharged in the case itself. If an attorney offers a long post-filing payment plan for Chapter 7, ask how that works and what stops if a payment is missed. And read every clause before signing; our guide to [retainer agreement clauses to read before signing](/blog/retainer-agreement-clauses-to-read-before-signing) applies word for word here.

## 3. Ask the 15 questions that reveal the real attorney

You do not need legal training to run a good consultation. You need a list, and you need to listen for specifics instead of slogans. Ask these:

1. How many bankruptcy cases do you file per year, and what share are Chapter 7 versus Chapter 13?
2. Given my income and household size, do I pass the means test — and what is my margin?
3. Which chapter do you recommend for me, and what would the other chapter cost me in comparison?
4. What property of mine is at risk under my state's exemptions, and what exactly protects it?
5. Have you handled cases before the trustee assigned to my district, and what do they scrutinize most?
6. What is your total fee, and what work is billed separately?
7. Does the fee include lien avoidance motions, reaffirmation agreements, and creditor calls after filing?
8. How will you handle debts I forgot to list, or a creditor who keeps calling after the automatic stay?
9. Have you handled cases with my specific complication — a recent property transfer, a business debt, a tax debt, a co-signed loan?
10. What documents do you need from me, and what happens if I am slow providing them?
11. Who will actually work on my case — you, or a paralegal I never meet?
12. How quickly do you return calls and emails during a case?
13. What is your case dismissal rate for Chapter 13 plans, and why do your plans fail when they do?
14. What happens if my income changes mid-case — a job loss, a raise, an inheritance?
15. If my case hits a problem, will you appear at the hearing yourself, and is that included in the fee?

Notice what these questions have in common: every one of them forces a concrete answer. "We handle all kinds of bankruptcy" is not an answer to question 1. "We will protect your assets" is not an answer to question 4. An attorney who answers in numbers, names, and procedures is showing you how your case will actually be run. An attorney who answers in reassurance is selling you comfort, and comfort is not a legal strategy.

## 4. Watch for the four red flags

Bankruptcy attracts high-volume operators, and a few warning signs should end the interview on the spot.

First, the bankruptcy mill: a firm filing hundreds of cases a month, where you never meet the attorney whose name is on the petition and every question is answered by a rotating intake staffer. Your case is a file number there, and file numbers get mistakes — missed creditors, wrong exemptions, unfiled amendments.

Second, the non-attorney petition preparer dressed up as a law firm. Preparers can type forms, but they cannot give legal advice, appear in court, or tell you which chapter fits. If the person advising you cannot stand up in bankruptcy court for you, you are not hiring a lawyer.

Third, the guarantee. No honest attorney guarantees a discharge, a kept house, or a specific outcome. Bankruptcy outcomes depend on trustees, judges, and facts that surface during the case. A guarantee is a marketing line, not a legal opinion.

Fourth, pressure to file today. A legitimate attorney may note a deadline — a foreclosure sale date, a garnishment start date — but will still give you time to read the agreement and compare options. "Sign now or the offer expires" is a sales tactic, and it has no place in a decision this consequential.

## 5. Check their local court and trustee record

Bankruptcy is federal law, but it is practiced locally. Each district has its own trustees, its own local rules, and its own unwritten expectations — how picky the trustee is about bank statements, how the court treats a recent tax refund, what documentation a particular judge wants before confirming a Chapter 13 plan.

Ask: "How many cases have you filed in this district in the last year, and which trustees do you appear before most?" A strong answer names trustees and their habits. A weak answer is "we file all over the state." Also ask about the meeting of creditors — the 341 meeting — which usually happens 21 to 40 days after filing. Your attorney should prepare you for it specifically: what the trustee will ask, which documents to bring, and what not to volunteer. If the attorney brushes off the 341 meeting as "just a formality," that is a sign they will not prepare you for the one hearing every filer actually attends.

One practical check anyone can do: your state's bar association directory confirms the attorney is licensed and in good standing, and court records in most districts let you see how many cases an attorney has filed recently. Five minutes of checking beats five months of regret.

## 6. Know what happens after you hire — the timeline test

A good attorney explains the timeline before you pay, not after. The broad shape: credit counseling before filing, then the petition, then the automatic stay that halts most collection activity, then the 341 meeting roughly a month later, then — for Chapter 7 — discharge typically 60 to 90 days after the 341 meeting, for a total of about 4 to 6 months. Chapter 13 adds plan confirmation and then 3 to 5 years of payments, with discharge at the end.

Ask the attorney to walk your case through that timeline with your facts: when the stay would start, what collection calls you should expect to stop, and what could delay discharge in a case like yours. Also ask the document question early. Bankruptcy runs on paperwork — pay stubs, tax returns, bank statements, debt statements — and the most common cause of delay is a filer who cannot produce documents on time. An attorney who hands you a clear checklist at the first meeting is an attorney who has run this process before. An attorney who says "we'll figure out documents later" is an attorney whose cases stall.

Also ask about communication during the long middle of a case. In Chapter 13 especially, you will live with this case for years. You need to know who to call when a creditor violates the stay, when your income changes, or when a plan payment is at risk — and how fast they will call back.

## 7. The honest exception: when you might not need an attorney at all

A good article about choosing a bankruptcy attorney should say this plainly: not everyone needs one. A simple Chapter 7 case — low income, no property above exemption limits, no business debts, no recent transfers, debts that are mostly credit cards and medical bills — is the scenario where some people file successfully on their own, and where legal aid clinics and court self-help resources can genuinely help. If money is so tight that attorney fees are themselves a hardship, a legal aid consultation costs nothing and can tell you whether your case is simple enough to self-file.

But the line is clear, and it is worth respecting: if your case has any complication — a home with equity, a business, a co-signed debt, a creditor threatening litigation, a recent property transfer, or income near the means-test boundary — the cost of a mistake dwarfs the attorney's fee. A dismissed case costs you the filing fee, months of delay, and a second filing with a worse record. A missed exemption can cost you property. The honest math is not "attorney versus free." It is "attorney versus the price of getting a complicated case wrong."

## 8. Score three consultations before you decide

Never hire the first attorney you meet, even if the meeting went well. Take three consultations — many bankruptcy attorneys offer a free or low-cost first meeting — and score each one on the same sheet:

- Chapter-fit reasoning: did they explain the means test and the alternative chapter in numbers? (0–3 points)
- Fee transparency: total cost, inclusions, and extras in writing? (0–3 points)
- Local knowledge: named trustees, district experience, 341 preparation? (0–3 points)
- Communication: who handles your case, and how fast do they respond? (0–3 points)
- Pressure: did they rush you, guarantee an outcome, or dodge a hard question? (lose 3 points for any yes)

The highest score usually is not the cheapest quote — and that is the point. You are not buying a form. You are buying judgment about which chapter, which exemptions, and which risks, applied to your finances by someone who will stand next to you at the 341 meeting and in front of the judge if it comes to that. Choose the attorney whose answers were the most specific, whose fee was the most transparent, and whose chapter recommendation survived your hardest questions. That is the attorney most likely to get your case from filing to discharge without surprises — and in bankruptcy, no surprises is the best outcome there is.
