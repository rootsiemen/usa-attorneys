Most guides on how to choose an estate planning attorney give you a list of questions and wish you luck. That is the wrong tool for the job. Questions without a scoring system produce vibes, not decisions — and three consultations later, every attorney blurs into the same polite person in a conference room. You need a way to compare them side by side, on the record, before you hand over a few thousand dollars and the legal future of your family.

So here is a different approach: a 100-point scorecard. Interview two or three attorneys, score each one on the same nine categories, and hire the highest total. The scorecard catches the things a gut feeling misses — the firm that quotes a trust but never funds it, the "estate planning" practice that is really 10% of a generalist's week, the flat fee that quietly excludes the only work that matters. Use it exactly as written, and the choice mostly makes itself.

## The one-question filter: who drafts, and who funds?

Before the scorecard, run a single filter that eliminates half the field in under a minute. Ask every candidate this exact question: **"After I sign, who retitles my house and bank accounts into the trust, and is that included in your fee?"**

This is the question that separates a document package from an actual plan. A trust that is never funded — meaning your assets are never legally moved into the trust's name — does nothing when you die. Your family still goes through probate, which is the expensive court process the trust was supposed to avoid. Industry surveys and practitioner experience consistently put the unfunded-trust rate at roughly one in three trusts, and the cause is almost always the same: the attorney drafted the documents, handed the client a funding checklist, and nobody ever followed through.

There are only three acceptable answers:

- **"We do it."** The firm handles retitling and deed recording itself, or supervises it directly, and the fee covers it. This is the gold standard.
- **"We do it with you, step by step."** The firm schedules funding appointments, reviews every retitled account, and does not close your file until funding is confirmed. Nearly as good.
- **"You do it, here is a checklist."** This is a document sale, not a plan. Score it accordingly — and if the firm charges trust prices for it, walk away.

If an attorney dodges the question, changes the subject to tax savings, or says funding is "easy, don't worry about it," cross them off before you score anything else. No scorecard rescues a plan that never gets funded.

## Start with your situation, not a search box

People usually start by searching for an attorney near them and calling the first three results. Flip the order. Your situation determines which kind of attorney you need, and two families with the same zip code can need very different lawyers.

**Simple situation:** married couple, one house, two kids, retirement accounts with named beneficiaries, no business, no property in another state. You need a competent drafter with a flat fee and a funding process. You do not need a tax specialist, and you should not pay for one.

**Complicated situation:** blended family with children from different marriages, a business, rental property in two states, a child with special needs, or an estate anywhere near the federal estate-tax exemption (about $14 million per person in 2026). You need someone who handles these fact patterns weekly, coordinates with your CPA, and can explain which structures fit your family instead of selling one package to everyone.

**Urgent situation:** a parent's health is declining fast, or incapacity is already a real risk. Speed matters more than polish. Ask directly how fast the firm can get a power of attorney and healthcare proxy signed — those two documents protect a living person, and they matter before the will ever does.

Once you know your category, build the shortlist from three sources, not one: your state bar's lawyer directory (which confirms the license is active), referrals from your CPA or financial advisor (who see the aftermath of bad plans), and one independent search. A firm that appears in only one of those three places is not disqualified — but a firm that appears in none of them is not on your list at all.

## The 100-point scorecard: grade every candidate the same way

Score each attorney out of 100 during or immediately after the consultation, while the answers are fresh. Do not score from memory the next day. Bring the scorecard to the meeting — good attorneys respect it, and the ones who bristle at being graded are telling you something.

| Category | Points | What earns full marks |
|---|---|---|
| License and focus | 15 | Licensed in your state, and estate planning is at least half their practice |
| Drafting ownership | 15 | The attorney you meet drafts your documents personally, not a paralegal or template mill |
| Funding process | 20 | Firm handles or directly supervises retitling, and confirms it in writing |
| Fee clarity | 15 | Flat fee quoted in writing before drafting, with every inclusion and exclusion listed |
| State-law fluency | 10 | Can explain your state's probate costs, homestead rules, and small-estate threshold without hedging |
| Communication | 10 | Answers within one business day, explains trade-offs in plain language, no jargon walls |
| Post-signing support | 10 | Includes a review cycle and a clear price for future updates |
| Conflict handling | 5 | Explains who the client is when spouses attend together, and what happens if interests diverge |

Why funding gets 20 points — double any other category — is simple arithmetic. Our breakdown of [estate planning attorney fees](/blog/estate-planning-attorney-fees-will-vs-trust-cost-math) shows a $2,500 funded trust package saving a family roughly $15,000 in probate costs on a $450,000 estate, while an unfunded trust at the same price saves nothing. The funding line is worth more than the entire fee discussion, because it is the difference between the plan working and the plan existing only on paper.

**How to read the totals:** 85 or higher means hire with confidence. 70–84 means acceptable if the missing points are in low-weight categories like communication style rather than funding or drafting. Below 70 means keep looking, no matter how likable the attorney was. And if any candidate scores zero on funding, the total does not matter — that is an automatic fail, the same way a car with no brakes fails inspection regardless of the paint job.

## 14 questions with checkable answers

Generic advice says "ask about experience." Experience is not checkable — anyone can claim twenty years. Ask questions whose answers you can verify after the meeting, and write the answers down verbatim.

1. **What is your state bar number?** Look it up that evening. An active license with no public discipline is the floor, not a bonus.
2. **What percentage of your practice is estate planning?** You want 50% or higher. A generalist who drafts two wills a month charges the same fee as a specialist who drafts forty, and the specialist has seen your fact pattern before.
3. **Who drafts my documents — you, an associate, or a paralegal?** If the answer is anyone but the attorney you met, ask who reviews the drafts and how many revision rounds are included.
4. **How many estate plans did you complete last year?** Volume is a proxy for current fluency. Under twenty a year is a side practice.
5. **Will you review my asset titles and beneficiary designations, or only draft documents?** Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and insurance override your will. An attorney who never looks at them is planning with one eye closed.
6. **Does your fee include funding the trust, recording the deed, and confirming every retitle?** Get the answer in the engagement letter, not just in conversation.
7. **What exactly is excluded from the flat fee?** Deed recording fees, extra properties, business entities, and out-of-state property are the usual surprises.
8. **How do you handle property I own in another state?** Some states require ancillary probate even with a trust, and the answer reveals whether the attorney thinks beyond your home state.
9. **What happens to my file and my plan if you retire or close the practice?** Solo practitioners should have a succession arrangement. "I am not planning to retire" is not one.
10. **How do you coordinate with my CPA or financial advisor?** Tax, investment, and estate decisions interact. An attorney who never talks to your other advisors is planning in a vacuum.
11. **When should we review the plan, and what does a review cost?** Life changes — marriages, divorces, births, moves, new tax law — and a plan that is never reviewed rots. Expect a review every 3–5 years, priced well below the original fee.
12. **Do you handle probate and trust administration after a client dies?** A firm that administers estates sees where its own plans break. That feedback loop is worth a lot.
13. **Can you explain, in plain language, whether I need a will or a trust — and why?** If the answer starts with a package price instead of your assets and your state, you are being sold, not advised.
14. **What is your typical turnaround from first meeting to signed documents?** Two to four weeks is normal for a standard plan. Same-week promises on a complex estate are a red flag, not a feature.

Take the same fourteen questions to every candidate. The scorecard only works if the inputs are identical — otherwise you are comparing a firm you grilled against a firm you chatted with.

## Fee math: what the quote must include

Estate planning is one of the few legal services still sold mostly on flat fees, which means you can — and must — get the whole number in writing before drafting starts. Typical 2026 ranges: a simple will package runs $300–$1,200, a revocable living trust package $1,500–$4,000, and complex estates $4,000–$10,000 or more. Hourly billing ($200–$500 per hour) usually appears only for unusual situations like cleaning up a DIY trust that was done wrong.

The number that matters is not the headline fee. It is the **total cost of ownership**: the fee now, plus what your family pays later if the plan is incomplete. Three add-ons routinely hide outside the quoted price:

- **Funding labor.** Some firms quote $1,500 for trust documents, then bill $150–$300 per asset to retitle things. Five assets later, your "cheap" trust costs more than the firm that quoted $2,800 all-in.
- **Deed recording and transfer fees.** County recording fees are small — usually under $100 — but some firms mark them up or bill a separate "recording service" charge.
- **Future updates.** A plan that costs $400 to amend every time a grandchild is born is a subscription, not a plan. Ask for the amendment price now, while you still have leverage.

Worked example: Firm A quotes $1,800 for a trust package, funding excluded, at $200 per asset for six assets. Firm B quotes $2,900 all-in, funding included and confirmed. Firm A costs $3,000 and leaves the funding to you; Firm B costs $2,900 and guarantees the plan works. The cheaper quote is the more expensive plan. This is why the scorecard weights funding above fee clarity — and why you should never pick a firm on the headline number alone. ## Red flags that end the interview

Some answers are not low scores. They are exits. If you hear any of the following, thank the attorney, leave, and do not score the rest of the meeting:

- **A price quoted before asking what you own or what state you are in.** The plan follows the facts. A firm that prices first is selling a product line.
- **"One trust avoids probate, taxes, and lawsuits."** No single document does all three. Absolute claims in estate planning are marketing, not law.
- **Pressure to sign or pay today.** Estate planning is rarely a same-day emergency. Urgency is a sales tactic, and it works best on people who are already anxious about mortality.
- **Refusal to identify who drafts the documents or who the responsible attorney is.** If you cannot get a name, you cannot verify a license.
- **Selling investments, insurance, or annuities inside the planning engagement.** An attorney who earns commission on the products inside your plan has a conflict, and disclosure does not cure it.
- **Asking you to sign blank documents, or requesting passwords to your accounts by ordinary email.** Both are hard stops. Walk out.
- **Dismissing your questions about state law or family conflict.** If the attorney will not discuss the hard parts before you pay, they will not discuss them after you pay either.

One more quiet red flag: the attorney who never asks about your family. Estate plans fail on people, not paperwork — the second marriage, the estranged sibling, the child who should not receive a lump sum at eighteen. A consultation that never touches your family dynamics is a form-filling exercise, and forms are available far cheaper than attorney fees.

## After signing: the 90-day funding check

Hiring well is only half the job. The other half is making sure the plan you bought actually takes effect, and the window for that is the first 90 days after signing.

**Week 1:** Confirm you received executed originals (or certified copies) of every document, plus a plain-language summary of what each one does. Store the originals somewhere your executor or successor trustee can actually find them — a safe deposit box nobody else can open is a classic failure mode.

**Weeks 2–6:** Funding. If the firm handles it, demand written confirmation for every retitled account and the recorded deed. If you handle it, work through the list with the firm's checklist and send the firm proof of each retitle. Do not accept "we will get to it" as a status.

**Day 90:** The funding audit. Sit down with your statements and check: is the house titled in the trust's name? Are the bank and brokerage accounts retitled? Do beneficiary designations match the plan? One afternoon of checking now prevents a probate case later — and if anything is missing, the firm is still engaged, still reachable, and still responsible.

**Ongoing:** Calendar a review every 3–5 years, and sooner after any marriage, divorce, birth, death, move to another state, or major tax-law change. Also remember that a will-based plan still sends your family through probate; if that prospect bothers you, our state-by-state breakdown of [how long probate takes](/blog/how-long-does-probate-take-timeline-state-by-state) shows why the funding question matters so much in the first place.

## When you do not need an estate planning attorney at all

Honest guides say when not to buy, and this one does too. You may not need an attorney right now if you are young, single, renting, with no children, no real estate, and all your accounts name beneficiaries. In that situation, a simple will from a reputable online service plus a healthcare proxy may cover you — and a good attorney will tell you that in the consultation instead of selling you a trust.

The line moves the moment any of these appear: you buy a house, you have a child, you marry or divorce, you start a business, you own property in another state, or a parent's health declines and you may need to act for them. At that point, the cost of getting it wrong — a guardianship proceeding at $3,000–$10,000 because nobody signed a power of attorney, or a probate case that eats a year of your family's life — dwarfs the attorney's fee. That is the real math behind hiring well: you are not buying documents. You are buying the certainty that the documents work when nobody is around to fix them.

## How to choose an estate planning attorney: the short version

Choosing comes down to three moves. First, filter on funding: if the firm will not own the retitling work, it is not a plan. Second, score two or three candidates on the same 100-point scorecard, with identical questions, and hire the highest total — not the most likable conversation. Third, audit the funding yourself at day 90, because a signed trust that holds no assets is an expensive illusion.
