Probate is the one legal bill where the price is written into the law itself. In some states, a statute dictates exactly what the attorney earns down to the dollar. In others, the attorney names a price and the judge nods along. And the number the fee is calculated on — the estate's gross value, not its equity — surprises almost every family that inherits a mortgaged house.

Most families discover all of this after the fact, staring at a $26,000 legal bill on a $500,000 estate that had almost no cash in it. This guide runs the math before you sign anything: how probate attorneys price their work, what the statutes say in the five biggest states, what the same estate costs in each, and the legal shortcuts that can skip probate entirely.

## The three ways probate attorneys get paid

Every probate engagement in America falls into one of three pricing structures. Which ones are available depends on your state.

**1. Statutory percentage fees.** In a handful of states — California is the famous one — the probate code sets a sliding-scale percentage of the estate's value as the default attorney fee. The attorney does not have to justify hours. The fee is the fee. Courts can adjust it, but the schedule is the starting point and usually the ending point.

**2. Hourly billing.** The default everywhere else. Probate attorneys bill $200 to $500 per hour in most US markets, with $250 to $400 the common band for uncontested estate administration. An uncontested probate typically consumes 15 to 35 attorney hours spread over 6 to 18 months — filings, creditor notices, inventory, accounting, distribution — which puts the realistic range at $3,500 to $12,000 depending on the market and the estate's messiness.

**3. Flat fees.** Common for routine, uncontested probates in states without statutory schedules. Typical flats run $3,500 to $8,000 for a straightforward estate with a will, no disputes, and one or two real properties. Flat fees are popular with families because the number is known upfront — but the engagement letter matters. A flat fee that excludes will contests, property sales, tax filings, and creditor disputes will flip to hourly billing the moment any of those appear, and at least one of them appears in roughly a third of estates.

## The detail that shocks every family: fees are calculated on gross value

Here is the single most misunderstood fact in probate pricing. In states with statutory percentage schedules, the fee is calculated on the estate's **gross probate value** — the full fair market value of the assets going through probate — not the equity in them.

A house appraised at $500,000 with a $450,000 mortgage generates statutory fees on the full $500,000. The estate's actual equity is $50,000. The fee schedule does not care. Mortgages, liens, and debts are ignored for fee purposes — the statute looks at what the property is worth, not what the family keeps.

This is why the gross-value rule is the first thing to internalize. A family inheriting a heavily mortgaged home can owe legal fees that exceed the estate's net value. The attorney did nothing wrong; the math works exactly as written. The only defense is knowing the math exists before the retainer is signed.

## California: the 4-3-2-1 schedule, worked on a $500,000 estate

California Probate Code section 10800 sets the most rigid probate fee schedule in the country:

- 4% of the first $100,000
- 3% of the next $100,000
- 2% of the next $800,000
- 1% of the next $9,000,000
- 0.5% of the next $15,000,000

Run it on a $500,000 estate — say, a house worth $450,000 plus $50,000 in accounts:

- 4% × $100,000 = **$4,000**
- 3% × $100,000 = **$3,000**
- 2% × $300,000 = **$6,000**
- **Attorney fee: $13,000**

Now the part that doubles it. The executor — the person named in the will, or the administrator the court appoints — is entitled to the **same statutory schedule** as their commission. So on that $500,000 estate:

- Attorney: $13,000
- Executor: $13,000
- **Combined: $26,000 — 5.2% of the gross estate**

If the executor is a family member, they can waive their commission, and many do. But if they don't — and they are not required to — the estate pays both fees. On a $1,000,000 California estate, the combined statutory total reaches $46,000. On a $2,000,000 estate, $66,000. The schedule is public, fixed, and non-negotiable in practice, which makes California the most predictable — and often the most expensive — probate state for mid-size estates.

"Extraordinary fees" sit on top of the schedule. Property sales, will contests, tax return preparation, business appraisals, and litigation each generate additional court-approved fees, billed at the attorney's hourly rate. A contested California probate can easily run 2 to 3 times the statutory base.

## Florida: the presumed-reasonable schedule, worked on the same $500,000 estate

Florida Statute 733.617 sets a "presumed reasonable" attorney fee rather than a mandatory one, but in practice courts treat it as the default:

- $1,500 for estates up to $40,000
- $3,000 for $40,001 to $70,000
- $4,500 for $70,001 to $100,000
- 3% of $100,001 to $1,000,000
- 2.5% of $1,000,001 to $5,000,000
- 2% of $5,000,001 to $10,000,000
- 1.5% above $10,000,000

On the same $500,000 estate:

- Base for the first $100,000 = **$4,500**
- 3% × $400,000 = **$12,000**
- **Attorney fee: $16,500**

And Florida has its own double-bill. The personal representative gets a separate presumed-reasonable commission under section 733.6171: 3% of the first $1,000,000. On a $500,000 estate that is another **$15,000** — for a combined **$31,500** if both parties take the full schedule.

The word "presumed" matters, though. Unlike California's fixed schedule, Florida's can be adjusted up or down on evidence — and a family-member personal representative who waives their commission saves the estate $15,000 in one decision. That single waiver is the highest-value signature in Florida probate.

## New York: commissions are fixed, attorney fees are "reasonable"

New York splits the two roles. Executor commissions are fixed by Surrogate's Court Procedure Act section 2307 on a sliding scale:

- 5% of the first $100,000
- 4% of the next $200,000
- 3% of the next $700,000
- 2.5% of the next $4,000,000

On a $500,000 estate:

- 5% × $100,000 = **$5,000**
- 4% × $200,000 = **$8,000**
- 3% × $200,000 = **$6,000**
- **Executor commission: $19,000**

Attorney fees, however, are not on a schedule at all. They must be "reasonable," and the Surrogate's Court reviews them — the attorney submits an affidavit of services and the court approves, reduces, or occasionally increases the fee. In practice, approved fees on an uncontested $500,000 estate commonly land between $15,000 and $25,000, often tracking near the executor's commission as an informal benchmark. The uncertainty is the point: in New York, the attorney's bill is negotiated twice — once with you, once with the court.

## Texas and Illinois: no schedule, just the market

Texas has no statutory probate fee schedule. Fees are set by agreement and must be reasonable, which means the market decides. The typical Texas uncontested probate — with a valid will and an independent administration, which Texas makes unusually streamlined — runs $3,500 to $8,000 flat, or $250 to $450 per hour for roughly 12 to 25 hours of work. Texas's independent administration procedure, which lets the executor act without constant court supervision, is genuinely one of the cheapest probate systems in the country. A simple Texas probate can close in 4 to 6 months for under $5,000 in legal fees.

Illinois is similar: no statutory schedule, hourly or flat by agreement. Uncontested estates typically cost $3,500 to $7,500. Cook County and the Chicago suburbs run hotter — $350 to $500 per hour is normal there — while downstate rates sit 20 to 30 percent lower for the same work.

The honest summary for both states: without a statutory schedule, everything is negotiable, and shopping two or three firms can move the number by thousands. That is both the opportunity and the risk — there is no statutory ceiling protecting you and no statutory floor protecting the attorney.

## The same $500,000 estate, five states, one table

Hold the estate constant — $500,000 gross probate value, uncontested, family-member executor who does not waive their fee — and the legal bill looks like this:

| State | Attorney fee | Executor/PR fee | Combined | % of estate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| California | $13,000 (statutory) | $13,000 (statutory) | **$26,000** | 5.2% |
| Florida | $16,500 (presumed) | $15,000 (presumed) | **$31,500** | 6.3% |
| New York | $15,000–$25,000 (court-approved) | $19,000 (statutory) | **$34,000–$44,000** | 6.8–8.8% |
| Texas | $3,500–$8,000 (market) | by agreement, often $0–$5,000 | **$3,500–$13,000** | 0.7–2.6% |
| Illinois | $3,500–$7,500 (market) | by agreement | **$3,500–$12,000** | 0.7–2.4% |

Two things jump out. First, the statutory states cost 3 to 5 times what the market states cost for the identical estate. Second, the executor's fee is the swing variable everywhere — a family member who waives it cuts the bill nearly in half in California and Florida. If you take one number from this article, take that one: ask the executor question before you ask the attorney question.

Note the gross-value trap is baked into every row. If that $500,000 estate is a $500,000 house with a $400,000 mortgage, the family's real inheritance is $100,000 — and in Florida, the combined $31,500 fee schedule consumes nearly a third of it.

## Flat fee vs. hourly: the break-even math

When a Texas or Illinois attorney offers you a choice — say, a $6,000 flat fee or $300 per hour — the decision is arithmetic, not vibes.

Break-even: $6,000 ÷ $300/hour = **20 hours**.

If the probate takes fewer than 20 attorney hours, the flat fee costs you more. If it takes more, the flat fee saves you money. An uncontested probate with a will, one property, and cooperative heirs typically takes 12 to 20 hours — right at the line. Add a property sale, a creditor dispute, a missing heir, or a second property, and hours climb past 25 fast.

The practical rule: take the flat fee if the estate has any complication at all, because complications push hours up and the flat fee caps your exposure. Take hourly only for the cleanest possible estates — valid will, one bank account, no real estate, all heirs in agreement — and even then, get a written hour estimate and a cap in the engagement letter. And whichever you choose, make sure the letter defines what counts as "extraordinary" work that reverts to hourly, because that carve-out is where flat-fee surprises live.

## The cheapest probate is the one you avoid: small-estate shortcuts

Every state has procedures that skip formal probate for small estates, and the thresholds are more generous than most families realize:

- **California:** estates under **$208,850** (adjusted for inflation; the figure was $184,500 through 2024) can transfer property with a small-estate affidavit 40 days after death — no court filing, no attorney required. A separate simplified procedure covers real property worth up to $68,700.
- **Florida:** estates under **$75,000** (excluding exempt property) qualify for summary administration — a single court petition, no formal probate, often completed in weeks for $1,500 to $3,000 in legal fees. If the decedent died more than two years ago, summary administration is available regardless of value.
- **Texas:** estates under **$75,000** (excluding homestead and exempt property) can use a small estate affidavit, approved by the court usually within 30 days, typically costing under $2,000.
- **New York:** estates under **$50,000** qualify for the small-estate voluntary administration procedure — a simplified filing with the Surrogate's Court, often handled without an attorney.

The planning takeaway is bigger than the thresholds: assets with named beneficiaries — life insurance, retirement accounts, payable-on-death bank accounts, transfer-on-death deeds — bypass probate entirely and are excluded from the fee calculations. Every dollar moved out of the probate estate before death is a dollar the statutory schedules never touch. That is precisely why a [will vs. trust cost comparison](/blog/estate-planning-attorney-fees-will-vs-trust-cost-math) matters: a funded living trust does not go through probate at all, which means a $500,000 estate in a trust pays $0 in statutory probate fees instead of $26,000 in California.

## Questions to ask a probate attorney before you sign

1. **"Is your fee statutory, flat, or hourly — and what exactly does it cover?"** Get the engagement letter before the first filing, not after.
2. **"What counts as extraordinary work, and what is your hourly rate for it?"** This is the clause that turns a $6,000 flat fee into a $14,000 bill.
3. **"Will the executor's commission be taken in full?"** If the executor is family, discuss a waiver before the attorney's first invoice — it is the largest single saving available.
4. **"Can any of this estate avoid probate?"** Beneficiary designations, small-estate affidavits, and summary administration should be evaluated before a formal probate is opened, not after fees start accruing.
5. **"What is the realistic timeline, and what happens if heirs disagree?"** A 6-month uncontested probate and an 18-month contested one are different products at different prices. Know which one you are buying.
6. **"Who does the actual work — you or a paralegal — and at what rate?"** Routine filings billed at partner rates are the quietest fee inflator in probate practice.

## How to keep the bill down, in order of impact

**First, waive or negotiate the executor's commission.** On a $500,000 estate in California or Florida, this single decision saves $13,000 to $15,000 — more than any attorney-fee negotiation ever will.

**Second, check the small-estate threshold before opening probate.** If the estate qualifies for an affidavit or summary administration, formal probate fees never start accruing.

**Third, organize the paperwork before the first meeting.** Death certificates, the original will, account statements, property deeds, and a list of debts — every hour the attorney spends hunting documents is an hour on your invoice.

**Fourth, keep heirs aligned.** Will contests and heir disputes are the number-one fee multiplier. An uncontested $500,000 probate in Texas costs $5,000; a contested one anywhere costs $30,000 to $100,000. Family agreement is worth more than any fee schedule.

**Fifth, fund the trust.** If you are reading this as an estate planner rather than an heir, a properly funded living trust is the only move that takes the entire probate fee — statutory or otherwise — to zero. The upfront cost of the trust is a fraction of one probate.

Probate fees feel arbitrary until you see the schedules. Once you see them, they are just math — and math can be planned around.
