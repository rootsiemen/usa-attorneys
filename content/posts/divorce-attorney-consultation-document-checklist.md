Your first meeting with a [divorce attorney](/divorce-attorney) usually runs one of two ways. Version one: you walk in with a tote bag of unsorted paperwork, spend 90 minutes verbally reconstructing your financial life from memory, and leave with a vague quote. Version two: you hand over eight organized folders, the lawyer spots your leverage points in 20 minutes, and the meeting is spent on strategy — how your case will actually play out.

Both versions can cost you money. Only one earns it back.

## The math that makes preparation a financial decision

Divorce attorneys typically charge $200–$450 an hour (see our [divorce attorney cost guide](/blog/how-much-does-divorce-attorney-cost-2026) for the full breakdown). Even "free consultations" aren't really free — they're auditions where the lawyer decides whether your case is worth taking and how much retainer to ask for. A client who shows up organized signals a case that will be efficient to run, and lawyers price accordingly.

Run the numbers:

- **Unprepared client:** 2-hour first consult at $350/hr = **$700**, of which ~75 minutes ($437) is the lawyer asking "and where is that account again?" while you scroll through your phone.
- **Prepared client:** 40-minute focused consult at $350/hr = **$233**, and the free-consultation slots go further because the lawyer can assess the case fast.

That's roughly **$465–$600 saved at the very first meeting**, before the case even starts. The deeper savings are structural: organized financials mean your lawyer spends billable hours negotiating and drafting, not playing detective with your bank statements.

This checklist has 27 items across 8 folders. Bring paper copies, a USB drive, or a single shared cloud folder — just don't bring chaos.

## Folder 1: Income (the number everything else hangs on)

Nearly every divorce financial question — support, division of property, fees — starts with income. Bring:

1. **Last 3 years of tax returns** (federal + state, all schedules and W-2s/1099s). Three years, not one — courts and lawyers look for patterns and sudden changes.
2. **Your last 3 pay stubs** and your spouse's, if you can access them.
3. **Year-end pay statements** showing bonuses, commissions, overtime, and benefits.
4. **Business income records** if either spouse is self-employed: last 2 years of profit-and-loss statements, business tax returns, and K-1s.
5. **Any other income documentation:** rental income records, Social Security statements, disability payments, trust distributions.

Why it matters: income determines temporary support while the case runs and permanent support afterward. An attorney who can see both incomes clearly can tell you in the first meeting roughly where support will land — instead of billing you to figure it out in month three. See our [divorce timeline guide](/blog/how-long-does-divorce-take-timeline-month-by-month) for how long that discovery phase usually drags on.

## Folder 2: Real estate and big property

6. **Deed(s) to every property** either spouse owns — primary home, rentals, land, timeshares.
7. **Most recent mortgage statements** with current balances, interest rates, and monthly payments for each property.
8. **Property tax bills** and any home-equity line of credit (HELOC) statements.
9. **Appraisals or market valuations** done in the last 2 years, if any exist.
10. **Vehicle titles and loan statements** for every car, boat, or RV titled in either name.

Why it matters: the home is the largest asset in most divorces and the most fought-over. A lawyer who can see equity, mortgage terms, and refinancing math on day one can model the "keep the house vs. sell it" decision in the first meeting. Bring the numbers and you'll get analysis; bring guesses and you'll get billed for research.

## Folder 3: Bank, retirement, and investment accounts

11. **Last 3–6 months of statements** for every checking, savings, and money-market account — yours, your spouse's, and joint accounts.
12. **Retirement account statements:** 401(k)s, IRAs, pensions, military retirement, government pensions — most recent quarterly statements for each.
13. **Brokerage and crypto statements:** stock accounts, mutual funds, and any cryptocurrency wallets with current values.
14. **Life insurance policies** (whole life has cash value — term doesn't, but the attorney still wants to see beneficiaries).

Why it matters: retirement accounts are the single most common source of "I didn't know that was marital property" surprises. In most states, the portion of a 401(k) earned during the marriage is divisible even if only one spouse's name is on the account. Dividing them requires a QDRO (qualified domestic relations order) — a separate court order lawyers often forget to mention costs extra. Having the statements upfront means the retainer quote accounts for it.

## Folder 4: Debts

15. **Credit card statements** for all cards — balances, minimums, interest rates.
16. **Student loan statements** for both spouses.
17. **Personal loans, medical debt, and tax debt** (IRS or state payment plans).
18. **A free credit report for yourself** — this catches accounts you forgot about and, occasionally, accounts your spouse opened that you didn't know about.

Why it matters: debts get divided too, and the interest rate math is unforgiving. A lawyer who sees $40,000 of joint credit card debt at 24% APR knows that's costing you ~$800/month in interest alone — a concrete argument for settling fast. This is one reason [mediation vs. litigation](/blog/divorce-mediation-vs-litigation-cost-math) has such different price tags: every month of fighting has a financing cost.

## Folder 5: The kids (custody lives or dies on documentation)

19. **A parenting schedule log** — 30 to 60 days showing who actually does school drop-offs, doctor visits, homework, bedtime. Judges trust logged behavior over claimed intentions.
20. **School records and medical records** for each child (grades, attendance, pediatrician).
21. **Childcare costs:** daycare invoices, after-school program fees, summer camp costs — with monthly totals.
22. **Any evidence relevant to fitness concerns** — documented, dated, factual. Not a rant, not a list of grievances: dates, events, witnesses.

Why it matters: custody isn't decided by who loves the kids more — it's decided by documented caregiving patterns and the child's best interests as defined by your state. The parent who walks in with a 60-day care log and cost totals is negotiating from evidence. The one who walks in with "the kids should stay with me because I'm the better parent" is negotiating from billable hours.

## Folder 6: Existing legal documents

23. **Prenuptial or postnuptial agreements** — the original, not your summary of it.
24. **Prior court orders:** custody orders, support orders, restraining orders, prior divorce decrees.
25. **Wills, trusts, and powers of attorney** that name your spouse — these usually need immediate revision.
26. **Any business operating agreements or partnership documents** involving either spouse.

Why it matters: a prenup can make half your checklist irrelevant — or it can be invalid and nobody knows until someone reads it. This is the highest-leverage folder: 20 minutes of attorney review here can save $5,000 of discovery later.

## Folder 7: Your one-page case summary (item 27)

27. **A single page with:** your names, marriage date, separation date, children's names and ages, each spouse's employer and gross monthly income, and a 5-line timeline of what led to the filing. One page. Not a novel.

This is the document your lawyer reads before you start talking. It turns the first 15 minutes of background into 3 minutes of confirmation — and it gives you a written record of what you told them.

## How to organize it all: the 2-hour system

You don't need a filing cabinet. Here's the fastest method:

1. **Create one folder** — physical or digital — with 7 labeled subfolders matching the list above.
2. **Pull digital first:** log into every bank, brokerage, retirement, and lender account and download the most recent statement. This takes 60–90 minutes and covers ~20 of the 27 items.
3. **Photograph the paper:** deeds, prenups, old court orders — phone photos are fine for the consultation.
4. **Write the one-page summary last** — by then you'll have the facts straight.
5. **Flag the missing items.** Couldn't access your spouse's pay stubs? Write "spouse income: estimated $X from 2024 tax return" on a note in Folder 1. Lawyers would rather see an honest gap than a guess presented as fact.

Do not spend a week perfecting this. A good attorney will tell you what else they need — the point of the consultation is strategy, and strategy needs roughly 80% of the picture, not 100%.

## What NOT to bring (or do) before the meeting

- **Don't hide accounts or debts.** Your attorney can't protect what they don't know about, and hidden assets discovered later cost far more than disclosed ones — sanctions, credibility damage, and a much bigger bill.
- **Don't delete messages or social media.** Deleting evidence after a case is foreseeable can create legal problems worse than the posts themselves.
- **Don't sign anything your spouse gives you** — quitclaim deeds, "temporary" custody agreements, refinancing paperwork — before your lawyer reads it.
- **Don't move large sums of money** out of joint accounts without legal guidance. A documented, reasonable withdrawal for living expenses is different from draining an account.
- **Don't bring your spouse to the consultation** unless the lawyer asks. The meeting is for your strategy, and anything you say together complicates attorney-client confidentiality.

## The real comparison: three clients, three bills

Same case, same lawyer, same $350 hourly rate:

| Client | Consult length | First billable milestone | Estimated first-3-months cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| No documents, tells story from memory | 2 hours, unproductive | Lawyer spends 8 hrs reconstructing finances | ~$3,500 |
| Partial documents, some gaps | 1 hour, decent | Lawyer spends 4 hrs filling gaps | ~$2,100 |
| 27-item checklist, one-page summary | 40 minutes, strategic | Lawyer starts negotiating immediately | ~$1,200 |

The difference isn't the lawyer — it's the input. Attorneys don't bill less because you're nice; they bill less when there's less to do.

## After the meeting: the 48-hour test

A good consultation ends with specifics: a realistic timeline (see our [divorce timeline guide](/blog/how-long-does-divorce-take-timeline-month-by-month)), a fee range with what's included, and 2–3 next steps with dates. If you leave with only "we'll figure it out," that's data too — interview another attorney.
