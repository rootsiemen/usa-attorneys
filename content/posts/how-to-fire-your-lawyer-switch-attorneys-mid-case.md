Every year, thousands of clients reach the same uncomfortable conclusion halfway through a case: the lawyer they hired is not the lawyer they need. Calls go unanswered for weeks. Strategy is never explained. Deadlines arrive as surprises. And then comes the question that keeps unhappy clients stuck for months — if I fire my lawyer now, will I lose my case, and will I end up paying two lawyers for one case?

The short answer: you can fire your lawyer at any time, for any reason, and the right to do so cannot be signed away in any [retainer agreement](/blog/retainer-agreement-clauses-to-read-before-signing). But the exit is a process, not a moment — and handled badly, it costs you weeks, a second retainer, and in rare cases a judge's refusal at the worst possible time. Handled well, it costs you almost nothing extra. This guide covers the switching math, the timing windows, and the exact sequence to follow.

## 1. Your right to fire your lawyer — and the one real limit on it

The attorney-client relationship is voluntary on both sides. You do not need your lawyer's permission to end it, you do not need to prove misconduct, and "I have lost confidence in this representation" is a complete reason. Your lawyer also cannot hold your case hostage: ethics rules in every state require a lawyer to take reasonable steps to protect your interests on withdrawal, including giving you notice and time to find new counsel.

There is one genuine limit, and it matters. Once a lawsuit is filed and the lawyer has formally appeared in court, the lawyer usually needs the judge's permission to withdraw. Judges grant it in the overwhelming majority of cases — a client who wants new counsel is not forced to keep unwanted counsel — but a judge can delay or, rarely, deny withdrawal if it would prejudice the other side or derail an imminent trial. A withdrawal request filed three weeks before a jury trial, with no replacement lawyer lined up, is the scenario most likely to be denied or postponed. The same request filed six months before trial, with new counsel already retained, is almost always granted on the spot.

Practical rule: hire the replacement first, then fire the original. Never create a gap where you are unrepresented in active litigation.

## 2. The 7 signs it is actually time to switch

Not every frustration justifies a switch — cases are slow, and losing a motion is normal. Switch when the problem is the representation itself, not the case:

1. **No communication for 3+ weeks** despite repeated calls and emails, with no explanation and no staff response. A busy lawyer is normal; a silent one is not.
2. **You learn about deadlines and hearings from the other side** or from court notices, not from your own lawyer.
3. **No strategy, ever.** You cannot get a straight answer to "what happens next, and what are we trying to achieve in the next 90 days?"
4. **Pressure to settle without numbers.** You are told to "take the offer" but never shown the math — the gross offer, the fee, the costs, and what you actually walk away with.
5. **Missed deadlines or blown filings.** Even one missed deadline is a red flag; two is a pattern that can cost you the case.
6. **Billing you cannot audit.** Invoices that say "legal services: $4,200" with no itemization, or charges for work you never authorized.
7. **A trust breakdown you cannot repair.** You have raised the problem directly, given the lawyer a chance to fix it, and nothing changed. One honest conversation should come before any switch — sometimes the fix is a staffing change or a communication plan, not a new firm.

Do not switch because the case is moving slowly. A [personal injury settlement timeline](/blog/personal-injury-settlement-timeline-month-by-month) routinely runs 12 to 18 months, and most of that time is medical treatment, discovery, and negotiation — not your lawyer doing nothing. Switching over impatience alone buys you a new lawyer, a new learning curve, and the same timeline.

## 3. The switching math: what the old lawyer gets paid

This is the question that keeps clients stuck, so here is the math in full. You do not pay two full fees. The total fee for the case stays roughly where your original agreement put it — the two lawyers split it, or the old lawyer is paid for work actually done.

**Hourly cases.** The rule is simple: you pay for hours earned through the termination date, and the unearned retainer comes back. Worked example: you deposited a $10,000 advance retainer at $375 per hour. The firm billed 22.6 hours before you terminated — $8,475 earned. Your refund is $1,525, and it should arrive with a final itemized invoice. If the agreement called the retainer "non-refundable" with no pro-rata refund formula, that clause is worth challenging through your state bar's fee-arbitration program — many states restrict non-refundable fees to reasonable amounts with written client consent.

**Contingency cases (personal injury and similar).** This is where the fear of "paying twice" lives, and it is mostly a myth. Say your original agreement promised the lawyer 33% of any recovery, and your case settles for $120,000 after you switch. The total fee is still about $39,600 — 33% of $120,000 — not $39,600 to each lawyer. What happens is a fee split: the old lawyer and the new lawyer agree, often in writing, on how to divide the single fee based on the work each did. A common outcome on a case that switched after 18 months of work might be 40% of the fee ($15,840) to the old lawyer and 60% ($23,760) to the new one. If the two lawyers cannot agree, the old lawyer's claim is decided by "quantum meruit" — Latin for "as much as deserved" — meaning the reasonable value of the hours and results the old lawyer actually contributed.

The one scenario where switching genuinely costs extra: the old lawyer asserts a lien for unpaid hourly fees or costs in a case that has not yet produced a recovery, and the new lawyer will not take the case until that lien is resolved. That is a negotiation problem, not a double-fee problem — and it is exactly why you get the new lawyer involved before you fire the old one, so the fee split is negotiated lawyer-to-lawyer while you stay out of the middle.

| Situation | What you pay | Who gets what |
|---|---|---|
| Hourly case, $10,000 retainer, 22.6 hrs at $375 | $8,475 total | Old lawyer $8,475; $1,525 refunded to you |
| Contingency case, $120,000 settlement, 33% fee | $39,600 total | Split between old and new lawyer (e.g. 40/60) |
| Contingency case, no recovery yet | Nothing yet | New lawyer's fee comes from any future recovery |
| Flat-fee case, work half done | Pro-rata share | Old lawyer keeps the earned portion; balance refunded or credited |

Compare that to the cost of staying: a lawyer who misses a filing deadline or pressures you into a low settlement can cost you tens of thousands in case value. The switching math almost always favors switching when the representation is genuinely broken. ## 4. What the new lawyer charges — the file-learning cost

The real hidden cost of switching is not a second fee. It is the learning curve. A new lawyer stepping into a case at month 14 needs 10 to 20 hours just to read the file, understand the evidence, and get up to speed — $3,500 to $7,000 at typical hourly rates, or the equivalent time on a contingency case where the new lawyer absorbs it into their share of the fee.

That cost is why timing matters so much, and why the cheapest switch is the early one:

| When you switch | File-learning cost | Disruption risk |
|---|---|---|
| Before filing (pre-suit) | Near zero — the file is thin | Minimal |
| During discovery (months 3–9) | Moderate — depositions and documents to review | Low |
| After discovery, pre-trial (months 10–18) | High — 10–20 hours to master the record | Moderate |
| Within 60 days of trial | Very high, plus judge may delay withdrawal | High |

Ask the new lawyer directly: "How many hours will you need to get up to speed, and who pays for them?" On a contingency case, the answer should be that the learning time comes out of the new lawyer's share of the fee, not as an extra charge to you. On an hourly case, negotiate a cap — "no more than 12 hours of file review billed" — before you sign. And run the same [questions to ask before hiring](/blog/questions-to-ask-before-hiring-criminal-defense-attorney) any attorney, even if your case is not criminal: who does the work, what does it cost, and how will we communicate are universal questions.

## 5. The step-by-step switch, in order

Do these in this exact sequence. Order is what prevents gaps, lost files, and missed deadlines.

1. **Interview and hire the new lawyer first.** Sign the new retainer before you terminate the old one. The new lawyer should know you are switching and agree to handle the transition.
2. **Let the new lawyer negotiate the fee split.** Lawyer-to-lawyer negotiation over the old lawyer's lien or fee share is faster, calmer, and cheaper than you negotiating it yourself. Get any split agreement in writing.
3. **Send a short written termination.** Email and certified mail: "Effective immediately, I am terminating your representation in [case name/number]. Please cease all work, preserve my file, and transfer it to [new lawyer, address, email] within [7–14] days. Please send a final itemized invoice and refund any unearned retainer." Keep it factual — no accusations, no long explanations. The letter becomes part of the record.
4. **Confirm the file transfer in writing.** Your file includes pleadings, correspondence, discovery, evidence, and work product. In most states the old lawyer must surrender it promptly, though some states allow a retaining lien on the file for unpaid fees — if that is invoked, your new lawyer should demand copies of everything time-sensitive immediately, since ethics rules generally require surrender of anything you need to avoid prejudice.
5. **File the substitution of counsel.** In active litigation, your new lawyer files a substitution of attorney with the court and serves it on the other side. Until that is filed, the old lawyer remains counsel of record — another reason to hire first and fire second.
6. **Audit the final invoice within 30 days.** Check every line against the work you actually saw happen. Object in writing to anything disputed and pay the undisputed portion. Silence plus nonpayment is how fee disputes are lost — your state bar's fee-arbitration program is the venue if the final bill is genuinely unfair.
7. **Calendar every deadline yourself during the transition.** The handoff week is when things slip. Ask the new lawyer, in writing, to confirm the next three deadlines in your case and who owns them.

## 6. What can go wrong — and how to prevent it

**The retaining lien.** In many states, an unpaid lawyer can hold your file until the bill is settled. Prevention: pay the undisputed portion of the final invoice immediately, dispute the rest in writing, and have the new lawyer request time-sensitive documents on day one.

**The gap.** A week with no lawyer of record in active litigation can mean a missed response deadline or a default. Prevention: never terminate before the new retainer is signed, and confirm the substitution filing the same week.

**The judge says not yet.** Near trial, a judge may postpone withdrawal until after a scheduled hearing to avoid prejudice. Prevention: switch early, and if you must switch late, present the judge with a retained replacement lawyer and a transition plan, not just a complaint.

**The fee fight.** Old and new lawyers sometimes cannot agree on the split, and the dispute drags past settlement. Prevention: a written split agreement before the switch, and a clause in the new retainer stating the total fee percentage will not exceed your original agreement.

**The duplicate-work bill.** The new lawyer re-does discovery the old lawyer already completed. Prevention: insist the file transfer is complete before the new lawyer bills file-review hours, and cap those hours in the new retainer.

## 7. When you should NOT switch

Switching is a tool, not a reflex. Do not switch when the real problem is one of these:

- **The timeline feels slow.** Most cases take 12 to 18 months. A new lawyer inherits the same court calendar, the same discovery schedule, and the same insurance company.
- **One ruling went against you.** Motions get denied. A lost motion is not a lost case, and it is not evidence of bad lawyering.
- **You found a cheaper quote.** A lawyer quoting half the fee for the same case is often quoting half the work. Compare scope, not just price — and remember the file-learning cost wipes out small fee differences.
- **You have not raised the problem yet.** Give the current lawyer one direct, specific conversation first: "I have not heard from you in three weeks, and I need a strategy update by Friday." If that fixes it, you just saved yourself a switch. If it does not, you have documented the breakdown that justifies one.

If you have raised the problem, documented it, and nothing changed — or if a deadline was missed, a settlement was pushed without numbers, or communication has collapsed entirely — switching is not disloyal. It is case management. The lawyer works for you, not the other way around.

## The bottom line

Firing your lawyer costs less than most clients fear and staying with a broken representation costs more than most clients admit. The total fee on a contingency case does not double — it splits. The unearned hourly retainer comes back. The real costs are the learning curve and the timing, and both are controllable: switch early when you can, hire the replacement before you fire the original, get the fee split in writing, and calendar every deadline through the handoff week.
