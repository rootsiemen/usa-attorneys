Ask ten people how long a personal injury case takes and you will get ten answers: "six weeks," "a year," "my cousin waited three." The truth is that almost every case follows the same sequence of phases, and the phases — not the people — decide the calendar. A straightforward soft-tissue car crash claim usually resolves in **5 to 9 months**. A disputed-liability case with surgery typically runs **12 to 24 months**. And a case that goes all the way to a jury verdict can take **2 to 4 years**.

This guide walks through each phase month by month, with the timeframes attorneys actually see, the math on what delays cost, and what makes a case move faster or stall. For the money side — how contingency fees slice your settlement — see [Personal Injury Lawyer Contingency Fee Math](/blog/personal-injury-lawyer-contingency-fee-math). For choosing who runs this timeline for you, see [How to Choose a Personal Injury Lawyer](/blog/how-to-choose-personal-injury-lawyer).

## The 30-Second Version

Most cases pass through five phases: **treatment (1–6 months), demand and negotiation (2–4 months), filing and discovery (6–18 months), mediation/trial prep (2–6 months), and payout (2–6 weeks)**. Only about 4–5% of filed personal injury lawsuits reach a trial; roughly 95% settle somewhere along the way. The single biggest lever on your timeline is medical treatment length — a claim generally cannot settle until treatment ends or reaches maximum medical improvement (MMI).

## Month 0–1: The Accident, the Investigation, and the Paper Trail

Day one starts the clock on evidence that only exists early. Police reports are usually available within **5–10 days**. Traffic-camera and business-surveillance footage is typically overwritten within **7–30 days**, which is why attorneys send preservation letters immediately. Witnesses' memories decay measurably within weeks — statements taken in the first 14 days are far more detailed than statements taken at month three.

What happens in month one:

- Medical intake: ER visit, then follow-ups with a primary doctor or orthopedist. The **first medical record** becomes the anchor every later document references.
- The attorney opens a claim with the at-fault driver's insurer and notifies your own insurer (required under most policies within a set window, commonly **30 days**).
- Property damage (the car) is handled separately and usually settles in **2–6 weeks** — don't confuse this with the injury claim, which is slower by design.

**Worked example:** You are rear-ended on October 1. ER visit the same day ($4,200 billed). Chiropractic care starts October 5 at $150/session, 3× weekly. By October 31, you have 13 sessions — $1,950 in treatment — plus documented missed work. Your file is small but clean: no gaps, no missing records. This is the ideal start.

## Months 1–6: Treatment — The Longest Phase Nobody Controls

This is where most of the calendar goes, and it is the phase attorneys have the least control over. Your body sets the schedule. A whiplash claim with physical therapy runs **2–4 months** of treatment. A herniated disc requiring injections runs **4–8 months**. A fracture requiring surgery runs **6–12 months** before you are even eligible to settle.

The rule that governs everything: **a settlement demand should not go out until treatment ends or reaches MMI** — the point where doctors say you are as healed as you are going to get. Why? Because no insurer pays for future medical costs without documentation of what those costs are, and no attorney can value pain-and-suffering without knowing whether you recovered or are permanently impaired.

Two treatment behaviors change timelines:

1. **Gaps in treatment** (a 3–6 week stretch with no visits) give the insurer's adjuster one sentence to cut your offer: "the injuries must not have been serious." A gap of 30+ days can shave **20–40%** off a soft-tissue offer.
2. **Following through on referrals** — if the ER refers you to orthopedics and you skip it for two months, the record reads "patient declined recommended care," which adjusters quote verbatim.

Meanwhile, behind the scenes, your attorney is ordering medical records and bills from every provider — a process that takes **2–6 weeks per provider**, and providers routinely charge $25–$75 per records request plus per-page fees.

**Worked example (continued):** Your whiplash treatment runs October through January — 16 weeks, 40 sessions, $6,000 in chiropractic bills plus the $4,200 ER visit. Total medical specials: **$10,200**. You missed 8 workdays at $240/day = $1,920 lost wages. The file is ready for a demand in February — 4 months after the crash.

## Months 4–8: The Demand Package and First Offer

Once treatment ends, your attorney compiles the demand package: medical records, bills, wage-loss documentation, the police report, photos, and a narrative letter with a settlement demand. Preparing the package takes **2–4 weeks**.

The demand is deliberately set above the real target — typically **2 to 3 times the actual case value** — because negotiation is expected. The insurer's response time varies wildly: a responsive adjuster answers in **2–4 weeks**; a slow one takes **6–8 weeks**, and some carriers (the ones plaintiff attorneys know by reputation) routinely sit on demands for **60–90 days**.

The first offer is almost always low — commonly **25–40%** of the demand, or roughly **1× medical bills** on a soft-tissue case. Counter-offers then bounce back and forth in rounds, each round taking **1–3 weeks**. A clean case with clear liability settles in **2–4 rounds**.

**Worked example (continued):** Demand goes out February 15 for **$45,000** (roughly 4× medicals, accounting for pain and suffering on a 4-month injury). The adjuster responds March 10 with **$12,000**. Two more rounds over the next five weeks land at **$22,000**. You accept. Total time from crash to settlement: just under **6 months**. Under a standard [33% contingency fee](/blog/personal-injury-lawyer-contingency-fee-math), your net (before case costs) is about **$14,740**.

Compare that with the gap-in-treatment version: same crash, but a 6-week gap in November–December. The offer stalls at $15,000 — **$7,000 less**, for the same injuries — because the adjuster has a documented gap to point at.

## Months 6–24: If It Doesn't Settle — Filing the Lawsuit

Roughly **50–70%** of injury claims settle in the demand phase. The rest get filed as lawsuits, and filing resets the clock in a specific way: it does **not** mean you are going to trial. Filing is leverage — it tells the insurer the claim will not die of old age. Most filed cases still settle, often within **6–12 months** of filing.

Filing is deadline-driven: every state has a **statute of limitations** (commonly 2–3 years for personal injury, but some states are 1 year and some are 4–6). Miss it and the case is gone, period. Attorneys usually file well before the deadline, often at the **12–18 month** mark if negotiations have stalled.

After filing, the discovery phase begins — both sides exchange documents, answer written questions (interrogatories), and take depositions. Timelines:

- **Months 1–6 post-filing:** written discovery, document exchange. Each side gets **30 days** to respond to each round, and there are usually 2–3 rounds.
- **Months 4–12 post-filing:** depositions. Your deposition takes **2–4 hours**; scheduling all depositions in a case takes months because four to six people's calendars must align.
- **Months 6–18 post-filing:** medical examinations by the defense's doctor, expert reports, and motions.

A case with surgery and disputed liability commonly looks like this: crash in January, surgery in June, demand in October (**$150,000**), negotiations stall at $60,000, lawsuit filed the following March, depositions through summer, settlement at mediation 16 months after filing for **$110,000**. Total: **~28 months**.

## Months 12–36: Mediation, Trial Prep, and the 5% That Go to Trial

Before any trial, courts in most jurisdictions require **mediation** — a full-day (sometimes two-day) settlement conference with a neutral mediator. Mediation settles roughly **70–80%** of the cases that reach it. It typically happens **12–18 months** after filing.

If mediation fails, trial prep begins: exhibit lists, witness prep, jury instructions, expert testimony dry-runs. A trial itself lasts **2–5 days** for a typical injury case, but getting a trial *date* is the bottleneck — court dockets in busy counties run **6–18 months** out from the ready-for-trial date.

The economics of going to trial explain why 95% of cases settle: a trial costs **$15,000–$50,000+** in expert fees, court reporters, and exhibits (advanced by your attorney and deducted from your recovery), your contingency fee typically jumps from **33% to 40%** once trial begins, and a jury is unpredictable — defense verdicts happen even in strong cases. Insurers know this math too, which is why serious settlement offers often arrive **2–4 weeks before trial**, after both sides have spent the prep money.

## Month of Settlement + 30–45 Days: The Payout Phase

"Settled" does not mean "paid today." The disbursement sequence after a settlement agreement:

1. **Release signed (1–2 weeks):** you sign the settlement release; the insurer processes it.
2. **Check issued (2–4 weeks):** the insurer mails the settlement check to your attorney's trust account. Federal and state rules require it to clear — typically **5–10 business days**.
3. **Liens resolved (1–3 weeks):** health insurers, Medicare/Medicaid, and medical providers with liens get paid or negotiated down from the proceeds. This is where an experienced attorney earns the fee a second time — lien reductions of **20–50%** are common and go straight to your pocket.
4. **Your check (1 week):** the attorney disburses your net share with a full accounting.

Total payout phase: **30–60 days** from agreement to money in hand. Medicare liens are the slowest — a final demand letter from Medicare can take **60–120 days**, and settling without resolving a Medicare lien exposes you to federal penalties, so this step is never skipped.

**Worked example (continued):** The $22,000 settlement from the whiplash case. Release signed week 1, check arrives week 4, clears by week 5. The health insurer paid $3,000 of your bills and asserts a lien; your attorney negotiates it to **$1,800** (a $1,200 savings to you). Case costs were $450 (records, postage, filing). Your final check: $22,000 − $7,260 (33% fee) − $1,800 (lien) − $450 (costs) = **$12,490**, received about **7 months** after the crash.

## What Speeds Cases Up — and What Slows Them Down

| Factor | Effect on timeline |
|---|---|
| Clear liability (rear-end, DUI driver) | −2 to −4 months |
| Soft-tissue only, short treatment | Settles in 5–9 months |
| Surgery or permanent impairment | +6 to +18 months |
| Gaps in treatment | Lower offers, +1–3 months of arguing |
| Commercial defendant (trucking company) | +6 to +12 months (more insurers, more lawyers) |
| Uninsured/underinsured motorist claim | +2–4 months (your own insurer fights harder than you'd expect) |
| Pre-existing injury to same body part | +3–6 months of medical causation debate |
| Client responds to attorney same-day | −1 to −2 months across the case |

The trucking-company row deserves emphasis: commercial cases involve the driver, the motor carrier, the trailer owner, the shipper, and sometimes a broker — each with its own insurer. See [Truck Accident vs Car Accident Lawsuit Differences](/blog/truck-accident-vs-car-accident-lawsuit-differences) for why these cases follow a different, longer playbook.

## The Comparison Nobody Makes: Time Value of Settling Early

Attorneys sometimes frame an early low offer as "leaving money on the table." Run the time-value math before deciding:

- **Offer A:** $22,000 at month 6 → net ~$14,740 (33% fee, before liens/costs).
- **Offer B (after filing):** $30,000 at month 16 → net ~$18,000 (40% trial-track fee, before liens/costs), minus roughly **$3,000–$5,000** more in advanced case costs.

The real gain from 10 extra months of litigation: **$0 to $3,000** — roughly $0–$300/month for your patience, with the risk that a jury or a bad deposition day erases the gain entirely. Filing is the right move when liability is disputed or the offer is genuinely insulting (under 1× medicals on a clean case). It is the wrong move when the offer is fair and you just want "a bit more" — the fee bump and costs eat the difference.

## Key Takeaways

- A clean soft-tissue case takes **5–9 months**; surgery cases take **12–24 months**; filed lawsuits that reach trial take **2–4 years**.
- Treatment length controls the timeline — settle only at or after MMI, never mid-treatment.
- Gaps in treatment are the most common self-inflicted delay, costing both time and **20–40%** of offer value.
- Filing a lawsuit is usually leverage, not a path to trial — most filed cases settle within a year of filing, often at mediation.
- After settlement, expect **30–60 days** to disbursement, with lien negotiation as the final (and financially meaningful) step.
