You have just been charged, and two doors stand in front of you. One is free: a court-appointed public defender. The other can cost as much as a car: a private [criminal defense attorney](/criminal-defense-attorney). Friends will tell you "never trust a free lawyer," and private firms will tell you a public defender is too overworked to care. Both claims are too simple, and both cost people real money and real outcomes.

The honest comparison is not free versus good. It is a math problem with three variables: how many cases your lawyer carries, how many hours your case actually gets, and how much investigation and expert money is available to spend on it. Run those three numbers, and the right choice for your specific charge usually becomes obvious.

## 1. The eligibility line: who actually gets a public defender

A public defender is not available to everyone who wants one. A judge appoints one only after finding that you cannot afford private counsel, and the test varies by state and even by county. Most courts look at income, assets, household size, and the seriousness of the charge. Some use a multiple of the federal poverty guidelines; others use a looser "can you hire a lawyer without substantial hardship" standard.

That means three groups of people fall through the cracks:

- **People who earn too much on paper but have no savings.** A $55,000 salary with rent, debt, and no liquid savings can still leave no realistic way to pay a $10,000 felony retainer, yet a strict income cutoff may still disqualify you.
- **People charged with low-level misdemeanors in busy courts.** Some jurisdictions appoint counsel only when jail time is a realistic possibility, which is the constitutional floor, not a generous policy.
- **People who own an asset they cannot sell quickly.** A car needed for work or a small retirement account can count against you even though it cannot fund a defense today.

If you are denied a public defender, you can ask the court to reconsider with a fuller financial picture — pay stubs, rent, debts, and bank balances, not just a salary figure. If you are appointed one, the representation is real legal representation, not a lesser category of lawyer: public defenders are licensed attorneys, they appear in the same courtrooms every day, and they often know the prosecutors and judges better than any outsider. That local knowledge is worth more than most people realize.

Note also that "free" is not always literally free. Some jurisdictions charge a small application fee, often in the $50 to $200 range, and a few seek partial reimbursement after the case if your finances improve. Ask the court clerk what, if anything, applies in your county so there is no surprise later.

## 2. The caseload math: the number that explains almost everything

The American Bar Association's long-standing workload standards recommend a maximum of 150 felony cases or 400 misdemeanor cases per attorney per year. Those numbers are not a suggestion for comfort — they are the ceiling at which a lawyer can still investigate, research, negotiate, and prepare for trial on each file.

Now do the hours. A full-time lawyer works roughly 2,000 hours a year after holidays, court days, and administration. Divide that by caseload:

- **150 felony cases:** about 13 hours per case per year, before trial preparation.
- **400 misdemeanor cases:** about 5 hours per case per year.
- **A public defender carrying 500 cases:** about 4 hours per case.
- **A public defender carrying 900 cases:** barely over 2 hours per case.

Those last two numbers are not hypothetical extremes. Reports from defender offices across the country have documented caseloads well above the ABA standards for years, with some attorneys carrying several hundred cases at once. A lawyer with 2 hours per case cannot interview every witness, visit the scene, review hours of body-camera footage, and research every motion — something has to give, and it is usually investigation and trial preparation, not court appearances.

A private attorney's caseload tells the opposite story. A solo practitioner handling 40 to 80 criminal cases a year has 25 to 50 hours available per case. That gap — 2 hours versus 40 hours — is the single biggest structural difference between the two options, and it explains most of the outcome differences people argue about. It is not about talent. It is about time.

That said, caseload is not destiny. A public defender with a manageable docket in a well-funded office can be excellent, and a private attorney who takes every paying case that walks in can be just as overloaded. Ask either lawyer directly: "How many open cases do you have right now?" The answer matters more than the firm's website.

## 3. The cost math: what a private attorney actually charges

Private criminal defense fees vary enormously by charge, location, and whether the case goes to trial. The ranges below reflect commonly reported market rates in the United States, not a quote for any specific case:

- **Simple misdemeanor (for example, first-offense shoplifting or disorderly conduct):** roughly $1,500 to $5,000 for a plea resolution.
- **Misdemeanor DUI:** roughly $2,500 to $7,500, higher if a trial or an expert is involved.
- **Non-violent felony (for example, drug possession or theft):** roughly $5,000 to $15,000 for a plea, $10,000 to $25,000 or more if it goes to trial.
- **Serious felony (violent crime, major fraud, sex offenses):** $15,000 to $50,000 or more, with complex trials running well beyond that.

On top of the fee come case expenses: investigators ($75 to $150 per hour is common), expert witnesses ($2,000 to $10,000 or more for a report and testimony), independent lab testing, and transcript and filing costs. A private attorney usually bills these separately or builds them into the retainer — ask which, in writing, before you sign.

### Three worked examples

**Example A: Misdemeanor DUI, first offense, no accident.** A private attorney quotes a $4,000 flat fee, plus $500 in costs. Total: $4,500. A public defender costs $0 to $100 in fees. The private advantage here is usually modest: the case turns on the stop, the breath test, and the officer's report, and a public defender handles dozens of identical cases a year. Unless there is a license consequence that threatens your job, such as a commercial driver's license, the $4,500 buys convenience and attention more than a different legal theory.

**Example B: Felony drug possession with a search issue.** A private attorney quotes $12,000 for pretrial work and $8,000 more if the suppression hearing goes to a full evidentiary fight — $20,000 total. A public defender costs nothing but may have 4 to 6 hours to review the search warrant, the body-camera footage, and the lab chain of custody. Here the money buys something concrete: hours spent hunting for the one defect that gets evidence thrown out. If a suppression motion is your whole case, that is where private money has its highest return.

**Example C: Serious felony going to trial.** A private attorney quotes $35,000, plus $8,000 for an investigator and a forensic expert — $43,000 total. A public defender team may have access to in-house investigators and experts, but with far less time to deploy them. If you cannot afford $43,000, this is not a reason to panic: public defenders try serious felonies every week, and a good one with a reasonable caseload is a far better option than going into debt you cannot repay or hiring the cheapest private lawyer you can find. A bad private lawyer is worse than a good public defender at any price.

## 4. The outcome question: what the research actually says

This is where both sides oversell. Private firms advertise wins; defender offices point to studies showing comparable results. The careful version of the evidence looks like this:

- **For routine cases that end in a plea, outcomes are often close.** When the evidence is strong and the charge is standard, the negotiation range is set by the prosecutor's office policy and the sentencing guidelines more than by who sits next to you. A skilled public defender who negotiates with the same prosecutor daily can get the same standard offer a private lawyer gets.
- **For complex cases, the gap widens.** Cases that turn on investigation, expert testimony, novel legal motions, or a long trial reward time and money. That is where a lower caseload and a real investigation budget show up in results.
- **For trials, preparation is the variable, not the label.** Studies that control for charge and criminal history tend to find smaller differences than the marketing suggests, but they also consistently find that defendants with more lawyer time and resources fare better at trial. Time is the mechanism, not magic.

The practical takeaway: do not ask "which type of lawyer wins more?" Ask "what does MY case turn on?" If it turns on a standard negotiation, a public defender is a strong option. If it turns on finding a fact nobody has found yet, hours and experts matter, and you should weigh the cost seriously.

## 5. The hidden difference: investigation and expert budgets

A criminal case is often decided before anyone enters a courtroom, by whoever investigated better. Consider what investigation can mean in practice:

- Interviewing a witness the police never spoke to, or who tells a different story off the record.
- Pulling surveillance video from a nearby business before it is overwritten, often within 30 days.
- Hiring an independent lab to retest a sample, or a forensic expert to challenge a breath-test machine's calibration.
- Reconstructing a crash or a timeline with records the prosecution did not collect.

A public defender's office usually has investigators on staff and a process for requesting experts, but approval takes time and budgets are tight. A private attorney can hire an investigator tomorrow and pay an expert next week. That speed matters when video gets deleted or a witness's memory fades. If your case has a fact that only investigation can uncover — an alibi, a missing camera, a faulty test — ask both options directly: "Who will investigate this, and when will they start?" The answer, not the fee, is the decision.

## 6. When paying for private counsel is usually worth it

Private representation tends to pay for itself most clearly in these situations:

1. **Your job, license, or immigration status is on the line.** A conviction that costs a professional license, a commercial driver's license, or a visa is worth far more than the fee difference. See also our guide on [choosing an immigration attorney](/blog/how-to-choose-immigration-attorney-avoid-notario-scam) if status is at stake.
2. **The case turns on a search, a confession, or a lab result.** Suppression motions and expert challenges are time-intensive, and time is what you are buying.
3. **You face a serious felony with a long potential sentence.** The stakes justify the spend if you can afford it without ruinous debt.
4. **You need a trial, and the case is fact-heavy.** Multiple witnesses, technical evidence, or a long timeline all reward preparation hours.
5. **You were denied a public defender but still cannot comfortably afford a big fee.** A mid-priced attorney who takes fewer cases can beat both extremes: cheaper than the big firm, more available than an overloaded office.

If none of these apply — a straightforward misdemeanor, a standard plea range, no collateral consequence — a public defender is often the rational choice, and spending $5,000 to get the same offer is money better kept.

## 7. When a public defender is the stronger choice

Public defenders are underestimated for specific, structural reasons:

- **Daily courtroom relationships.** A defender who appears before the same judge and negotiates with the same prosecutor every day knows what offer is actually available, which arguments move that prosecutor, and which judge cares about what. An out-of-town private attorney starts from zero.
- **Volume experience on your exact charge.** A defender may handle 200 DUI or drug cases a year. That repetition builds pattern recognition: which lab reports have recurring errors, which officers have credibility problems, which diversion programs actually accept people.
- **No financial incentive to prolong your case.** A flat-fee private lawyer is paid the same whether your case ends in a month or a year; an hourly one is paid more the longer it runs. Neither is a reason to distrust a private lawyer, but a public defender's incentives are simpler: resolve the case well and move on.
- **Team resources.** Larger defender offices have social workers, mitigation specialists, and investigators who build sentencing presentations — school records, treatment history, employment letters — that a solo private lawyer may not staff.

The weakness to watch for is not the title on the door, it is the caseload. If your defender has 400 open files and returns calls weeks later, that is a capacity problem, and it is worth raising with the court if it affects your defense.

## 8. How to decide, and what to do if you chose wrong

Use this sequence rather than deciding on price alone:

1. **Find out if you qualify for appointed counsel, and what the fee is.** Apply early — waiting until your first court date wastes weeks.
2. **Ask both options the same three questions:** How many open cases do you carry? Who will investigate my case, and when? What is the realistic range of outcomes for a charge like mine, and what would change it? Compare the answers, not the brochures.
3. **Match the spend to the stakes.** If the worst realistic outcome is a fine and a class, do not borrow $10,000. If the worst outcome is years in prison or losing your livelihood, treat the fee as an investment and get a second quote.
4. **Read the fee agreement before signing.** Flat fee or hourly, what triggers extra charges, what happens to unused retainer funds, and who pays for experts — all of it in writing. Our guide to [retainer clauses worth reading](/blog/retainer-agreement-clauses-to-read-before-signing) applies to criminal cases too.
5. **Know you can switch, but switching has a cost.** Changing lawyers mid-case means the new lawyer must learn the file from scratch, and courts do not always grant delays for it. If communication has broken down or preparation is clearly not happening, switching is legitimate — our guide to [firing and switching lawyers mid-case](/blog/how-to-fire-your-lawyer-switch-attorneys-mid-case) walks through the mechanics — but do it early, not on the eve of trial.

## The bottom line

A public defender is not a second-class lawyer, and a private attorney is not a guaranteed win. The difference is measurable: hours per case, investigation budget, and how much your particular charge rewards those two things. A $0 lawyer with 4 hours for your case and a $20,000 lawyer with 40 hours are not the same product, but neither is automatically the right buy — it depends on what your case turns on and what the stakes are.

Get the caseload number. Get the investigation plan. Get the fee in writing. Then choose based on your case, not on a billboard or a slogan.
