Filing for bankruptcy costs money — which feels backward when you're filing *because* you have no money. But the fee structure is actually one of the more transparent corners of the legal world, and the math behind it is worth understanding before you sign anything.

Nationally, a Chapter 7 bankruptcy attorney costs **$1,200–$2,500** (average around $1,700) and a Chapter 13 attorney costs **$2,000–$5,000** (average around $3,500). Those are attorney fees only. Court filing fees, credit counseling, and trustee costs sit on top. By the time a typical Chapter 7 filer walks out of their last hearing, the all-in cost lands somewhere between **$1,700 and $3,200**. For Chapter 13, it's **$2,600 to $6,000+**, usually paid off over 3–5 years inside the repayment plan.

This guide breaks down what you're actually paying for, why fees swing by $1,000+ depending on your zip code and your facts, and how to run the numbers on whether a lawyer pays for itself in your case.

## Why Bankruptcy Fees Are Structurally Different From Other Legal Fees

Most lawyers bill by the hour. Bankruptcy lawyers mostly don't — at least not for Chapter 7. Here's why:

Bankruptcy has **two fixed, knowable documents**: the petition and the schedules. An experienced attorney can estimate, from a 30-minute consultation, exactly how many hours your case will take. A no-asset Chapter 7 with a W-2 income and $30,000 in credit card debt is a 10–15 hour case, almost identical to the last one. Predictable work gets flat-fee pricing.

That's why roughly **80%+ of Chapter 7 consumer cases** are flat-fee. The flat fee typically covers:

- The means test calculation and pre-filing analysis
- Preparing and filing the petition and schedules
- Representing you at the 341 meeting of creditors
- Handling routine creditor objections and exemption claims
- The post-filing debtor education filings

What it usually does *not* cover: adversary proceedings (lawsuits inside the bankruptcy), reaffirmation agreement negotiations on a car loan (sometimes billed separately, ~$300–$500), and converting to a different chapter mid-case.

Chapter 13 is different. It's a 3–5 year case with ongoing court appearances, plan amendments, and motion practice. Many districts use **"no-look" fees** — a presumptive reasonable fee the court approves without itemized billing. These typically run **$3,000–$5,000** depending on the district, with a portion paid upfront (often $500–$1,500) and the rest paid through the repayment plan itself.

## The Full Cost Stack: Attorney Fees Are Only Part of It

One of the most common surprises is that the attorney quote isn't the total. Here's the complete stack for a 2026 filing:

| Cost item | Chapter 7 | Chapter 13 |
|---|---|---|
| Attorney flat fee | $1,200–$2,500 | $2,000–$5,000 |
| Court filing fee (federal, set by statute) | $338 | $313 |
| Credit counseling course (pre-filing, required) | $15–$50 | $15–$50 |
| Debtor education course (post-filing, required) | $15–$50 | $15–$50 |
| Credit report pull (some attorneys charge this) | $0–$75 | $0–$75 |
| **Typical all-in range** | **$1,700–$3,200** | **$2,600–$6,000+** |

The filing fees ($338 / $313) are set by Congress and are the same in every state — the one part of the bill with zero geographic variance. The counseling and education courses are federal requirements; you can take them online from any DOJ-approved provider, and price-shopping between providers can save you $30–$60. It's small money, but it's *your* small money.

### Can you get the filing fee waived?

For Chapter 7 only: yes, possibly. If your household income is below **150% of the federal poverty line**, you can apply for a fee waiver (Official Form 103B). For a household of two in 2026, 150% of the poverty line is roughly **$31,700/year** — about $2,640/month. Courts grant these routinely when the numbers check out, saving you the full $338. Chapter 13 has no waiver — it offers installment payment of the filing fee instead (up to 4 installments within 120 days).

## The Geography Premium: Why the Same Case Costs $1,200 in Ohio and $2,400 in California

Attorney fees vary more by location than by case complexity. Here's the pattern:

- **Low-cost markets** (much of the Midwest and South: Ohio, Indiana, Mississippi, Alabama): simple Chapter 7 runs **$1,000–$1,500**. Some rural attorneys still file no-asset cases for under $1,000.
- **Mid-cost markets** (Texas, Florida, Arizona, most suburbs): **$1,500–$2,000** is the modal quote for Chapter 7.
- **High-cost markets** (coastal metros — New York City, Los Angeles, San Francisco, Seattle, Washington D.C.): **$2,000–$2,800** for the same no-asset Chapter 7. Chapter 13 no-look fees in these districts often sit at the top of the $4,000–$5,000 band.

Why? Overhead, mostly — office rent and staff cost more in Manhattan than in Memphis, and local bar custom sets expectations. But here's the nuance: **paying the local rate is usually worth it**. A bankruptcy attorney who files 200 cases a year in your district knows your trustee's quirks, your judge's document preferences, and exactly which exemptions fly. A cheaper out-of-area attorney who has to learn your district on your case is a false economy.

One important exception to the geography rule: some districts publish **fee guidelines**. If your local bankruptcy court's no-look fee for Chapter 13 is $4,500, nearly every competent attorney in that district will quote $4,500. The number is public, so don't overpay above it — and be suspicious of anyone quoting dramatically below it, because Chapter 13 cases at cut-rate fees tend to get cut-rate attention over a five-year case.

## The Complexity Multipliers: When Your Case Costs More

Flat fees assume a *typical* case. These facts push you above the typical quote:

1. **You own a business or have self-employment income.** Business debt, inventory, and receivables complicate the schedules. Expect **+$500–$1,500** on Chapter 7.
2. **You have non-exempt assets.** If the trustee might sell something — a second car, valuable collectibles, a tax refund over the exemption — the attorney's exemption-planning work increases. Expect **+$300–$800**.
3. **You need to stop a foreclosure or repossession this week.** Emergency filings (bare-bones petition filed to trigger the automatic stay, schedules completed in 14 days) carry rush premiums of **+$300–$700**.
4. **You've filed before.** Prior filings within 8 years (Chapter 7) or 2–6 years (Chapter 13) trigger automatic-stay complications requiring a motion to extend or impose the stay: **+$500–$1,000** in motion practice.
5. **Creditor challenges are likely.** Recent luxury purchases, cash advances over $1,000 within 70 days of filing, or a creditor threatening an adversary proceeding for fraud: **+$1,000–$3,000+**, sometimes billed hourly at $250–$400/hr.
6. **Means test complications.** Above-median income with high allowed expenses (mortgage arrears, childcare, health costs) means a full Form 122A-2 analysis instead of a pass on the first screen: **+$200–$500**.

A realistic "complex but not litigated" Chapter 7 — self-employed, one non-exempt asset, above-median income — lands around **$2,500–$3,500** all-in. Still flat-fee in most markets; just a bigger flat fee.

## Worked Example 1: The Typical Chapter 7 (Maria, $38,000 in Debt)

Maria is a retail manager in Columbus, Ohio, earning $42,000/year. She has $31,000 in credit card debt, $7,000 in medical debt, a car worth $8,000 (owed $6,500), and rents her apartment. No assets beyond household goods and the car.

**Her cost stack:**

- Attorney flat fee: $1,400 (Columbus market rate for a clean no-asset case)
- Filing fee: $338
- Credit counseling: $25 (online provider)
- Debtor education: $25
- **Total: $1,788**

**Her outcome:** $38,000 in unsecured debt discharged in about 4 months. She keeps the car (Ohio's motor vehicle exemption covers her $1,500 in equity) and everything else.

**The ROI math:** $38,000 discharged ÷ $1,788 cost = **21x return**. Put differently, she's paying 4.7 cents on the dollar to erase the debt. Even if she'd negotiated settlements at 40 cents on the dollar ($15,200), the bankruptcy costs one-eighth of that — before counting the tax bill on forgiven debt outside bankruptcy (cancelled debt is generally taxable income; debt discharged *in* bankruptcy is not — a difference worth thousands on a $38,000 write-down).

## Worked Example 2: The Chapter 13 Payment-Plan Build (James, Behind on the Mortgage)

James is a contractor in Tampa, Florida, earning $68,000/year. He's $14,000 behind on his mortgage, has $22,000 in credit card debt, and owes $9,000 on a truck. He wants to keep the house.

**His cost stack:**

- Attorney fee: $4,200 (Middle District of Florida no-look fee), with **$800 paid upfront** and $3,400 paid through the plan
- Filing fee: $313
- Counseling + education: $50
- **Out-of-pocket day one: ~$1,163. Total cost: ~$4,563**

**His plan math (60 months):** The plan pays the $14,000 mortgage arrears in full over 60 months ($233/month toward arrears, plus the regular mortgage payment resumes), the truck's $9,000 balance (possibly crammed down if the truck is worth less than owed and was bought over 910 days ago), and a percentage of the $22,000 in unsecured debt — for an above-median earner, often 10–30%, so roughly $2,200–$6,600.

His monthly plan payment lands around **$700–$900**, of which the attorney's remaining $3,400 is a small slice (~$57/month). The key insight: **in Chapter 13, most of the attorney fee is financed inside the plan at 0% interest**. James's day-one outlay ($1,163) is *less* than Maria's Chapter 7 total, even though his total fee is nearly triple. That's the structural reason Chapter 13 is accessible to people who couldn't scrape together a Chapter 7 flat fee upfront — and it's exactly why attorneys must be paid partly upfront in Chapter 7 (pre-petition debt, including the attorney's own fee, gets discharged) but can be paid through the plan in Chapter 13.

## Filing Without a Lawyer: The Pro Se Math

You are legally allowed to file bankruptcy yourself. The filing fee is the same $338. So why do roughly **93% of Chapter 7 filers** use an attorney?

The success-rate data is stark. Cases filed with an attorney reach discharge at dramatically higher rates than pro se cases — the gap is widest in Chapter 13, where pro se completion rates are famously dismal (single digits in many districts, versus ~50%+ with counsel). The paperwork looks simple until it isn't: one misclaimed exemption can cost you a $5,000 asset; one missed deadline can get your case dismissed; one wrong answer on the Statement of Financial Affairs can draw a fraud inquiry.

**When pro se can make sense:** a truly simple no-asset Chapter 7, below-median income, no real property, debts all clearly dischargeable, and you're detail-oriented enough to complete 60+ pages of schedules accurately. Even then, many legal aid societies and law school clinics will review your paperwork for free — use them.

**When pro se is reckless:** anything involving a house, a business, recent asset transfers, a pending lawsuit, tax debt, or above-median income. The downside isn't just losing the case — a dismissed bankruptcy still shows on your credit report, and you burned your filing's automatic-stay protection for nothing.

A middle path exists: **limited-scope ("unbundled") services**. Some attorneys will review your self-prepared petition for $300–$600, or handle just the 341 meeting for a flat fee. Not every district allows unbundled bankruptcy work, and not every attorney offers it — but it's worth asking about if the full fee is out of reach.

## Payment Plans and the "File Now, Pay Over Time" Question

"I can't afford a bankruptcy lawyer" is the most common reason people delay filing — while interest and late fees compound monthly. Here's how people actually bridge the gap:

- **Pre-filing payment plans (Chapter 7):** Most Chapter 7 attorneys accept monthly payments *before* filing — $200–$400/month until the fee is paid, then they file. The catch: creditors can keep calling and suing during the payment period, because the automatic stay doesn't exist until filing. Every month of pre-filing payments is a month of continued collections.
- **Post-filing payment through the plan (Chapter 13):** As shown in James's example, the fee rides inside the plan. This is the cleanest financing in consumer bankruptcy.
- **"Bifurcated" Chapter 7 fees:** A newer model where you pay a small amount pre-filing and the rest post-filing under a separate agreement. Courts are split on whether this is permissible — some districts have banned or restricted it. Ask whether your district allows it.
- **Legal aid:** If your income is low enough, legal aid societies handle consumer bankruptcies free. Law school clinics do too. Availability is limited and waitlists are real, but the price is unbeatable.

**The delay-cost calculation** is the one most people skip. Suppose you owe $30,000 at an average 24% APR across cards. That's ~$600/month in interest alone. A three-month delay to save up a $1,500 fee costs you $1,800 in interest — more than the fee itself. If judgments are looming (wage garnishment can take 25% of disposable earnings in most states), delay is actively expensive. Run your own numbers before deciding to wait.

## Red Flags: Fee Quotes That Should Make You Walk Away

Bankruptcy has more than its share of fee games. Watch for:

- **The $499 Chapter 7.** At that price, something is missing — usually the 341 meeting representation, or it's a petition-preparer (not a lawyer) operating illegally. A real attorney cannot profitably file your case for $499.
- **Fees that balloon after the consultation.** The quote was $1,200; the engagement letter says $1,200 "plus $800 for the means test." A flat fee should be flat. Get the quote in writing with the scope defined.
- **Pressure to file immediately, today, right now.** Emergency filings are sometimes genuinely needed (foreclosure sale tomorrow). But "sign today or the price goes up" is a sales tactic, not legal advice.
- **No written fee agreement.** Federal law (11 U.S.C. § 528) *requires* a written contract spelling out the services and fees. No contract = walk away, and consider reporting it.
- **Promises about specific outcomes.** "I'll definitely save your house" or "your student loans will be discharged" — no honest attorney guarantees results. (Student loans, note, are dischargeable only through a separate adversary proceeding proving undue hardship — the Brunner test in most circuits — and it adds $1,500–$4,000+ to the bill.)

## Questions to Ask Before You Hire

Take these to every consultation (most bankruptcy consultations are free, 30–60 minutes):

1. What is the total flat fee, and exactly what does it include and exclude?
2. How much is due before filing, and do you offer pre-filing payment plans?
3. How many consumer cases have you filed in *this* district in the last year? (You want triple digits.)
4. Will *you* attend my 341 meeting, or will a coverage attorney?
5. What happens to the fee if my case gets dismissed or I need to convert chapters?
6. Do you see any red flags in my facts — recent transfers, cash advances, non-exempt assets?

## The Bottom Line

For most filers, the decision isn't really "can I afford a bankruptcy attorney" — it's "can I afford *not* to have one." A $1,700 Chapter 7 fee that discharges $38,000 of debt, stops a garnishment, and avoids a taxable forgiven-debt bill is one of the highest-ROI legal purchases a consumer can make. A $4,200 Chapter 13 fee that saves a house from foreclosure and stretches arrears over five years at zero interest is the same story at a different scale.

Get three quotes. Get them in writing. Pick the attorney who explains the math clearly — because the one who can explain the fee clearly is usually the one who'll explain the case clearly too.
