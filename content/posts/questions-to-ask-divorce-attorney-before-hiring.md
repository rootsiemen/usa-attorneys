A divorce is often the most expensive legal matter a person ever handles — and the most personal. Yet most people hire their [divorce attorney](/divorce-attorney) after a single 30-minute conversation, without knowing what the case will cost, how long it will take, or whether the lawyer's approach even matches their goals. A consultation is not a sales pitch. It is a job interview, and you are the one hiring.

Bring this list to every consultation. Ask the same questions of two or three lawyers, write down the answers, and compare them side by side. The differences will tell you more than any review or referral ever could.

## 1. "How much of your practice is divorce and family law?"

Divorce law is a specialty, and the gap between a dedicated family lawyer and a generalist is wide. Family courts have their own judges, their own unwritten local rules, and their own rhythms — a lawyer who files a divorce once a year simply does not know them the way a daily practitioner does.

Ask for specifics, not slogans:

- What percentage of your caseload is divorce and family law?
- How many divorce cases have you handled in the last two years?
- Do you regularly appear before the family court judge who would hear my case?

A lawyer who knows the local court in [Texas](/attorneys/texas) or [Florida](/attorneys/florida) can tell you how that judge tends to rule on temporary support, how long that court takes to set a trial date, and which local mediators actually settle cases. That local knowledge is worth more than a glossy website.

Also ask whether the lawyer handles cases like yours specifically. A high-conflict custody fight, a business-owner divorce, and an uncontested short marriage are three different jobs. If your case involves a business, a military pension, or a special-needs child, say so upfront and ask for directly comparable experience.

## 2. "What will this cost — in full, not just the retainer?"

This is the question that matters most, and the one most often answered vaguely. A retainer is a deposit, not a price. According to our breakdown of [how much a divorce attorney costs in 2026](/blog/how-much-does-divorce-attorney-cost-2026), an uncontested divorce may run $1,500 to $3,500, a moderately contested case $5,000 to $15,000, and a high-conflict case with custody and property disputes $20,000 to $50,000 or more. Your lawyer should be able to place your case in that range — and explain why.

Get these answers in writing:

- What is the hourly rate, and does it differ for the lead attorney, associates, and paralegals?
- How large is the retainer, and is it refundable or evergreen — meaning you must top it up when it drops below a threshold?
- What is billed besides attorney time: filing fees, process servers, expert witnesses, appraisals, forensic accountants?
- What happens if the retainer runs out mid-case? Do you stop work, or bill monthly?
- Do you offer flat-fee pricing for any part of the case, such as an uncontested filing?

Do the math on the spot. A $5,000 retainer at $350 per hour buys roughly 14 hours of work. A contested case routinely takes 40 to 100 hours. If the retainer number sounds small and the hourly rate sounds high, ask directly: "At your rate, how many hours does a case like mine usually take?" An honest lawyer gives you a range and the factors that push it up or down. A lawyer who says "don't worry about cost" is the one to worry about. Read the [retainer agreement clauses to check before signing](/blog/retainer-agreement-clauses-to-read-before-signing) before you put your name on anything.

## 3. "What is your approach — settlement first, or trial-ready from day one?"

Every divorce lawyer claims to be a "strong advocate." That phrase tells you nothing. What you need is their actual philosophy, because it shapes everything: how they write the first letter to your spouse's lawyer, whether they push mediation early, and how much of your money goes to courtroom fights.

Ask:

- What percentage of your cases settle before trial?
- When do you recommend mediation, and when do you recommend filing contested motions?
- How do you handle a spouse who refuses to negotiate?

The numbers matter here. A lawyer who settles 90 percent of cases may be a skilled negotiator — or may be someone who pressures clients to accept bad deals to avoid trial. A lawyer who tries 40 percent of cases may be a fighter — or may be someone whose clients pay trial prices for cases that should have settled. Neither extreme is automatically right. What matters is whether the lawyer can explain, with examples, when each path made sense for a client in a situation like yours.

Our comparison of [divorce mediation vs. litigation costs](/blog/divorce-mediation-vs-litigation-cost-math) shows the stakes: mediation often costs $3,000 to $8,000 total, while a litigated divorce can cost five to ten times that. But mediation only works when both sides disclose finances honestly and negotiate in good faith. If your spouse is hiding assets or using delay as a weapon, a settlement-first lawyer who never files a motion is the wrong hire. Ask how the lawyer handles that exact scenario.

## 4. "How do you handle custody and parenting time?"

If you have children, this question outranks every other. Custody disputes are where divorce cases become most expensive and most emotional, and where a lawyer's judgment matters most.

Ask:

- What custody arrangements do you most often see ordered in this court?
- How do you build a parenting plan — what does a realistic schedule look like for a family like mine?
- When do you recommend a custody evaluation, and what does one cost? (Evaluations commonly run $2,500 to $10,000.)
- How do you handle relocation disputes, school decisions, and medical decisions?

Listen for whether the lawyer talks about your children's needs or only about "winning." A lawyer who promises you sole custody before seeing a single fact of your case is selling, not advising. A good lawyer explains the standard the court applies — the best interests of the child — and what evidence actually moves that needle: stability, involvement, school records, and documented caregiving history, not character attacks on your spouse.

Also ask about the lawyer's own view of shared parenting. In many states, courts now start from a presumption of shared time. If you want primary custody, ask what evidence you would need. If your spouse wants primary custody, ask how the lawyer would defend your parenting time. Either way, you want a concrete answer, not a pep talk.

## 5. "How will you divide our property, debts, and retirement accounts?"

Property division is where vague answers cost real money. A house, two retirement accounts, a small business, and $40,000 in credit card debt can be divided a dozen ways, and the differences are worth tens of thousands of dollars.

Ask:

- Is my state a community-property state or an equitable-distribution state, and what does that actually change for us?
- How are retirement accounts divided — is a Qualified Domestic Relations Order (QDRO) needed, and who pays the $500 to $1,500 drafting fee?
- How is the family home handled — buyout, sale, or deferred sale? Our breakdown of [who keeps the house in a divorce](/blog/who-keeps-the-house-in-divorce-buyout-vs-sell-math) shows how a buyout at the wrong valuation can cost the buying spouse $20,000 or more.
- What happens to debts taken on during the marriage, including debts in only one spouse's name?
- How do you value a business, and when is a business valuation expert worth the $5,000 to $15,000 fee?

Worked example: suppose a couple owns a home worth $400,000 with a $250,000 mortgage — $150,000 in equity. If one spouse buys out the other, the buyout is not automatically $75,000. The buying spouse typically refinances, pays closing costs of $5,000 to $10,000, and may owe taxes or adjustments for deferred maintenance. A lawyer who walks you through that arithmetic in the consultation is a lawyer who will protect you in the settlement. A lawyer who waves it away with "we'll figure it out later" is not.

Also ask about hidden-asset discovery. If you suspect undisclosed accounts, ask what tools the lawyer uses — subpoenas, forensic accountants, discovery requests — and what each costs. Finding a hidden $30,000 account is only a win if the search did not cost $25,000.

## 6. "How long will my divorce take — and what could slow it down?"

Nobody can give you an exact date, but any experienced divorce lawyer can give you a realistic range for your court and your fact pattern. Our [month-by-month divorce timeline](/blog/how-long-does-divorce-take-timeline-month-by-month) shows the spread: an uncontested divorce may finish in 2 to 4 months, a moderately contested case in 6 to 12 months, and a high-conflict case in 18 months or more.

Ask:

- What is the typical timeline in this court, from filing to final decree, for a case like mine?
- What are the mandatory waiting periods in my state? (Some states require 60 days, others 6 months or a year of separation before a divorce can be finalized.)
- What slows cases down most often in your experience — court backlogs, discovery disputes, custody evaluations, or a spouse who will not respond?
- What can I do to keep the case moving?

Be suspicious of two answers: "a few weeks" for a contested case, and a flat refusal to estimate at all. The first is a sales line. The second may mean the lawyer has not handled enough local cases to know. A useful answer sounds like: "Uncontested cases here run about 3 months. With custody and property disputes, I would plan on 9 to 14 months, and a custody evaluation would add 3 to 4 months."

Also ask about temporary orders. While the divorce is pending, who pays the mortgage, who lives in the house, and what is the temporary support amount? Temporary orders are often decided within 30 to 60 days of filing and can set the pattern for the final settlement — so ask how quickly the lawyer moves on them.

## 7. "Who will actually work on my case, and how do I reach you?"

In many firms, you meet the senior partner at the consultation and never see them again. Your file goes to an associate, your calls go to a paralegal, and you find out at the first hearing. That is not always a bad arrangement — associates bill at lower rates — but you deserve to know it before you pay.

Ask:

- Will you personally handle my hearings, or will an associate?
- Who prepares my motions and settlement drafts?
- How quickly do you return calls and emails? (A 24- to 48-hour response standard is reasonable; same-day is not always realistic during trial weeks.)
- Do you communicate by phone, email, or a client portal, and how often will I get case updates?
- What happens if you are on vacation or in trial when something urgent comes up?

Communication failures are the number one complaint clients make about divorce lawyers — ahead of fees, ahead of outcomes. A lawyer who sets a clear communication policy in the consultation is far less likely to leave you guessing later. If the lawyer seems annoyed by these questions, that is your answer.

## 8. "What do you need from me, and what should I stop doing right now?"

A divorce lawyer should give you a short, specific homework list at the first meeting — and a short list of things to avoid. Bring your financial documents to the consultation if you can; our [divorce consultation document checklist](/blog/divorce-attorney-consultation-document-checklist) covers tax returns, pay stubs, bank and retirement statements, mortgage documents, and debt statements.

Ask:

- What documents do you need first, and what can wait?
- Should I open a separate bank account, and how do I handle joint accounts and joint credit cards?
- What should I not post on social media, and what should I not say to my spouse in writing?
- Can I move out of the house, and what are the consequences if I do?
- Should I change beneficiaries, passwords, or direct deposits — and what must I not touch until the court says so?

The answers here reveal practical judgment. Moving out, for example, can affect custody arguments and who pays the mortgage. Closing a joint account can look like hiding assets. A good lawyer gives you clear, case-specific guidance on these moves in the first meeting, not a generic "be careful."

## 9. "What are the red flags I should watch for — in you, and in my spouse's lawyer?"

Ask the lawyer to describe their own profession's warning signs. The answers are revealing, because an honest lawyer will name them plainly:

- **Guaranteed outcomes.** No lawyer can guarantee custody, a dollar amount, or a timeline. Guarantees are marketing.
- **No written fee agreement.** If fees are not in writing, they are not agreed.
- **Pressure to sign today.** A good lawyer wants you to compare options. High-pressure tactics at a consultation predict high-pressure tactics with your settlement.
- **Trash-talking your spouse in the first meeting.** A lawyer who fuels anger in hour one is often a lawyer who profits from conflict in month six.
- **No questions about your goals.** If the lawyer never asks what outcome matters most to you — the kids, the house, the business, a fast resolution — they are running their playbook, not your case.

Also ask how the lawyer handles an aggressive opposing counsel. Some lawyers match aggression with aggression, which doubles the bill. Others stay procedural and let the record speak. Neither style is universally right, but you should know which one you are hiring.

## 10. "What happens if I want to change lawyers later?"

Nobody plans to fire their lawyer, but it happens often in divorce — usually over communication or cost. Ask before you hire, not after you are frustrated:

- If I switch lawyers, what happens to my unused retainer?
- Will you cooperate with a new lawyer and transfer my file promptly?
- What does your fee agreement say about withdrawal?

A lawyer who answers this calmly and clearly is a lawyer confident in their own work. A lawyer who gets defensive about the question is telling you something too.

## 11. "Can you give me references or recent comparable outcomes?"

Attorney advertising rules vary by state, and many lawyers cannot share client testimonials or specific results. But you can still ask:

- Can you describe, without names, two or three recent cases similar to mine and how they resolved?
- Are there public reviews or bar-association records I can check?
- Have you ever faced bar discipline? (You can also verify this yourself through your state bar's website.)

Comparable outcomes are more useful than star ratings. A lawyer who settled a business-owner divorce with a clean buyout structure last year is more relevant to you than a lawyer with fifty five-star reviews for uncontested filings.

## 12. "What is my realistic best case, worst case, and most likely outcome?"

This is the question that separates advisors from salespeople. Ask it plainly, and expect three answers, not one:

- Best case: what happens if the evidence breaks your way and the court agrees with your positions?
- Worst case: what happens if it does not?
- Most likely: where do cases like yours usually land in this court?

A lawyer who will only discuss the best case is not preparing you — they are recruiting you. A lawyer who walks you through the worst case is the one you want beside you if it arrives. Write the three scenarios down. They become your decision framework for every settlement offer that follows.

## 13. "How do you charge for the small stuff?"

The big numbers get attention, but the small billing practices add up. Ask:

- What is the minimum billing increment — 6 minutes, 15 minutes, or a full hour?
- Do you bill for short emails and phone calls?
- Do you charge for photocopies, postage, and travel time?
- Will I get itemized monthly statements?

The difference between 6-minute and 15-minute billing increments is real: a 4-minute phone call billed at 15 minutes costs you nearly four times the actual time. Over a year-long case, that gap can reach thousands of dollars. Itemized statements are non-negotiable — they are how you catch errors and control costs.

## 14. "What would you do differently if you were in my position?"

End the consultation with this question. It forces the lawyer out of sales mode and into advisor mode. The best answers are specific: "I would file for temporary support this week, before your spouse closes the joint account." "I would not move out until we have a temporary custody order." "I would take the mediation offer seriously, because this judge rarely awards what you are asking for at trial."

Compare that answer across the lawyers you interview. The one whose advice sounds like it was tailored to your facts — not recited from a brochure — is usually the right hire.

## Your consultation scorecard

After each consultation, score the lawyer on five points, 1 to 5:

1. **Experience fit** — comparable cases, local court knowledge.
2. **Cost clarity** — written fees, realistic range, billing transparency.
3. **Strategy fit** — an approach that matches your goals, not just their style.
4. **Communication** — clear response standards, a named contact, regular updates.
5. **Honesty** — a real worst case, real risks, no guarantees.

A lawyer who scores 4 or higher across the board is rare. A lawyer who scores 2 on cost clarity or honesty should not be hired no matter how impressive the office is. Divorce is expensive enough without paying twice — once for the lawyer, and once for the mistakes a bad hire creates.

Bring this list, take notes, and hire slowly. The cheapest consultation is free. The most expensive mistake is hiring the wrong lawyer in a hurry.
