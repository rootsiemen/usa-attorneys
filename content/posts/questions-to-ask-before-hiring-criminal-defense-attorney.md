Getting arrested or charged is one of the scariest moments of your life — and the [criminal defense attorney](/criminal-defense-attorney) you hire in the days after may matter more to your future than any other decision you make. The problem: most people hire the first lawyer who answers the phone, because they don't know what to ask.

Use this list at your free consultation. The answers separate experienced, genuinely prepared defense lawyers from everyone else.

## 1. "How much of your practice is criminal defense — and have you handled my specific charge?"

Criminal law is a world of its own, with its own procedures, evidence rules, and prosecutors. A general practitioner who "also does criminal" is not the same as a lawyer whose entire practice is defending people accused of crimes.

Be specific. Ask:

- What percentage of your cases are criminal defense?
- How many cases like mine — DUI, drug possession, assault, theft, domestic violence — have you handled in the last two years?
- Do you regularly practice in the court where my case will be heard?

A lawyer who knows the local prosecutors and judges in [Texas](/attorneys/texas) or [Florida](/attorneys/florida) has a practical edge no textbook can give: they know which prosecutors negotiate, which judges punish delay, and how cases like yours usually resolve in that courtroom.

## 2. "What are the realistic outcomes for a case like mine?"

No honest attorney will promise you a dismissal on day one. What they *can* do is explain the realistic range of outcomes — dismissal, diversion, plea to a lesser charge, acquittal at trial, conviction — and how often they see each in cases like yours.

Red flags to watch for:

- **Guaranteed results.** A lawyer who guarantees you will win is selling you, not advising you. Outcomes depend on evidence, the prosecutor, and the judge — nobody controls all three.
- **No explanation of the worst case.** If a lawyer won't tell you the maximum penalty you face, they're avoiding the conversation you most need.
- **Copy-paste answers.** If the "strategy" sounds identical for a DUI and an assault charge, it isn't a strategy.

## 3. "Who will actually handle my case?"

In many firms, you meet the senior partner at the consultation and never see them again — your case gets passed to a junior associate. That's not always bad, but you deserve to know upfront.

Ask who will appear at your hearings, who prepares your motions, and who you call with urgent questions. If someone else does the work, ask to meet that person before you sign.

## 4. "What is your fee structure — and what does it cover?"

Criminal defense fees are usually flat fees for misdemeanors and either flat fees or hourly billing for felonies. But "flat fee" can mean very different things. Get specifics:

- Does the flat fee cover trial, or only pre-trial work?
- What happens if the case is dismissed early — is any fee refunded?
- Are there extra costs: investigators, expert witnesses, court fees, bail-bond coordination?
- What are the payment options? Many criminal defense lawyers offer payment plans because clients often hire under financial stress.

A written fee agreement is non-negotiable. If a lawyer resists putting fees in writing, walk away.

## What criminal defense actually costs: fee ranges by charge

Nobody publishes a price list, but after talking to defense lawyers across the country, here's what people actually pay in 2026 — and what separates the low end from the high end.

| Charge level | Typical flat fee range | What moves it up |
|---|---|---|
| First DUI, no accident | $2,500 – $7,500 | Prior DUIs, high BAC, accident with injuries |
| Misdemeanor (theft, simple assault, drug possession) | $2,000 – $8,000 | Trial vs. plea, criminal history |
| Felony drug charges | $8,000 – $25,000 | Drug quantity, trafficking vs. possession, federal vs. state |
| Violent felonies (aggravated assault, robbery) | $15,000 – $50,000+ | Trial length, expert witnesses, victim testimony |
| Federal charges | $25,000 – $100,000+ | Complexity, mandatory minimums, multi-defendant cases |
| Homicide / serious sex offenses | $50,000 – $250,000+ | Months of investigation, forensics, weeks of trial |

The DUI row alone has enough moving parts — DMV hearings, interlock costs, license reinstatement — that we break out [how much a DUI lawyer costs](/blog/how-much-does-dui-attorney-cost-2026) separately, line by line.

Two things most people don't realize: **going to trial roughly doubles or triples the fee** versus a plea, because trial means weeks of preparation, motions, jury selection, and days in court. And **the cheapest lawyer is often the most expensive** — a $2,500 lawyer who pleads you out on day one to a charge carrying a 5-year collateral record can cost you hundreds of thousands in lifetime earnings. Price-shop, but price-shop among lawyers you'd actually trust with your freedom.

Payment plans are standard in criminal defense precisely because arrests don't wait for payday. Most lawyers accept a down payment of 30–50% with monthly installments. Get the payment schedule in the written agreement — verbal "we'll work something out" deals are how fee disputes start.

## 5. "What is your trial record — and do you take cases to trial?"

Most criminal cases settle through plea deals, but you want a lawyer the prosecutor *believes* will go to trial. Prosecutors offer better deals to lawyers with a track record of winning in front of a jury, because the threat of trial is what creates leverage.

Ask directly: "How many jury trials have you done in the last two years, and what were the results?" A lawyer who never tries cases negotiates from weakness.

## 6. "How will you investigate and challenge the evidence against me?"

This question reveals whether the lawyer thinks like a defender or a form-filler. Strong answers mention:

- Reviewing police reports, body-cam footage, and 911 calls for inconsistencies
- Checking whether the stop, search, or arrest was legal — illegal searches can get evidence thrown out entirely
- Interviewing witnesses before their memories fade or the prosecution locks them in
- Hiring experts (forensic, toxicology, accident reconstruction) when the evidence needs one

Weak answers sound like "I'll review your file and negotiate the best deal." That's not defense work — that's order-taking.

## 7. "How will we communicate, and how fast will you respond?"

Criminal cases move on deadlines: arraignments, discovery cutoffs, motion filing dates. A lawyer you can't reach is a liability.

Establish expectations now:

- Direct phone number or messaging — not just a receptionist
- Response time commitment (24 hours is reasonable; same-day is better for urgent matters)
- How you'll get updates: scheduled calls, email summaries, or only when something happens?

Also ask about after-hours availability. Arrests don't happen on a schedule, and neither do bail hearings.

## 8. "What do you need from me right now?"

This question does two things: it shows the lawyer whether you'll be a cooperative client, and it shows you whether the lawyer is organized enough to know what matters early.

In the first days of a case, a good lawyer typically needs:

- The charging documents or arrest paperwork
- A written timeline of exactly what happened, while it's fresh
- Names and contact info for any witnesses
- Photos, videos, or messages relevant to the incident
- A list of anything you said to police — because anything you said can surface later

The flip side, which your lawyer should also tell you: **do not discuss your case with anyone except your lawyer.** No social media posts, no texts to friends about what happened, no "explaining your side" to anyone. Anything you say can become evidence.

## 9. "Can you explain my charges and penalties in plain language?"

The statute language on your paperwork is deliberately confusing. A good lawyer translates it immediately:

- Exactly what the charge means and what the prosecution must prove
- The maximum penalties — jail or prison time, fines, probation terms
- The collateral consequences people forget: loss of a professional license, immigration consequences, gun rights, voting rights, background-check impact on employment

This matters enormously for certain charges. A misdemeanor domestic-violence conviction, for example, can trigger federal firearm restrictions. An immigration attorney may need to be consulted alongside your criminal defense lawyer if you aren't a U.S. citizen — [immigration consequences](/immigration-attorney) of criminal charges can include deportation proceedings even for long-time residents.

## 10. "What will you do at my first court appearance?"

The arraignment — your first court date — sets the tone for everything. Ask the lawyer what happens there:

- Will they enter a not-guilty plea on your behalf?
- Can they argue for lower bail or release on your own recognizance?
- How soon can they get your discovery (the prosecution's evidence)?

A lawyer who has a concrete plan for day one is a lawyer who stays ahead of the case instead of chasing it.

## 11. "What are my alternatives to a guilty plea?"

Diversion programs, deferred adjudication, pretrial intervention, drug court, veteran's court — many jurisdictions offer paths that avoid a conviction entirely for eligible defendants. But eligibility windows are narrow and some require applying before your first court date.

Ask specifically:

- Does your jurisdiction have diversion or deferred-prosecution options for my charge?
- Am I eligible, and what would I have to complete?
- What happens to my record if I complete the program?

First-time offenders especially should press on this point. A completed diversion program can mean no conviction on your record — a life-changing difference from a plea that follows you through every background check.

## 12. "Can you give me references or reviews from past clients?"

Online reviews are a starting point, but ask for more: "Can I speak with a past client who faced a similar charge?" Lawyers with satisfied clients are usually happy to connect you — with the client's permission.

Also verify credentials independently:

- Check your state's bar association for the lawyer's license status and any disciplinary history
- Look up recent case results — not cherry-picked "big wins" on their website, but their actual record
- Be skeptical of "top 100" awards that lawyers pay to display

## How to use your free consultation

Almost every [criminal defense attorney](/criminal-defense-attorney) offers a free initial consultation. Treat it as a job interview — because it is one. You're hiring someone to protect your freedom.

Bring your paperwork, write down these twelve questions, and take notes. Then sleep on it if you can. The urgency of a criminal charge is real, but hiring the wrong lawyer in a panic is one of the most common regrets defendants have.
