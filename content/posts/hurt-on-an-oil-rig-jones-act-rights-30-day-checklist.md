An oil rig is not a factory floor with an ocean view. It is a moving steel island where a dropped wrench can kill, a crane swing can crush a leg, and the nearest hospital is a helicopter ride away. When a rig worker gets hurt, the question is not just "how bad is it" — it is which law pays for it. And **oil rig injury compensation** works nothing like a normal workers' comp claim, because for most rig workers the Jones Act replaces workers' comp entirely, and it pays more — if you protect the claim in the first 30 days.

Here is the number that frames everything else: a roughneck earning $1,500 a week who misses six months of work loses $39,000 in wages alone, before a single medical bill. Under the Jones Act, that lost income is recoverable in full — past and future — plus medical costs, pain and suffering, and, in the right case, punitive damages. Under ordinary workers' comp, most of that would be capped or gone. The difference between those two outcomes is decided by what you do, say, and sign in the month after the injury. This checklist runs in order.

## The first 24 hours: report it, treat it, write it down

The clock on your claim starts the moment the injury happens, not the day you decide to do something about it. Three things matter in the first day, and none of them can be done later.

**Report the injury in writing.** Tell your supervisor, then file a written incident report before your shift ends — an email or a signed form, not just a conversation. Offshore operators keep meticulous incident logs; if your injury never enters that log, the company can later argue it happened off the rig, or never happened at all. Get a copy or a photo of the report before you leave the rig.

**Get medical treatment and say how it happened.** Tell the medic — and every doctor after — exactly what happened on the rig, in the same words, every time. Inconsistent medical records are the single most common weapon used against injured seamen: a note that says "back pain" with no mention of the rig becomes "a pre-existing condition" in the insurer's file. If the company sends you to its own doctor, you still have the right to see a doctor of your own choosing. Use it. A company doctor's first job is getting you back to work; your doctor's first job is getting you well.

**Write down the names.** Before memories fade — and before the crew rotates off the rig — note the names of everyone who saw the accident, the equipment involved, the weather and sea conditions, and the exact time. Crews change every two to three weeks. A witness who has flown home to another state is a witness your lawyer may never find.

## Are you a Jones Act seaman? The 30% test decides everything

The Jones Act, passed in 1920, lets a "seaman" sue their employer for negligence — a right most land-based workers do not have. But not every rig worker is a seaman, and the test is specific. The Supreme Court settled the framework in *Stewart v. Dutra Construction Co.* (2005), and it comes down to two questions.

**Do you spend at least 30% of your time in service of a vessel in navigation?** "Vessel" is broader than it sounds. A jack-up rig that moves from location to location counts as a vessel. A drillship counts. A supply boat, a crew boat, a tug, a barge — all count. What usually does *not* count is a fixed platform bolted to the seabed: workers on fixed platforms are generally covered by the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act (LHWCA) instead, or by state workers' comp extended offshore. Almost every rig worker who works on a mobile rig clears the 30% threshold easily — the rig is where they live and work for weeks at a time.

**Does your work contribute to the function of the vessel?** You do not need to be a deckhand. Cooks, mechanics, crane operators, roustabouts, and medics have all qualified — the test is whether your work helps the vessel do its job, not whether you steer it.

Why this matters: a Jones Act seaman can recover full damages, including pain and suffering and future lost earnings, and can sue for negligence under a much lower burden of proof than a normal lawsuit — even slight employer negligence can be enough. An LHWCA worker gets scheduled benefits and medical care, but not pain and suffering, and not a negligence lawsuit against the employer. Getting the classification right in week one changes the value of the case by multiples, not percentages.

If you work on a fixed platform, do not assume you have no claim. Third-party claims — against the equipment manufacturer, a contractor, or the platform owner — often exist alongside LHWCA benefits, and they can recover the damages comp cannot.

## Maintenance and cure: the money you are owed while you recover

This is the part of maritime law most injured rig workers never hear about until it is too late — and it is money you are owed regardless of whose fault the accident was. Under general maritime law, your employer must pay two things until you reach maximum medical improvement (MMI), the point where doctors say you are as healed as you are going to get:

**Maintenance** is a daily living allowance — money for room and board while you cannot work. The typical rate paid by operators runs **$30 to $40 per day**, and many employers pay at the low end unless challenged. Over a six-month recovery that is roughly $5,400 to $7,200 — real money, and it is yours by right, not by negotiation.

**Cure** is your medical care: every reasonable treatment, surgery, therapy session, and prescription tied to the injury, paid until MMI. There is no deductible, no co-pay, and no network restriction. If the company doctor clears you to return to work before you are healed, a second opinion from your own doctor can keep cure flowing.

Do the math on a realistic case. A derrickhand earning $1,600 a week is injured in a fall and cannot work for 26 weeks. Lost wages alone: $41,600. Maintenance at $35 a day for 182 days: $6,370. Medical care, surgery and rehab: often $50,000 to $150,000 for a serious injury. The maintenance-and-cure piece alone is worth more than $12,000 before a single dollar of negligence damages is counted — and it starts the day you are hurt, not the day a lawsuit is filed. If your employer is slow to pay it, or cuts it off early, that delay itself can support additional damages under maritime law.

One warning: maintenance and cure is not a settlement. Accepting it does not waive your right to sue for negligence or unseaworthiness. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you something.

## What never to sign in the first 30 days

The most dangerous moment in a rig-injury claim is not the accident. It is the quiet conversation a week later, when a friendly company representative offers help with the paperwork. Everything below has cost injured workers real money. Refuse all of it until you have spoken to a maritime attorney.

**A recorded statement.** You are not required to give one, and the recording will be used to lock in your words before you know the full extent of your injuries. "I feel okay" on day three becomes the insurer's exhibit when you need surgery on day thirty.

**A waiver or release in exchange for a small check.** A few thousand dollars "to help with bills" in return for signing away your Jones Act claim is the oldest move in the book. Once signed, that release is almost impossible to undo — even if the injury turns out to be far worse than anyone knew.

**A light-duty assignment you cannot safely do.** Returning to the rig before you are medically ready does two kinds of damage: it can re-injure you, and it hands the company a record that says you were fit for work. If your own doctor has not cleared you, the company's doctor clearing you is not enough.

**A resignation or "voluntary" separation.** Some injured workers are quietly nudged off the payroll. Losing your job does not end your claim — but it does end your leverage, your income, and sometimes your access to the rig's incident records. Do not resign, and do not sign anything that ends your employment, without legal advice.

**A settlement offer before your treatment is finished.** You cannot know what a case is worth until doctors know what the injury will cost — and that takes months, not days. Any offer in the first 30 days is priced for the company's ignorance about your injury, not yours.

## Jones Act negligence vs. unseaworthiness vs. LHWCA — which claim pays what

Three different legal theories can apply to one rig injury, and they pay very differently. Understanding which ones you have is the difference between a comp check and a full recovery.

**Jones Act negligence** applies if you are a seaman and the employer (or a fellow employee) was negligent — even slightly. Unsafe procedures, inadequate training, short crewing, faulty equipment the employer knew about, a supervisor who rushed a lift: all of it counts. The burden is famously low, and damages include medical costs, past and future lost wages, loss of future earning capacity, and pain and suffering. For an offshore worker whose high salary reflects dangerous, specialized work, loss of future earning capacity is often the largest single piece of the case.

**Unseaworthiness** is a separate claim under general maritime law against the vessel owner. A vessel must be reasonably fit for its intended use — safe equipment, adequate crew, seaworthy gear. Unlike a Jones Act claim, unseaworthiness does not require proving anyone was negligent: if the vessel was unfit and that caused your injury, the owner is liable. This is why defective cranes, corroded cables, and broken safety equipment matter so much — they can support a claim even when no individual made a mistake.

**LHWCA benefits** apply to harbor workers and fixed-platform workers who are not seamen. They pay medical care and a percentage of wages on a schedule, but they do not pay pain and suffering and they do not allow a negligence suit against the employer. What they do allow is a third-party lawsuit against anyone else who caused the injury — the equipment maker, a contractor, the vessel owner — and those third-party claims can recover the full range of damages.

A single accident can involve all three: a seaman on a jack-up rig, hurt by a contractor's faulty crane, may have a Jones Act claim against the employer, an unseaworthiness claim against the vessel owner, and a third-party claim against the crane company. Sorting that out is a lawyer's job — but knowing it exists is yours, because the company will never volunteer it.

## The 30-day checklist, day by day

Print this. Check each item off as it happens.

**Days 1–3:** Report the injury in writing and keep a copy. Get medical care and describe the accident the same way to every provider. Photograph the scene, the equipment, and your injuries if you safely can. Write down witness names and contact details. Start a pain-and-treatment diary — one line a day is enough.

**Days 4–7:** Confirm in writing that maintenance and cure has started, and note the daily rate. See your own doctor for a second opinion. Do not give a recorded statement. Do not sign anything the company puts in front of you. Tell your family where the incident report and your notes are kept.

**Days 8–14:** Request copies of the incident report, your medical records, and any drug-test results. Ask, in writing, for the preservation of evidence — the rig's logs, maintenance records, safety meeting notes, and any video. (Maritime lawyers call this a spoliation letter; it puts the company on notice that destroying evidence has consequences.) Keep every pay stub and benefits statement — they prove your wage rate.

**Days 15–30:** Talk to a maritime attorney — most offer free consultations and work on contingency, so there is no upfront cost. Bring your incident report, medical records, pay stubs, and your diary. If the company has offered a settlement, bring that too and do not sign it first. Confirm your maintenance payments are arriving on time and in full. If you have been cleared for light duty, get your own doctor's view before you accept it.

**The hard deadline to know:** the Jones Act gives you **three years** from the date of injury to file a lawsuit. Three years sounds generous. It is not — evidence degrades, crews rotate, and rigs are sold or scrapped. The cases that recover the most are the ones where a lawyer got involved in the first month, while the evidence still existed.

## What oil rig injury compensation actually covers

Pulling it together, a full Jones Act recovery can include: all past and future medical expenses; past lost wages and loss of future earning capacity; pain and suffering, past and future; maintenance and cure during recovery; and, where the employer's conduct was reckless, punitive damages. Run the numbers on a mid-range case: a 34-year-old floorhand earning $78,000 a year, out of work for one year and back at reduced capacity earning $45,000 for the next 20 years. Past lost wages: $78,000. Future earning-capacity loss: roughly $33,000 a year for 20 years — over $400,000 even before discounting to present value. Medical costs: $75,000 and counting. Pain and suffering for a career-ending back injury: commonly in the six figures. A case the company might value at a $50,000 quick settlement can be worth ten times that when the math is done properly.

That is why the first 30 days matter more than the next three years. The claim is built from the incident report, the medical records, the witness list, and the evidence that still exists — and every one of those is perishable. ## When to get a lawyer — and what it costs

Talk to a maritime attorney if any of these are true: the injury will keep you off work for more than a few weeks; the company is disputing how the accident happened; maintenance payments are late, short, or stopped; you were asked to sign anything; or you work on a fixed platform and were told you have "no claim." Almost all maritime injury lawyers work on contingency — typically around one-third of the recovery, paid only if you win — and the first consultation is usually free. There is no upfront cost to finding out what your case is worth, and there is a real cost to finding out too late.

The rig will keep drilling with or without you. Your claim will not wait. The first 30 days decide whether the Jones Act works for you or whether you end up with a comp check that covers a fraction of what the injury actually cost. Use the checklist, protect the evidence, and do not let anyone rush you into signing your rights away before you know what they are worth.
