If you're wondering what an [immigration attorney](/immigration-attorney) costs, the honest answer is: it depends entirely on what you're asking them to do. Helping a U.S. citizen petition for a spouse is a paperwork job worth a few thousand dollars. Defending someone in removal proceedings in front of an immigration judge is months of litigation that can cost five figures. Both are "immigration cases." They're priced like different planets.

This guide breaks down what immigration lawyers actually charge in 2026, the USCIS filing fees you'll pay on top of legal fees, and what three real immigration bills looked like line by line.

## The quick answer: immigration attorney fee ranges by case type

Most immigration attorneys work on **flat fees** for predictable cases (visas, green cards, citizenship) and **hourly rates** for unpredictable ones (deportation defense, appeals). Here's what 2026 pricing looks like:

| Case type | Typical attorney fee |
|---|---|
| U.S. citizen petition for a spouse (I-130) | $1,500 – $3,500 |
| Marriage-based green card package (I-130 + I-485) | $2,500 – $5,000 |
| Family visa petition for a parent or sibling | $1,500 – $3,000 |
| H-1B work visa petition | $2,000 – $5,000 |
| Employer-sponsored green card (PERM) | $5,000 – $10,000 |
| Naturalization / citizenship (N-400) | $1,000 – $2,500 |
| Asylum application | $2,500 – $7,500 |
| Deportation / removal defense | $5,000 – $15,000+ |
| Immigration appeal (BIA) | $3,000 – $8,000 |

Hourly rates for immigration attorneys typically run **$200–$400 per hour** in most markets, $350–$500+ in major metros like New York, Los Angeles, or Washington, D.C. The number on the right side of this table is the one people miss: attorney fees are only part of the bill. USCIS filing fees — which you pay no matter who does the work — can add **$500 to $5,000+** on top.

## The other half of the bill: USCIS filing fees you can't avoid

Hiring an attorney doesn't make the government fees go away. These are the filing costs your lawyer forwards to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, and they've been climbing:

- **I-130** (Petition for Alien Relative): $535 for paper filing, $625 online
- **I-485** (Adjustment of Status): $1,440 for adults (includes biometrics)
- **I-765** (Work authorization): $260–$520
- **I-131** (Travel document): $575–$630
- **N-400** (Naturalization): $640–$760
- **I-589** (Asylum application): free to file
- **H-1B registration + petition**: $215 registration fee, then petition fees that can total $2,000–$4,460 depending on employer size, plus a $500 fraud prevention fee and potentially a $4,000 fee for employers with 50+ workers

The trap: a marriage-based green card looks like a $3,500 legal fee, but the full bill is closer to **$6,000** once you add I-130 ($535), I-485 ($1,440), I-765 ($520), I-131 ($630), and the medical exam ($300–$600). Always ask your attorney for the **total with filing fees**, not just their fee.

## Three real immigration bills: where the money actually went

**The $4,100 marriage-based green card.** U.S. citizen petitions for a spouse already in the country legally. Attorney flat fee: $3,000 for the full package (I-130, I-485, I-765, I-131) including the interview. Government fees: $1,440 (I-485) + $535 (I-130) + $520 (I-765) + $630 (I-131) = $3,125 — but this couple had already filed I-130 a year earlier, so they only added $2,090 in new filing fees. Total: ~$5,090. Processing time: 14 months.

**The $7,800 asylum case.** Filed an I-589 asylum application with one RFE (Request for Evidence) answered by the attorney. Base flat fee: $5,500. The RFE response added $1,500 (most retainer agreements price RFE responses separately, at $1,000–$2,000 each). The asylum application itself is free to file, but biometrics and translations added $800. Interview prep: two sessions included in the base fee. Total: ~$7,800. Timeline: filing to interview, 19 months.

**The $13,500 deportation defense.** Notice to Appear issued; the attorney handled master calendar hearings, filed for cancellation of removal, and represented the client through two merits hearings. Priced hourly at $350: roughly 30 billable hours ($10,500) plus $3,000 in expert report and transcript costs. Total: $13,500. The case is still pending after 2+ years — removal proceedings routinely run that long, which is why flat fees are rare here.

The pattern: simple, predictable filings are flat-fee and affordable. Anything involving a judge, an RFE, or an appeal moves toward hourly billing and climbs fast.

## What drives the price up (and what keeps it down)

**Your immigration history matters more than the case type.** Prior deportation orders, fraud findings, criminal convictions, or visa overstays turn a standard filing into a complex case. An attorney who'd charge $2,500 for a clean marriage-based green card may charge $6,000+ for the same green card when a 10-year unlawful-presence bar needs a waiver (I-601, which alone adds $1,950 in USCIS fees).

**RFEs and NOIDs are the budget killer.** A Request for Evidence or Notice of Intent to Deny typically costs $1,000–$2,000 to answer because the attorney has to assemble evidence, write a legal brief, and sometimes get expert opinions. Strong initial filings avoid this: attorneys who charge a bit more up front often cost less in total.

**The "waiver tax."** Cases needing waivers — I-601 (unlawful presence), I-212 (prior deportation), I-601A (provisional waiver) — add both legal work and government fees. A waiver application alone typically costs $2,500–$5,000 in attorney fees plus $715–$1,950 in filing fees. If your case needs one, get a fee estimate in writing before signing.

**Geography and firm size.** Solo practitioners and boutique immigration firms charge 20–40% less than big corporate immigration practices, and their fees are often more negotiable. Rural and smaller-city attorneys typically charge less than those in immigration-heavy metros. But don't hire on price alone for high-stakes cases.

## How to hire an immigration attorney without overpaying (or getting burned)

1. **Get the fee agreement in writing before you sign.** It should spell out exactly which forms and services the flat fee covers, what's extra (RFEs, appeals, additional hearings), and what happens to your fee if the case is denied.
2. **Ask about filing fees separately.** "What's my total out-the-door cost including USCIS fees?" should be the first question you ask. The attorney should quote the current government fees from memory.
3. **Beware of "notarios."** In many Latin American countries, *notarios* are licensed legal professionals. In the U.S., a notary public is **not** a lawyer and cannot give immigration advice. Every year, thousands of people lose their cases — and their money — to notario fraud. Only a licensed attorney or an accredited representative can represent you before USCIS.
4. **Check your state's bar.** Verify any attorney's license on your state bar's website before paying a retainer. If you're not sure where to start, browse attorneys by location on this site — for example, [California immigration attorneys](/attorneys/california) or [Texas immigration attorneys](/attorneys/texas).
5. **Ask about payment plans.** Many immigration attorneys split flat fees into 2–4 payments tied to filing milestones (half at signing, half when the case is filed). Get the schedule in the written agreement.
6. **Compare three quotes.** Fees vary enormously for the same case. A $3,000 quote and a $5,500 quote for the same marriage-based green card usually reflect different experience levels and different levels of hands-on work — ask what the difference buys you.

## When a flat fee is fair, and when it's a warning sign

Flat fees are fair for: I-130 petitions, marriage-based green card packages, N-400 naturalization, and straightforward H-1B petitions. These are predictable, form-driven cases.

A flat fee is a warning sign for: deportation defense, asylum with complex facts, appeals, and anything involving waivers. If an attorney quotes a low flat fee for a removal case, read the fine print — it probably covers only the first hearing, with everything else billed hourly afterward. That's not dishonest, but it should be disclosed up front.

For hourly cases, ask for a **fee range estimate** ("based on cases like mine, what's the low end and high end?") and request monthly billing statements so nothing surprises you.

## What if you can't afford an immigration attorney?

Private attorneys aren't the only option, and for lower-income filers they're often not necessary:

- **Nonprofit legal aid organizations** handle family immigration and asylum cases for free or on a sliding scale. The Department of Justice maintains a public list of recognized organizations and accredited representatives who can legally represent you before USCIS and immigration court.
- **Law school immigration clinics** give law students hands-on cases under a professor's supervision — supervised, careful work at little or no cost. Clinics in cities with major law schools often take asylum, DACA, and family-based cases.
- **BIA-accredited representatives** at recognized nonprofits can charge capped, low fees (often under $1,000 for a family petition) and are authorized to represent you.
- **Fee waivers and reduced fees.** USCIS offers fee waivers (Form I-912) for many applications if your household income is at or below 150% of the federal poverty guidelines. For an N-400, that's the difference between paying $760 and paying $0.

Even if you hire a private attorney, these options give you a price floor to negotiate against. And one consultation — many immigration attorneys offer a $100–$200 initial consult, and some offer them free — can tell you whether your case is simple enough to file yourself or complex enough to warrant full representation.

## The bottom line

Most people hiring an [immigration attorney](/immigration-attorney) spend between **$2,000 and $7,000** all-in for routine cases — a family visa, a marriage-based green card, or citizenship. That range doubles or triples the moment your case involves a judge, a waiver, or a prior immigration violation. The cheapest attorney is rarely the cheapest outcome: a well-prepared initial filing avoids the RFE responses, denials, and re-filings that quietly double the cost of a "bargain" hire.
