After a car crash, a slip-and-fall, or any serious injury, one decision matters more than almost any other: which [personal injury lawyer](/personal-injury-lawyer) you hire. Insurance companies keep detailed records on which attorneys actually take cases to trial — and they make settlement offers accordingly. A lawyer with a real trial record routinely recovers two or three times what a settlement-mill firm gets for the same injuries.

But most people have never hired a lawyer before, and the billboards all say the same thing. Here's how to cut through the noise: ask these seven questions at every consultation, and watch closely for the red flags at the end.

## 1. "How many cases like mine have you taken to trial?"

This is the single most important question. Most personal injury cases settle — but *the threat of trial* is what drives settlement value. Insurance adjusters know which firms actually try cases and which ones always fold at the last minute.

A good answer sounds like: "I've tried 40 personal injury cases in the last ten years, including 12 jury trials last year." A bad answer sounds like: "We prefer to settle — trials are risky." Every lawyer prefers to settle; the question is whether they *can* win at trial when the insurer won't pay fairly.

Ask a follow-up: what were the results? You don't need names, but verdict ranges tell you whether this lawyer handles cases at your level of seriousness.

## 2. "Who will actually handle my case day to day?"

High-volume advertising firms often have you meet a senior partner at the consultation, then hand your file to a junior associate — or worse, a paralegal — you never met. There's nothing wrong with associates doing work, but you deserve to know who makes the decisions on your case.

Ask directly: Will you be my attorney of record? Who returns my calls? How many active cases does that person carry? A lawyer juggling 300 files can't give yours real attention, no matter how good their TV commercial is.

## 3. "What is your fee, and what happens to case expenses if we lose?"

Almost all personal injury lawyers work on contingency — typically around one-third of the recovery, sometimes more if the case goes to trial. That's standard. The part people forget to ask about is *expenses*: filing fees, expert witnesses, medical record retrieval, deposition costs. On a serious case, expenses can run into the tens of thousands of dollars.

The key question: if we lose, do I owe you for those expenses? Reputable firms absorb case costs when a case doesn't recover — get that in writing. Also ask whether the percentage is calculated before or after expenses are deducted; the difference can be thousands of dollars.

Never sign a fee agreement you haven't read fully. A trustworthy lawyer will walk through it line by line without rushing you.

## Contingency fee math: three worked examples

The "one-third" fee sounds simple until you see what it does to real numbers. Here's how the same $100,000 settlement breaks down under three common arrangements — and why the fine print matters more than the headline percentage.

**Example A: 33% fee, expenses deducted after.** Settlement: $100,000. Case expenses: $8,000 (experts, filings, depositions). Lawyer takes 33% of $100,000 = $33,000. Then $8,000 in expenses comes off the top. You receive: **$59,000.**

**Example B: 33% fee, expenses deducted before.** Same $100,000 settlement, same $8,000 expenses. Expenses come off first: $92,000 remains. Lawyer takes 33% of $92,000 = $30,360. You receive: **$61,640.** Same fee percentage, $2,640 more in your pocket — just from the order of subtraction.

**Example C: sliding scale, 25% pre-suit / 40% at trial.** Your case settles before a lawsuit is filed: lawyer takes 25% = $25,000, expenses $4,000 (lower because no litigation). You receive: **$71,000.** But if the same case goes to trial and wins $150,000 with $25,000 in trial expenses: lawyer takes 40% = $60,000, and you receive **$65,000** — less than the smaller pre-suit settlement netted you. This is why "we'll take it to trial" isn't always the win it sounds like; run the numbers both ways before deciding.

The lesson: never compare lawyers on percentage alone. Compare them on the *net to you* under realistic scenarios, and get the expense terms in writing.

## 4. "What is my case worth — and what could go wrong with it?"

Be suspicious of any lawyer who quotes you a big number in the first meeting. An honest attorney can't value your case before seeing your medical records, the police report, and the insurance policy limits — anyone who does is telling you what you want to hear.

What you want instead is a lawyer who explains the *variables*: the severity and permanence of your injuries, how clear liability is, the at-fault driver's policy limits, and your state's fault rules. In [California](/attorneys/california) or [Texas](/attorneys/texas), comparative fault reduces your award by your share of blame; in contributory-negligence states like [Alabama](/attorneys/alabama) or [North Carolina](/attorneys/north-carolina), even 1% of fault can bar recovery entirely. A lawyer who explains the weaknesses in your case upfront is one you can trust with its strengths.

## 5. "How long will my case take, and how will you keep me updated?"

Simple injury cases can settle in months; complex ones involving surgery, disputed liability, or multiple defendants can take two years or more. What matters isn't speed — it's communication. The number one complaint clients file against personal injury lawyers isn't about results; it's about unreturned phone calls.

Ask: How often will I hear from you? Do you respond to email? Will I get copies of everything filed? Set the expectation now. A firm with a real client-communication system (regular status updates, a portal, a dedicated contact) is worth more than a slightly higher advertised settlement average.

## 6. "Have you handled cases in the court where mine would be filed?"

Personal injury law is intensely local. The judges, the jury pools, the defense firms, even the unwritten customs of a courthouse vary enormously — a lawyer who dominates in [Florida](/attorneys/florida)'s Miami-Dade courts may be a stranger in Orlando. Ask whether the attorney has actually appeared before the judges likely to hear your case.

This matters even more in states with unusual rules. [New York](/attorneys/new-york)'s scaffold law, [Michigan](/attorneys/michigan)'s no-fault reform, [Louisiana](/attorneys/louisiana)'s civil-law system — each rewards genuine local expertise and punishes dabblers.

## 7. "Can you give me references from past clients?"

Ethics rules limit what lawyers can share, but many firms can connect you with former clients willing to talk, or point you to detailed reviews. Check your state bar's website for disciplinary history — it takes two minutes and surprises people more often than you'd think.

Also trust your gut at the consultation. Did the lawyer listen, or lecture? Did they explain things plainly, or hide behind jargon? You'll work with this person for a year or more through one of the most stressful periods of your life. Competence matters most, but you should also feel heard.

## The settlement-mill problem: how to spot one

Some firms run on volume: sign 1,000 cases a year, settle every one fast for whatever the insurer offers, and move on. The business model works for the firm. It rarely works for the client. Here's how to tell the difference between a settlement mill and a real trial practice.

**The math gives it away.** A firm carrying 400+ active injury files per attorney cannot prepare any of them for trial — there aren't enough hours in the year. Ask directly: "How many open files does each attorney carry?" Under 50 is a real practice. Over 150 is a processing plant.

**Watch the first meeting.** Settlement mills sign you up in 15 minutes, often with a non-lawyer "investigator" who shows up at your house or hospital room. Real firms have a lawyer evaluate your case, explain the weaknesses, and sometimes *decline* cases they can't add value to. A firm that accepts literally everyone isn't selective — it's hungry.

**Check their trial footprint.** Search the firm's name plus "jury verdict" or "trial." Real trial lawyers leave a paper trail: reported verdicts, courtroom news coverage, speaking engagements at trial-lawyer conferences. If all you find is advertising, that's your answer.

**The settlement pattern.** Ask: "What percentage of your cases settle before filing a lawsuit?" Anything over 95% filed-to-settled without meaningful litigation is a red flag — it means the firm never applies pressure, and insurers price that in. Your herniated disc is worth whatever the adjuster's spreadsheet says, because the adjuster knows no jury will ever see it.

None of this means every big advertiser is bad. Some large firms do excellent trial work. But the billboard tells you about their marketing budget, not their courtroom record. The seven questions above tell you the rest.

## Red flags: when to walk away

- **They guarantee a result.** No ethical lawyer promises an outcome — it's actually against the rules in most states.
- **They pressure you to sign today.** A good case will still be a good case tomorrow. High-pressure sign-ups are a business model, not a law practice.
- **They want money upfront.** Reputable personal injury lawyers work on contingency. Upfront fees for an injury case are a major warning sign.
- **They won't put the fee agreement in writing.** Walk out immediately.
- **You can't reach them after signing.** If communication is bad during the consultation — when they're trying to win your business — it will only get worse.

## What to bring to the consultation

Come prepared and you'll get a far more useful evaluation. Bring: the police or incident report (or its report number), photos from the scene, the other party's insurance information, your auto insurance declarations page, all medical records and bills so far, proof of missed work and lost wages, and any letters or calls you've already received from an insurance company. Also write down a timeline of what happened while it's fresh — dates, symptoms, conversations. A lawyer who sees organized documentation takes your case more seriously, and can spot issues (like a looming filing deadline or a coverage problem) in that first meeting instead of discovering them months later.

## The bottom line

Choosing a personal injury lawyer isn't about finding the biggest advertiser. It's about finding a trial-ready advocate who communicates clearly, charges fairly, and knows your local courts. Ask the seven questions above at two or three firms, compare the answers honestly, and hire the lawyer — not the commercial — that earns your trust.

Most consultations are free, so there's no reason to hire the first lawyer you meet. Take a week, do the interviews, check the state bar records, and then decide with confidence.
