A rear-end collision and an 80,000-pound semi plowing into stopped traffic can produce the exact same injuries — a herniated disc, a shattered wrist, eighteen months of rehab. The hospital bills don't know the difference. But the lawsuits? They belong to different planets.

A car accident case is a state-law dispute between drivers and insurers. A truck accident case is a federal-regulation dispute against an entire supply chain, with insurance floors thirty times higher, evidence that deletes itself, and damage math that can turn a capped $150,000 recovery into a seven-figure one. If your lawyer treats your truck wreck like a big fender-bender, you are leaving money on the table — possibly hundreds of thousands of dollars of it.

This guide walks through the seven structural differences, with the actual numbers, so you understand what your case really is.

## 1. The insurance floor: $25,000 vs $750,000

Everything in a car accident case flows from one question: how much insurance is there? Most states let drivers carry shockingly little. Compare the minimums:

- **California:** $15,000 per person / $30,000 per accident
- **Florida:** $10,000 personal injury protection (not even liability in many cases) / $20,000
- **New York:** $25,000 / $50,000
- **Texas:** $30,000 / $60,000

If the driver who hit you carries the state minimum and you have no underinsured motorist coverage of your own, your recovery has a hard ceiling. A $400,000 injury against a $25,000 policy is, in practice, a $25,000 case.

Federal law erases that ceiling for commercial trucks. Under the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration's financial responsibility rules (49 CFR 387), any interstate carrier operating vehicles over 10,001 pounds must carry at least **$750,000** in liability coverage. Move oil or hazardous materials and the floor jumps to **$1 million to $5 million**, depending on the cargo.

Do the ratio: $750,000 ÷ $25,000 = **30×**. The legal floor on a truck case is thirty times the floor on a car case. And that is only the floor — national carriers routinely carry $1 million primary policies plus umbrella layers of $5–10 million. The money exists. The fight is over how much of it your injuries are worth.

## 2. One defendant vs an entire supply chain

In a car crash, your defendant list is short: the driver, possibly the car's owner, and their insurer. Liability is usually one question — who was at fault?

A truck crash hands you a chain of companies, each with its own insurer and its own legal exposure:

1. **The driver** — fatigue, distraction, speed, impairment.
2. **The motor carrier** — the trucking company, liable for the driver's acts under vicarious liability (respondeat superior), plus its own direct negligence: hiring an unqualified driver, skipping background checks, ignoring prior violations.
3. **The freight broker** — the intermediary that matched the load to the carrier. Broker liability is contested and fact-specific, but where a broker selected an unsafe carrier to save money, courts in many states let that theory reach a jury.
4. **The shipper or loader** — improperly loaded or secured cargo shifts weight and causes rollovers and jackknifes.
5. **The maintenance contractor or shop** — skipped brake inspections, bald tires, deferred repairs.
6. **The parts manufacturer** — a defective brake chamber or tire that failed.

Each of these is a separate settlement conversation and a separate insurance policy. Direct-negligence theories — negligent hiring, retention, entrustment, supervision, and training — matter because they let you argue that the company was careless in its own right, not just responsible for the driver's mistake. That is how truck cases reach damages that no single driver policy could cover.

## 3. The rulebook switches from state to federal

Car accident liability is governed by state traffic law: who had the right of way, who was speeding, who ran the light. Truck cases add an entire second rulebook — the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Regulations — and that rulebook is written in numbers a jury can verify.

The hours-of-service rules are the centerpiece:

- **11-hour driving limit** — after 10 consecutive hours off duty, a driver may drive at most 11 hours.
- **14-hour window** — driving is prohibited after the 14th consecutive hour on duty following 10 hours off.
- **30-minute break** — required after 8 cumulative hours of driving time.
- **60/70-hour limits** — no driving after 60 hours in 7 days (or 70 hours in 8 days) on duty.

Since the electronic logging device (ELD) mandate took effect in December 2017, driving hours are recorded automatically — tampering with them is itself a federal violation. There are also federal rules on pre-trip inspections, brake adjustment limits, cargo securement, and drug and alcohol testing.

Why does this matter for your case? Because a regulatory violation can shortcut the hardest part of a lawsuit — proving negligence. In many states, violating a safety regulation is negligence *per se*: the violation itself establishes that the carrier was careless, and the argument moves to causation and damages. Your lawyer isn't just arguing "the driver was tired." They are arguing "the ELD data shows 13.4 driving hours against an 11-hour federal limit." One is an opinion. The other is a spreadsheet.

## 4. Evidence that deletes itself

A car accident leaves relatively stable evidence: police report, photos, maybe a traffic camera. A truck accident leaves a data trail — and that trail is on a countdown timer.

Modern commercial trucks record engine control module (ECM) data: speed, braking, throttle position, cruise control status, seatbelt use, and hard-braking events. Many carriers overwrite this data on rolling windows as short as 30 days. The driver's qualification file, dispatch records, delivery schedules, maintenance logs, and dashcam footage all live in company hands, and companies are allowed to follow their ordinary document-retention schedules — until someone tells them not to.

That is why truck accident lawyers send a **spoliation letter** (also called a preservation letter) within days of being hired. It puts the carrier on notice: preserve the ECM data, the ELD logs, the driver file, the maintenance records, the dispatch communications. Once that letter lands, destroying the data is no longer "routine housekeeping" — it is spoliation, and judges can punish it with sanctions that include instructing the jury to assume the destroyed evidence would have hurt the company.

Meanwhile, major carriers deploy rapid-response teams — investigators and sometimes lawyers — to serious crash scenes within hours. Their job is to build the defense before you have even left the hospital. If you were in a truck crash and nobody has sent a preservation letter yet, the clock is the most expensive thing in your case.

## 5. The settlement math: same injuries, two price tags

Here is where the differences become dollars. Take the same injury profile in two cases:

- Herniated cervical disc requiring surgery, plus a fractured wrist
- Medical bills: **$180,000**
- Lost wages (2 years at $45,000/year): **$90,000**
- Future medical care: **$60,000**
- Total economic damages: **$330,000**

**Case A: car accident.** The at-fault driver carries a $50,000 policy. Your own underinsured motorist coverage adds $100,000. Total available insurance: $150,000. Your true damages are $330,000 plus pain and suffering — but the money physically in play is $150,000, and medical liens eat first. Even with perfect liability, your lawyer's realistic target is the $150,000 policy ceiling, and your take-home after liens, costs, and fees might be $60,000–$80,000. (Your [underinsured motorist coverage](/blog/what-to-do-after-car-accident-legal-checklist) is the only thing standing between you and the driver's minimum policy — see our accident checklist for why it matters.)

**Case B: truck accident.** Same $330,000 in economic damages, plus non-economic damages (pain, suffering, loss of enjoyment) that a jury evaluates based on the severity of the event — and an 80,000-pound impact reads differently than a fender-bender. Total case value: **$600,000–$900,000**, depending on jurisdiction and permanence of injury. The carrier's policy floor is $750,000, so the money exists. After a 33.3% contingency fee on, say, a $750,000 settlement, roughly $40,000 in case costs, and medical liens, your take-home is in the **$330,000–$400,000** range — four to five times the car-case outcome for identical injuries.

The injuries didn't change. The *structure* changed: the insurance floor, the number of liable parties, and the federal violations that prove negligence. That is why truck cases settle for more — not because lawyers wave a magic wand, but because the math underneath is different. ## 6. Punitive damages live in truck cases

Punitive damages — money awarded to punish the defendant, not to compensate you — are rare in ordinary car accident cases. Courts reserve them for conduct that is willful, wanton, or grossly negligent, and a moment of inattention behind the wheel rarely qualifies.

Truck cases cross that threshold far more often, because the evidence is corporate. A driver who falsified ELD logs to hide 16-hour days. A carrier that knew a driver's license was suspended and dispatched him anyway. A maintenance program that deferred brake work across a fleet to hit quarterly numbers. Those are not split-second mistakes — they are business decisions, documented in emails and spreadsheets, and juries punish business decisions.

The amounts can be enormous, though many states cap them. Texas, for example, generally limits punitive damages to the greater of $200,000 or two times economic damages plus up to $750,000 in non-economic damages. Even with caps, a punitive award in a truck case can exceed the entire value of a comparable car case. When your lawyer evaluates your case, ask specifically whether the facts support a punitive-damages claim — it changes the settlement posture completely, because the carrier's exposure stops being "what the injuries cost" and becomes "what a jury does to companies that do this."

## 7. The case costs more to build — and takes longer

A straightforward car accident case might cost $2,000–$10,000 to litigate: medical records, one deposition, maybe a treating physician's testimony. Most settle within 6–12 months.

A truck case is a different budget:

- **ECM/telematics download and analysis:** $2,000–$5,000
- **Accident reconstructionist:** $5,000–$25,000
- **FMCSA compliance expert:** $5,000–$15,000 to audit the carrier's safety record
- **Life-care planner** (for catastrophic injuries): $10,000–$20,000
- **Vocational economist:** $5,000–$10,000 to quantify lost earning capacity
- **Depositions across multiple companies:** $3,000–$8,000 each

Total case costs of $25,000–$100,000+ are normal, and the timeline stretches to 18–36 months because you are litigating against several defendants with separate counsel, each running its own delay strategy. This is precisely why the contingency-fee structure matters more here: you are not funding a $50,000 war chest out of pocket. ## 8. What changes about the lawyer you need

None of this means every [personal injury lawyer](/personal-injury-lawyer) can handle a truck case. The skill set is specific:

- **FMCSA literacy.** If a lawyer can't explain the 11-hour rule, the ELD mandate, and what a BASIC score is, they can't read the carrier's safety record — and the safety record is often where the case is won.
- **Rapid evidence preservation.** The spoliation letter goes out in days, not months. Ask any prospective lawyer: "What do you preserve in the first two weeks of a truck case?" The answer should be immediate and specific.
- **Multi-defendant litigation experience.** Suing a driver is one deposition. Suing a driver, carrier, broker, and maintenance shop is a coordinated discovery campaign. Ask how many trucking cases they have taken past the pleading stage.
- **Trial credibility against corporate defendants.** Trucking insurers keep the same scorecards on lawyers that car insurers do — our guide on [choosing a personal injury lawyer](/blog/how-to-choose-personal-injury-lawyer) explains how those scorecards set your settlement number. A firm the carriers know will go to trial gets different offers than a settlement mill.

Trucking law is also local in the ways that matter: state courts, state damage caps, and state rules on broker liability all vary. If you are comparing firms, start with attorneys who actually litigate in your state — browse [Texas truck accident attorneys](/attorneys/texas), [California](/attorneys/california), or your own state — and ask each one the FMCSA questions above. The right lawyer will answer them before you finish asking.

Make sure your lawyer is doing that math, not the car-crash math.
