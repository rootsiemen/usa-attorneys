Most people walk into a bankruptcy consultation believing the choice is theirs: wipe the slate clean with Chapter 7, or pay it back slowly with Chapter 13. The uncomfortable truth is that the choice is mostly made by arithmetic before you ever sit down. Your income, your assets, and the federal means test form a funnel, and only one chapter usually comes out the other end. This guide runs that funnel step by step, with real numbers, so you know which chapter you qualify for — and what each one will actually cost you — before you pay for an hour of anyone's time.

## The one-sentence difference that matters

Chapter 7 is liquidation bankruptcy: a trustee sells your non-exempt assets, pays creditors what little there is, and the court discharges your remaining unsecured debts in roughly 3 to 4 months. Most Chapter 7 filers keep everything they own because their assets fall inside exemption limits — which is why it is sometimes called a "fresh start" bankruptcy.

Chapter 13 is reorganization bankruptcy: you keep your property and repay creditors through a court-supervised plan that lasts 3 to 5 years. You propose the plan, the trustee administers it, and at the end the remaining eligible balances are discharged. It is built for people with regular income who either earn too much for Chapter 7 or have assets they want to protect.

That is the theory. The practice is a series of financial tests. Here they are.

## The means test: the formula that picks your chapter

The means test is a two-part calculation Congress added in 2005 to stop high earners from using Chapter 7. It runs like this:

**Step 1 — Compare your income to your state's median.** Take your average gross monthly income over the six full calendar months before filing, multiply by 12, and compare it to the median income for a household your size in your state (published by the U.S. Trustee Program). If your annualized income is at or below the median, you pass the means test automatically. You can file Chapter 7.

**Step 2 — The disposable income test.** If your income is above the median, the test deducts allowed living expenses — housing, food, transportation, health care, taxes, childcare, based partly on IRS standard allowances and partly on your actual costs — from your income. What is left is your "disposable income." If that number is high enough to repay a meaningful portion of your unsecured debt over five years (roughly $15,000+ in total, under the current thresholds), you are presumed to be abusing Chapter 7 and must file Chapter 13 instead.

### Worked example: does Maria qualify for Chapter 7?

Maria lives alone in Florida. Her numbers:

- Average gross monthly income over the last 6 months: **$5,200** → annualized: **$62,400**
- Florida median income for a one-person household: approximately **$63,000** (figures are updated periodically; always check the current U.S. Trustee table)
- Result: $62,400 is below the median. **Maria passes the means test at Step 1.** She may file Chapter 7.

Now change one fact: Maria earns $5,600/month instead — $67,200 annualized, above the median. Now she faces Step 2. Her allowed deductions might look like:

- Housing and utilities (IRS standard for her county): $1,850
- Food, clothing, personal care (IRS national standard, one person): $800
- Transportation (ownership + operation costs): $650
- Health care and insurance: $450
- Taxes withheld: $900
- **Total allowed expenses: $4,650**
- **Disposable income: $5,600 − $4,650 = $950/month → $57,000 over five years**

That is well above the abuse threshold. Maria would be steered into Chapter 13. A $400/month raise cost her Chapter 7 eligibility — which is exactly why timing your filing matters, and why a ([bankruptcy attorney](/bankruptcy-attorney)) will sometimes advise waiting a few months if your income recently dropped.

## What each chapter does to your property and timeline

The structural differences, side by side:

| | **Chapter 7** | **Chapter 13** |
|---|---|---|
| Typical duration | 3–4 months | 3–5 years |
| How debts are handled | Discharged after liquidation | Repaid through a court plan, then remaining balances discharged |
| Keep your house? | Yes, if current on payments and equity is within your state's homestead exemption | Yes — the plan lets you catch up on arrears over time |
| Keep your car? | Yes, if equity is within the vehicle exemption and payments are current | Yes, and the plan can sometimes reduce the loan balance to the car's value ("cramdown") |
| Minimum repayment to unsecured creditors | $0 | Depends on income and assets — can be 0–100% |
| Income requirement | Must pass the means test | Must have regular income to fund the plan |

**Exemptions are the quiet kingmaker in Chapter 7.** Every state has its own exemption schedule (some states let you choose federal exemptions instead). Texas and Florida offer unlimited homestead exemptions; California's is far more modest. A filer in ([Texas](/attorneys/texas)) with $200,000 of home equity may lose nothing in Chapter 7, while the same filer in a low-exemption state could see the trustee sell the house. Your state's exemption table is the single most important variable after income — look it up before you do anything else.

**Chapter 13's superpower is the cure.** If you are $18,000 behind on a mortgage, Chapter 7 does not fix that — the lender can still foreclose. Chapter 13 lets you spread that $18,000 over the life of the plan (about $300/month on a 60-month plan) while resuming regular payments. People choose Chapter 13 deliberately for this reason even when they qualify for Chapter 7.

## The true cost of filing: fees, classes, and attorney math

Bankruptcy is not free, and the irony is real: the people who need it most must find several thousand dollars first.

**Court filing fees (federal, standard):** Chapter 7 is **$338**; Chapter 13 is **$313**. These go to the court, not your lawyer, and can sometimes be paid in installments or waived in extreme hardship cases.

**Mandatory credit counseling:** two courses — one before filing, one before discharge — typically **$25–$50 each** from approved providers.

**Attorney fees — the real variable:**

- **Chapter 7:** national averages run **$1,500–$3,000** for a straightforward case, climbing to $3,500+ with business debts, adversary proceedings, or above-median complexity. Most Chapter 7 attorneys require payment in full before filing, because once you file, the debt you owe them is itself dischargeable.
- **Chapter 13:** national averages run **$3,000–$6,000**, but here is the twist — many bankruptcy courts set "no-look" fee amounts (a presumptively reasonable fee the court approves without scrutiny, often $4,000–$5,000 depending on district). Critically, most or all of the Chapter 13 attorney fee can be paid *through the plan itself*, meaning you can file with little or nothing down.

That last point flips the cash-flow math: a broke filer who cannot scrape together $2,500 for Chapter 7 can often file Chapter 13 immediately, with the attorney paid monthly out of the plan. Lawyers know this. Some less scrupulous ones steer cash-strapped clients toward Chapter 13 for exactly that reason — which is why you should always ask whether you qualify for Chapter 7 first.

**The five-year cost comparison most people never see:** a Chapter 13 plan paying $950/month for 60 months moves **$57,000** through the trustee's hands (trustee takes a percentage, up to 10%, as commission). A Chapter 7 costs you roughly $2,000–$3,500 total and is over in four months. If you qualify for Chapter 7 and have no assets worth protecting, Chapter 13 is almost never the better financial deal — you are paying tens of thousands more for the privilege of repaying debt you could have discharged.

## Worked examples: two households, two different answers

### Household A: the Patels — Chapter 7 territory

- Combined income: $58,000/year (below their state's median for a family of three)
- Unsecured debt: $46,000 in credit cards and medical bills
- Home: $190,000 market value, $175,000 mortgage → $15,000 equity (within exemption)
- Cars: two paid-off vehicles worth $9,000 combined (within vehicle exemptions)

The Patels pass the means test at Step 1. Their equity is exempt. Filing Chapter 7 costs them about **$2,200 in attorney fees + $338 filing fee + ~$75 in courses ≈ $2,613 total**, and in about four months the $46,000 in unsecured debt is gone. A Chapter 13 plan for the same household would have moved $40,000+ to creditors over five years. For them, Chapter 7 is the obvious answer.

### Household B: David — Chapter 13 is the tool he needs

- Income: $88,000/year (above median; fails Step 1, fails Step 2 with $1,100/month disposable income)
- Mortgage arrears: $21,000 (nine months behind after a layoff; now re-employed)
- Unsecured debt: $34,000 in credit cards
- Home equity: $65,000 — above his state's homestead exemption by about $25,000

David cannot file Chapter 7 even if he wanted to — and he should not want to, because a Chapter 7 trustee could sell his house to capture that $25,000 of non-exempt equity. His Chapter 13 plan: $1,100/month for 60 months = **$66,000** through the plan. That covers the $21,000 arrears cure, his attorney's fee (paid through the plan), the trustee's commission, and a partial dividend to unsecured creditors — with the remainder discharged at the end. He keeps the house. The math picked his chapter, and the chapter saved his home.

## The debts neither chapter erases

Bankruptcy is powerful but not magic. These obligations generally survive both chapters:

- **Child support and alimony** — never dischargeable, and in Chapter 13 they must be paid in full through the plan.
- **Most student loans** — dischargeable only by proving "undue hardship" in a separate adversary proceeding, a standard courts interpret brutally narrowly. Fewer than 1% of filers even attempt it.
- **Recent tax debts** — income taxes less than three years old (with returns filed on time) survive. Older, properly filed tax debts sometimes can be discharged — one of the few areas where timing is everything.
- **Debts from fraud, DUI injuries, or willful misconduct** — excluded by statute.
- **Criminal fines and restitution** — survive in full.
- **Debts you "forget" to list** — in Chapter 7, an unlisted debt in most districts is not discharged. List everything, including debts to family members.

If your $60,000 problem is $50,000 of student loans and $10,000 of credit cards, bankruptcy solves the smaller problem and leaves the larger one. Know your debt composition before you file — add up each category separately.

## Questions to ask a bankruptcy attorney before you sign

Bring these to the consultation. The answers separate careful counsel from a document mill:

1. **Do I pass the means test today, and would waiting change the answer?** If your income dropped recently, the six-month lookback may still be using the old numbers.
2. **Which exemptions apply to me — state or federal — and what exactly is protected?** Ask for the dollar figures on your home equity, vehicles, retirement accounts, and cash.
3. **What is your total fee, what does it include, and what triggers extra charges?** Adversary proceedings, lien avoidance motions, and reaffirmation hearings are the common add-ons.
4. **For Chapter 13: what is my estimated plan payment, and what percentage will unsecured creditors actually receive?** If the answer is 100%, ask point-blank why Chapter 13 beats Chapter 7 for you.
5. **Have you handled cases in front of the trustees in my district?** Local trustees have individual quirks — document demands, exemption challenges, plan objections — and local experience matters more here than in most practice areas.

The means test already did the first cut. A good ([bankruptcy attorney](/bankruptcy-attorney)) does the second: checking the exemptions, timing the filing, and making sure the chapter the math picked is actually the chapter that serves you. In ([Florida](/attorneys/florida)) or ([Texas](/attorneys/texas)), generous homestead exemptions change the calculus entirely; in low-exemption states, the same numbers produce a different answer. The formula is federal. The outcome is local.
