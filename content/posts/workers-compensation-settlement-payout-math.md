Nobody at the warehouse sits around calculating their injury settlement the way they calculate their paycheck — until the injury happens, and then the first question is always the same: "So... how much do I actually get?" The answer frustrates people, because a [workers' compensation attorney](/workers-compensation-attorney) will tell you there is no single number. But that's not the same as saying there's no logic. Workers' comp runs on formulas, and once you see the formulas, the mystery number stops looking like a lottery ticket and starts looking like a math problem. A math problem you can actually check someone's work on.

## The two-thirds rule: where every payout starts

Almost every state builds its wage-replacement benefits on the same skeleton: if a workplace injury keeps you from working, temporary total disability (TTD) benefits pay roughly **two-thirds of your average weekly wage (AWW)** — 66⅔%, to be exact — until you hit your state's maximum or minimum weekly cap.

AWW itself is usually calculated from your gross wages over the 52 weeks before the injury, divided by 52. Overtime, bonuses, and second jobs at the same employer often count, depending on the state.

Here's a worked example. Take a warehouse associate in [Illinois](/attorneys/illinois) earning $62,400 a year:

- AWW = $62,400 ÷ 52 = **$1,200/week**
- TTD benefit = $1,200 × 66.67% = **$800/week**

If she's out for 14 weeks recovering from a back injury, wage benefits total $800 × 14 = **$11,200** — plus all authorized medical treatment, which workers' comp pays in full (no copays, no deductibles) in nearly every state. That $11,200 is the floor of the claim's value, and it's the part people most often undercount, because they forget that medical bills, physical therapy, prescriptions, and even mileage to appointments are part of the payout, not separate from it.

## The four buckets of benefits (and why the mix matters)

Workers' comp isn't one check. It's up to four different benefit streams, and settlements are really negotiations about how much of each stream gets converted into one lump number:

1. **Temporary Total Disability (TTD)** — wage replacement while you can't work at all. Ends at maximum medical improvement (MMI), when the doctor says you've healed as much as you're going to.
2. **Temporary Partial Disability (TPD)** — if you can work light duty at lower pay, TPD covers a percentage of the wage gap.
3. **Permanent Partial Disability (PPD)** — the big one for settlements. Paid when the injury leaves a lasting impairment — a stiff shoulder, a fused disc, a hand that never regains full grip.
4. **Permanent Total Disability (PTD)** — lifetime benefits for catastrophic injuries that end your earning capacity entirely. Rare, and rarely part of a lump-sum discussion.

Most lump-sum settlements are fights over bucket #3. Buckets #1 and #2 are arithmetic — weeks times rate. Bucket #3 is where ratings, schedules, and negotiation live.

## Permanent disability ratings: the multiplier that moves the needle

When treatment ends, a doctor assigns an **impairment rating** — a percentage expressing how much permanent function you lost. A 5% rating on a knee and a 25% rating on a spine are different universes of money. How the rating converts to dollars depends on the state's system:

- **Schedule systems** (used in states like [New York](/attorneys/new-york)) assign fixed values to body parts. New York's "schedule loss of use" (SLU) awards pay a set number of weeks per body part — a total loss of an arm, for instance, is worth 312 weeks of benefits at your compensation rate. Lose 50% use of that arm and you get 50% of 312 weeks. It's a table; you look it up.
- **Whole-person systems** multiply your impairment percentage by a state dollar figure. A worker with a 10% whole-person impairment in a state that values each percentage point at, say, $2,500 receives $25,000 in PPD — before any wage-loss or future-medical add-ons.
- **Wage-loss systems** look at the gap between what you earned before and what you can earn now, and pay a portion of that gap for a set number of weeks.

Run a comparison and the state lottery effect becomes obvious. The *same* 15% impairment can be worth low five figures in one state and deep into six in another, because the multipliers, weekly maximums, and scheduled values are all set by legislatures, not by medicine. This is the single biggest reason two workers with identical injuries compare notes online and conclude one of them got robbed. Usually neither did — they just live under different tables.

## Lump sum vs. open medical: the real settlement tradeoff

When an insurer offers a lump-sum settlement — called a compromise and release, a clincher, or a full and final, depending on the state — they're offering to close the claim entirely in exchange for one payment. The number is roughly: *remaining wage benefits + PPD value + estimated future medical costs, discounted for the insurer's risk and your desire for certainty.*

The tradeoff is stark and worth stating plainly:

- **Take the lump sum:** you get cash now, no more approvals, no more surveillance of your medical appointments. But if the injury flares up in three years and needs surgery, that's your bill — Medicare set-asides aside, the claim is closed.
- **Keep the claim open:** medical stays covered as long as it's related and authorized. But every treatment needs approval, the checks keep coming only while you qualify, and the insurer keeps a file open on you.

Neither choice is universally right. Workers with injuries that are genuinely stable and done healing often do better taking the money. Workers with hardware in their spine, progressive conditions, or a real chance of future surgery often regret closing. The deciding question isn't "how much" — it's "how finished is this injury, really?"

## Why workers' comp lawyers cost less than you think

Here's a pricing fact that surprises people used to personal injury math: workers' comp attorney fees are **capped by statute** in most states, and the caps are low. Common caps run 10–20% of the recovery — 15% is typical in many states — and the fee usually has to be approved by a workers' comp judge. Compare that to the standard 33–40% contingency fee in a [personal injury](/personal-injury-lawyer) case (see the math on [how contingency fees actually work](/blog/personal-injury-lawyer-contingency-fee-math)), and comp representation is roughly half the price.

That cap structure changes the economics of hiring one. On a $60,000 settlement, a 15% fee is $9,000. Insurers' first offers on PPD-heavy claims routinely come in 30–50% below what the rating math supports — a $20,000–$30,000 haircut on that same $60,000 claim. The fee pays for itself whenever the attorney moves the number more than the percentage they take, which is most of the time on any claim with a real impairment rating.

## What actually moves a settlement up or down: five levers

Strip away the anecdotes and nearly every settlement number traces back to five inputs:

1. **Average weekly wage.** Higher earners get bigger wage benefits, period. And AWW disputes are common — insurers sometimes compute it from base pay only, leaving out overtime and bonuses the law says should count. A $150/week AWW error, over 20 weeks of TTD, is $3,000 gone before negotiations even start.
2. **The impairment rating.** A second medical opinion that moves a rating from 8% to 12% can add five figures in a whole-person state. Ratings are opinions, not measurements — which is exactly why they're contested.
3. **Future medical exposure.** A 35-year-old with a lumbar fusion faces decades of potential follow-up care. Insurers price that exposure into settlements; workers who can document it (future surgery recommendations, lifetime medication needs) price it higher.
4. **Wage-loss duration.** Every additional week of documented disability is another week at the two-thirds rate. Gaps in treatment get weaponized here — more on that below.
5. **State.** As shown above, the same injury in [California](/attorneys/california), [Florida](/attorneys/florida), and [Texas](/attorneys/texas) can produce wildly different numbers. Texas is the famous outlier: it's the only state where most private employers can legally opt out of workers' comp entirely ("non-subscribers"), which means an injured Texas worker at a non-subscribing employer may be outside the comp system altogether — and into a different kind of claim.

## Typical settlement ranges by injury type (and what they hide)

Published analyses of closed claims consistently show the same pattern: soft-tissue injuries (sprains, strains) settle lowest, often in the **$10,000–$25,000** range; fractures and herniated discs land in the **$30,000–$75,000** band; injuries requiring surgery or producing permanent restrictions push into **$75,000–$150,000+**; and catastrophic injuries (amputations, severe brain trauma) run far higher, sometimes into seven figures.

These ranges are useful as a smell test and dangerous as a target. They hide the two variables that matter most: your state's multiplier table and your wage. A $20,000 settlement for a sprained ankle at a $40,000 salary in a low-multiplier state can be perfectly fair, while the same $20,000 for a herniated disc at a $90,000 salary in a high-multiplier state can be a bad deal. Compare your number to your state's formula, not to someone's number on the internet.

## Five mistakes that quietly shrink the check

1. **Late reporting.** Every state has a reporting deadline — commonly 30 days, sometimes as short as a few days for written notice. Miss it and the insurer gets its first and best argument: that the injury didn't happen at work.
2. **Gaps in treatment.** Skip three weeks of physical therapy and the insurer's doctor will testify you must have healed — or that something else caused the ongoing pain. Continuity of care is continuity of evidence.
3. **Downplaying symptoms to the doctor.** "I'm fine" goes in the medical record verbatim. The record is what the rating is built on. Accurate, specific symptom reporting isn't complaining — it's documentation.
4. **Social media.** The investigator's photo of you carrying groceries doesn't prove you're uninjured; it proves you carried groceries once, which is all a defense needs to muddy a claim about lifting restrictions.
5. **Returning to full duty too early.** Going back before MMI because the bills are piling up can terminate TTD benefits and hand the insurer an argument that the disability has resolved. Light duty exists for exactly this situation — use it.

## When the formula works against you

Not every injured worker gets the textbook version. Claims get denied — for alleged pre-existing conditions, for injuries "outside the course of employment," for missed deadlines — and denied claims settle, if at all, at a discount, because the worker is negotiating from the weaker position of having to win the claim first. Cumulative-trauma injuries (carpal tunnel, repetitive-strain back injuries) face extra skepticism in several states, with special proof requirements.

And then there's the Texas exception noted above: at a non-subscribing employer, there's no comp claim to settle — the worker's remedy may be a negligence lawsuit against the employer instead, with no damage caps but a burden of proving fault. Same injury, completely different legal universe, determined entirely by which state the warehouse sits in.

The through-line of all of this is the same: workers' comp payouts look arbitrary until you learn the formulas, and then they look like what they are — arithmetic with a negotiation layered on top. Learn the arithmetic first. It's the part nobody can bluff you on.
