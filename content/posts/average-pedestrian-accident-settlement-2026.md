A pedestrian accident settlement is the money a driver (or, more precisely, the driver's insurer) pays after a car hits someone on foot. It is one of the most lopsided negotiations in personal injury law: the person with no steel around them, no airbag, and no crumple zone is negotiating against a company whose entire business is paying less than a claim is worth. Understanding the numbers before you talk to an adjuster is the single biggest advantage you can give yourself.

The reported national average for a pedestrian accident settlement is about **$67,511**, with a median closer to **$30,000**. That gap between the average and the median tells you almost everything about how these cases work: a small number of catastrophic cases drag the average far above what most people actually receive, while the typical claim — a fracture, a surgery, months of physical therapy — lands in the $50,000 to $150,000 band. The range below is not a promise. It is a map of what the same injury tends to be worth, and why two people hit in the same crosswalk can walk away with wildly different checks.

## Pedestrian Accident Settlement Amounts by Injury

The injury is the single biggest driver of value, because it decides the medical bills, the time off work, and the pain-and-suffering multiplier all at once. Here is how reported settlements break down in 2026:

| Injury severity | Typical injuries | Reported settlement range |
|---|---|---|
| Minor | Bruises, sprains, road rash, minor whiplash | $10,000 – $75,000 |
| Moderate | Single fracture, torn ligament, surgery | $50,000 – $250,000 |
| Serious | Multiple fractures, head injury, spinal damage | $250,000 – $1,000,000 |
| Catastrophic | Traumatic brain injury, paralysis, amputation | $1,000,000 – $10,000,000+ |
| Wrongful death | Fatal pedestrian crash | $500,000 – $5,000,000+ |

Two patterns in that table matter more than the numbers themselves. First, the jump from "moderate" to "serious" is where surgery enters the picture — and surgery is the line adjusters use to decide whether a claim is a five-figure file or a six-figure file. A broken leg that heals with a cast settles in a different universe from a broken leg that needs a plate, screws, and a second operation to remove the hardware. Second, the top of every band is set not by the injury alone but by the insurance policy behind it. A $1 million injury against a driver carrying a $25,000 state-minimum policy is, in practice, a $25,000 case unless another source of coverage exists — the driver's umbrella policy, your own uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage, or a third party like a bar that overserved the driver.

## The Settlement Math Insurers Actually Use

Adjusters do not pull a number out of thin air. Most pedestrian claims are valued with a version of the same formula, and knowing it lets you see exactly where your case gains or loses value:

**Settlement ≈ (medical bills + lost wages) × pain-and-suffering multiplier + future costs − fault reduction**

The multiplier is where the argument lives. Minor injuries with a few weeks of treatment typically draw a multiplier of 1.5 to 2. A fracture requiring surgery and months of rehab draws 3 to 4. A permanent injury — a limp that never fully resolves, a brain injury with lasting cognitive effects — can draw 4 to 5 or higher. The multiplier is applied to your "special damages" (the bills and the lost pay), which is why two cases with the same pain can be worth very different amounts: the person with $60,000 in medical bills and a multiplier of 3 is looking at roughly $180,000 in pain and suffering, while the person with $8,000 in bills and a multiplier of 2 is looking at $16,000.

Worked example, with real numbers. Say a pedestrian is hit in a crosswalk and suffers a fractured tibia that needs surgery:

- Emergency room, surgery, and hospital stay: $38,000
- Physical therapy over 5 months: $7,500
- Follow-up imaging and hardware-removal surgery: $9,000
- Lost wages (10 weeks off work at $1,100/week): $11,000
- **Total special damages: $65,500**
- Pain-and-suffering multiplier of 3: $196,500
- **Case value before fault and policy limits: about $262,000**

Now the two things that can shrink that number. If the pedestrian is found 20% at fault — say they were crossing against the signal — a pure comparative negligence state cuts the recovery by 20%, to about $209,600. And if the driver's policy limit is $100,000 and no other coverage exists, the practical settlement ceiling becomes $100,000 no matter what the math says. That is why the first question in any serious pedestrian case is never "how much is it worth?" but "how much insurance is there?"

## Why Pedestrian Cases Settle Higher Than Car-vs-Car Cases

The same crash that would be a fender-bender between two cars is a life-changing event for a person on foot, and the numbers reflect that. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration reported **7,314 pedestrians killed in 2023**, and pedestrian deaths have risen sharply over the past decade even as overall traffic deaths have flattened. Insurers know this, juries know this, and it shows up in the valuations in three specific ways.

First, there is no "low-impact" defense. In a rear-end collision between cars, the insurer's favorite argument is that a 10-mph tap cannot cause a serious injury. That argument evaporates when a 4,000-pound vehicle hits an unprotected body at any speed — the physics do the claimant's arguing for them.

Second, the medical record is usually cleaner. Pedestrian injuries are documented from the first minute: the ambulance, the trauma bay, the CT scan. There is rarely a gap in treatment for the insurer to exploit, because the injuries are too serious to walk away from.

Third, liability is usually clearer. A driver who hits a pedestrian in a marked crosswalk has a hard story to tell a jury, because every state gives pedestrians the right of way in crosswalks. Even outside a crosswalk, drivers owe a duty of care to watch for pedestrians — and a driver who was looking at a phone has no sympathetic version of events.

The one systematic discount in pedestrian cases is the "pedestrian bias" — the quiet assumption, in adjuster files and sometimes in jury rooms, that the person on foot must have done something wrong. It shows up as a fault percentage shaved off the top of every offer. The antidote is evidence gathered early, which is exactly what the first-5-steps section below is for.

## Your First 5 Steps After Being Hit as a Pedestrian

What you do in the first hours decides how much of the settlement math above you actually collect. These five steps are in order for a reason.

**1. Get medical care immediately, even if you feel "okay."** Adrenaline masks fractures and internal injuries for hours. A same-day emergency room visit creates the medical record that ties every later bill to the crash. Waiting three days to see a doctor hands the insurer its best argument: "If you were really hurt, why did you wait?"

**2. Call the police and get the report number.** A police report is the single most valuable document in a pedestrian claim. It records the driver's statement, the location, the crosswalk, and any citation — and it is written by a neutral third party, not by either side's lawyer.

**3. Photograph everything before it disappears.** The crosswalk markings, the traffic signal, the skid marks, the car's damage, your injuries, the street lighting. Road crews repaint crosswalks and weather erases skid marks within days. If a nearby business has a security camera facing the street, note the business name — that footage is usually overwritten within 7 to 30 days.

**4. Do not give a recorded statement to the driver's insurer.** The adjuster will call quickly, sound friendly, and ask you to "just tell us what happened" on tape. Every word becomes a transcript they will use to argue fault. You are not required to give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurer. Give them only the basic facts — your name, the date, the location — and nothing about fault or how you feel.

**5. Preserve the evidence only you can preserve.** Keep every medical bill, every pay stub showing missed work, and a short daily note of your pain and what you could not do — stairs, lifting your child, sleeping through the night. That diary is what turns a multiplier argument from abstract to concrete: it is the difference between "my client suffered" and "my client could not climb the stairs to her own bedroom for eleven weeks."

## Fault Rules That Change Your Pedestrian Accident Settlement

The same crash is worth a different amount in different states, because the fault rule decides how much of your recovery survives your own share of blame. Three systems cover nearly the whole country:

**Pure comparative negligence (California, New York, Florida, and others).** Your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault, with no cutoff. If you are 40% at fault, you still recover 60% of your damages. This is the most forgiving system for pedestrians, and it is one reason California and New York pedestrian cases tend to settle at the higher end of the national ranges.

**Modified comparative negligence, 51% bar (Texas, Illinois, and most states).** You recover only if you are 50% or less at fault (in some states, less than 51%). Cross that line and you recover nothing. This is where the pedestrian-bias discount becomes dangerous: an adjuster who can push your fault share from 45% to 55% does not just cut your settlement — they eliminate it. That is why the evidence in step 3 above matters so much in these states.

**Contributory negligence (Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia, Washington D.C.).** The harshest rule in American law: if you are even 1% at fault, you recover nothing. In these states, a pedestrian case lives or dies on proving the driver was entirely responsible, and the first-72-hours evidence gathering is not a nice-to-have — it is the whole case.

New York adds one more wrinkle worth knowing: it is a no-fault state, which means your own auto policy's personal injury protection can pay your initial medical bills even though you were on foot. That coverage exists, it is underused, and it is worth asking about before any bills go to collections.

## How Long a Pedestrian Accident Settlement Takes

The timeline follows the medical treatment, not the calendar. Minor-injury claims with a few weeks of treatment commonly settle in **3 to 6 months**. Moderate cases — a fracture, a surgery, several months of therapy — typically take **6 to 18 months**, because no sensible claimant settles before knowing whether the injury has fully healed or will need another operation. Serious and catastrophic cases can take **18 months to 3 years**, and the ones that go to trial longer still.

There is a trap in that timeline that costs pedestrians real money: settling before maximum medical improvement. Maximum medical improvement is the point where your doctor can say, with confidence, what your recovery will look like — what is permanent, what will need future surgery, what it will cost. Settle before that point and the future surgery is on you, because a signed release is final. Insurers know this and push early offers precisely when the medical picture is still unclear. The first offer is almost always low — it is a test of whether you know what the case is worth, not a valuation of the case.

The statute of limitations is the hard edge on the other side. Most states give you **two years** from the date of the crash to file a lawsuit, but some give only one, and claims against a government entity (a city bus, a poorly designed crosswalk) can have notice deadlines as short as **90 days**. The clock starts on the day of the crash, not the day you feel ready to deal with it.

## What Lowers a Pedestrian Settlement — and How to Fight It

Five factors do most of the damage to pedestrian settlements, and all five are manageable if you see them coming.

**Shared fault.** The adjuster's first move is almost always to blame the pedestrian: crossing outside the crosswalk, looking at a phone, wearing dark clothes at night. The counter is the police report, the crosswalk camera, and the driver's own duty to watch for pedestrians — a duty that exists even when the pedestrian made a mistake.

**Gaps in medical treatment.** Missing two weeks of physical therapy reads, in an adjuster's file, as "the injury must have healed." Keep every appointment, and if you must miss one, reschedule it the same week and tell your doctor why.

**Pre-existing injuries.** A bad knee from an old sports injury does not cancel a new fracture, but the insurer will try to blame everything on the old injury. The legal rule is the "eggshell plaintiff" doctrine: the driver takes the pedestrian as they find them. A doctor's note separating the new injury from the old one is worth more than any argument.

**Low policy limits.** The most common reason a strong case settles for less than it is worth. The fix is looking beyond the driver's policy: your own uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage, the driver's employer if they were working, a bar under a dram shop law if the driver was overserved, or the city if the crosswalk itself was defectively designed.

**The recorded statement.** Already covered above, but it deserves repeating: the single most expensive sentence in a pedestrian case is usually the one the victim says to the other driver's adjuster in the first week, before they know the full extent of their injuries.

## The Bottom Line on Pedestrian Accident Settlements

A pedestrian accident settlement in 2026 most often lands between **$50,000 and $150,000** for a typical claim, with minor injuries settling for $10,000 to $75,000 and catastrophic injuries reaching seven figures. The national reported average of about $67,511 and the median near $30,000 are useful anchors — but your case is not an average. It is a specific injury, a specific set of medical bills, a specific fault rule in your state, and a specific insurance policy behind the driver. Those four numbers, run through the multiplier math above, are what your case is actually worth.

If you were hit as a pedestrian, the highest-value thing you can do this week is not to negotiate — it is to protect the claim: get the medical record started, get the police report, photograph the scene, and say nothing on tape to the other driver's insurer. The negotiation comes later, and it goes far better when it starts from a file full of evidence instead of a file full of gaps.
