Your contractor walked off the job with $6,800 of your deposit. Your former landlord kept the security deposit over "cleaning fees" that don't exist. A client owes you $9,500 and stopped answering the phone. The injury is real, the amount is real — and the first question everyone asks is the wrong one. They ask "do I have a case?" The question that actually decides what happens next is **"does the math work?"**

Because here is the uncomfortable arithmetic of American civil justice: a lawyer who bills $300 an hour and spends 25 hours on your $6,800 dispute has just turned your winning case into a net loss. You can be completely right and still lose money. Small claims court exists precisely for this gap — but it has its own traps, limits, and arithmetic. This guide works the numbers so you can decide in an hour instead of learning them over six months of bills.

## 1. The break-even formula: when a lawyer costs more than the claim

Strip away the jargon and every dispute reduces to one equation:

**Net recovery = (claim value × probability of winning) − total cost of pursuing it**

Run it for a $7,500 dispute — an unpaid invoice, say — with an 80% chance of winning:

**With a lawyer at $300/hour.** A straightforward civil matter takes 20–40 hours of lawyer time from demand letter through judgment: intake and research (3–5 hours), drafting the complaint and serving it (3–4), discovery — document requests, interrogatories, maybe one deposition (8–15), motions and settlement negotiation (4–8), and a one-day trial with preparation (6–10). Call it 30 hours. That's $9,000 in fees, plus $500–$1,500 in filing fees, service, and court costs. Total: roughly **$10,000–$10,500** to recover $7,500 at 80% odds.

Expected value: ($7,500 × 0.8) − $10,000 = $6,000 − $10,000 = **−$4,000.** You are paying four thousand dollars for the privilege of being right.

**In small claims court, self-filed.** Filing fees run $30–$300 depending on the state and claim size. Service of process: $35–$100 through the sheriff or a process server. A few hours of your own time preparing evidence. Total out of pocket: roughly **$100–$400.**

Expected value: ($7,500 × 0.75) − $250 = $5,625 − $250 = **+$5,375.** (I shaved the win probability slightly — self-represented parties lose a few points on procedure, which Section 4 addresses.)

The swing between the two paths on the same dispute is nearly **$9,400**. That is not a rounding error. It is the entire economics of the American small-claims system, and it is why courts in all 50 states maintain a simplified track for smaller disputes.

The rule of thumb that falls out of the math: **if the realistic legal bill exceeds 40% of what you're chasing, full representation is probably a bad investment.** On a $7,500 claim, that means anything above ~$3,000 in fees should push you toward small claims or the middle path in Section 5.

(If you do hire counsel, the fee agreement decides everything — read our breakdown of [the 14 retainer clauses that decide what you'll actually pay](/blog/retainer-agreement-clauses-to-read-before-signing) before you sign anything, and check the [2026 business lawyer cost guide](/blog/how-much-does-business-lawyer-cost-2026) for current hourly rates by market.)

## 2. What small claims court actually costs — and what you get for it

Small claims courts trade formality for speed and cost. The rules of evidence are relaxed, discovery is limited or nonexistent, hearings last 15–60 minutes, and most cases resolve within 30–90 days of filing. Compare that to regular civil court, where the median time from filing to trial runs well over a year.

Real costs, from the verified numbers:

- **Filing fee:** $30–$75 in California, $15–$20 in New York City, $55–$300 in Florida, around $54 in Texas. The fee usually scales with the claim amount.
- **Service of process:** $35–$100. Some courts let you serve by certified mail for under $10.
- **Your time:** 5–15 hours across preparing the claim, organizing evidence, and attending the hearing.
- **Total cash outlay: typically $100–$400.**

What you get: a binding judgment, enforceable like any court judgment — wage garnishment, bank levies, and liens are all available depending on the state. Small claims is not mediation and not a suggestion box. The judge's order has teeth.

What you don't get: in many states, **you cannot bring a lawyer into the courtroom with you.** California, for example, generally bars attorneys from appearing in small claims hearings — parties represent themselves. New York allows lawyers but most parties go without. Texas and Florida allow them. This matters for the decision in Section 4: if your opponent is likely to show up with counsel and your state allows it, the DIY math gets worse.

## 3. The 50-state limit problem: know your ceiling before you file

Every state caps what you can claim in small claims court, and the caps vary by a factor of ten. File above your state's limit and the clerk rejects the claim. File below it when you're owed more, and you **permanently waive the excess** — you cannot collect the rest later.

Verified limits for the states where most of these disputes arise:

| State | Small claims limit | Filing fee (approx.) | Lawyers in court? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tennessee | $25,000 | varies by county | allowed |
| Texas | $20,000 (justice court) | ~$54 | allowed |
| Georgia | $15,000 (magistrate court) | $45–$75 | allowed |
| California | $12,500 (individuals); $6,250 (businesses) | $30–$75 | generally no |
| Pennsylvania | $12,000 | $40–$100 | allowed |
| New York | $10,000 (NYC); $5,000/$3,000 elsewhere | $15–$20 | allowed, rarely used |
| Illinois | $10,000 | $20–$250 | allowed |
| Florida | $8,000 | $55–$300 | allowed |
| [Maryland](/attorneys/maryland) | $5,000 | varies by county | allowed |
| Kentucky | $2,500 (lowest in the country) | varies | allowed |
| Arizona | $3,500 | $15–$65 | allowed |

Source cross-checks: California's $12,500 individual / $6,250 business split, Texas's $20,000 justice-court ceiling, Florida's $8,000 county-court cap, and New York's $10,000 NYC limit are consistent across current 2026 references. Limits move when legislatures act — **confirm your exact number with the court clerk before filing.**

Now the waive-the-excess math, because this is where people quietly lose real money. Suppose you're in Florida (cap $8,000) and a contractor owes you $11,000:

- **Option A — small claims, waive $3,000.** Cost ~$250. Win probability ~75%. Expected value: ($8,000 × 0.75) − $250 = **$5,750.**
- **Option B — county court for the full $11,000 with a lawyer.** Fees ~$6,000–$9,000 on a contested matter. Win probability ~85%. Expected value: ($11,000 × 0.85) − $7,500 = $9,350 − $7,500 = **$1,850.**

Even after waiving $3,000, small claims wins by nearly $4,000 in expected value. But flip the numbers — a $20,000 claim in Florida — and waiving $12,000 to stay in small claims becomes madness. The general rule: **waiving makes sense when the gap between what you're owed and the cap is under ~$2,000–$3,000.** Beyond that, the forfeited amount starts exceeding what a lawyer would cost.

Note the state quirks that change the calculus. In California, a business entity is capped at $6,250 (half the individual limit) and cannot file more than two claims over $2,500 per year. In North Carolina, the limit is set county by county between $5,000 and $10,000. In New Hampshire, claims over $5,000 trigger mandatory mediation. Your ceiling is local — treat any national table as a starting point, not gospel.

## 4. When the DIY math breaks: five cases where you still need a lawyer

Small claims court is a machine built for simple, document-driven disputes: unpaid invoices, security deposits, minor property damage, straightforward breach of contract. Step outside that envelope and the expected-value math from Section 1 reverses. Hire counsel when:

**1. The other side has a lawyer and your state allows them in the courtroom.** A represented opponent in Texas or Florida small claims will file motions, object to your evidence, and exploit every procedural gap. Your 75% DIY win probability can drop to 50% or worse. A $300/hour lawyer for 5 hours of coaching (Section 5) or limited-scope representation changes that back.

**2. There's a counterclaim.** Nothing transforms a $5,000 small-claims case faster than the defendant counterclaiming for $15,000 — suddenly you're the one exposed, possibly above the small-claims ceiling, in a forum with relaxed rules that now works against you. If the relationship has any plausible counterclaim (contractor disputes and landlord fights are the classics), get a consultation before you file.

**3. The facts need expert testimony.** A cracked foundation, a misdiagnosed condition, a defective product that failed under load — if winning requires someone with credentials to explain *why* something failed, small claims' informal format works against you. Judges in small claims give less weight to expert reports submitted on paper than a full trial court gives to live expert testimony.

**4. You need something other than money.** Small claims courts award dollars, not orders. If you need the neighbor to stop the runoff, the ex-partner to return the equipment, or the company to honor the warranty with actual repairs, you need injunctive or specific relief — that's regular civil court, and that's lawyer territory.

**5. The defendant is judgment-proof or out of state.** A judgment against someone with no wages to garnish and no assets to levy is a framed piece of paper. And suing an out-of-state defendant raises jurisdiction questions that small claims clerks won't help you untangle. A lawyer's pre-filing asset and jurisdiction check — often part of a single consultation — costs $200–$400 and can save you the entire filing.

For injury-related disputes specifically, the calculus differs because contingency fees change the math entirely — see our [personal injury lawyer contingency fee breakdown](/blog/personal-injury-lawyer-contingency-fee-math), and if the dispute started with a crash, the [after-accident legal checklist](/blog/what-to-do-after-car-accident-legal-checklist) covers the evidence that decides those cases.

## 5. The middle path: unbundled legal coaching for $300–$800

The most underused option in American law is also the cheapest: **limited-scope (unbundled) representation.** Instead of hiring a lawyer for the whole case, you hire one for a slice — and small claims is where this model shines.

What lawyers sell by the slice:

- **Pre-filing case review ($200–$400):** A one-hour consultation where the lawyer reads your evidence, tells you your real odds, flags the counterclaim risk from Section 4, and confirms the correct defendant and court. This single hour has the highest ROI of any legal dollar in small disputes.
- **Demand letter on letterhead ($300–$600):** A lawyer's letterhead changes settlement psychology. Our experience-backed estimate: roughly a third of small commercial disputes settle within two weeks of a credible demand letter, because the recipient's mental math suddenly includes *their* legal costs.
- **Hearing preparation coaching ($300–$500):** The lawyer organizes your evidence into a three-ring narrative, rehearses your 5-minute opening, and tells you which three documents the judge will actually look at. In courts that bar lawyers from the hearing room, this is the closest thing to having one.
- **Document review ($150–$300):** The lawyer reviews your claim form, evidence packet, or settlement agreement before you file or sign.

Total for a fully coached DIY case: **$500–$1,200** — against $8,000–$10,000 for full representation and $100–$400 for pure DIY. Your win probability moves most of the way from the DIY 75% toward the represented 85%, while costs stay in the small-claims universe. Ask for this explicitly: "Do you offer limited-scope or unbundled services for small claims matters?" About half of solo and small-firm practitioners do; the ones who don't will often refer you to one who does.

One caution from the [retainer-clause guide](/blog/retainer-agreement-clauses-to-read-before-signing): even a limited-scope engagement needs a written agreement spelling out exactly what the lawyer is and isn't doing. "Coaching" without a defined scope is how clients end up surprised when the lawyer won't appear at the hearing.

## 6. The demand letter test: the cheapest win is the one you never file

Before either path, run the cheapest play in the book. A formal written demand — sent certified mail, return receipt — costs under $15 and resolves a meaningful share of small disputes without any court at all. The structure that works:

1. **The facts in three sentences.** What was agreed, what was paid, what wasn't delivered. Dates and amounts, no adjectives.
2. **The number.** The exact dollar figure, with a one-line calculation showing how you got there.
3. **The deadline.** Fourteen days is standard. Thirty for larger commercial disputes.
4. **The consequence.** "If I do not receive payment by [date], I will file in [county] small claims court." Name the court. Specificity signals seriousness.

Why it works is pure economics: the recipient does their own version of the Section 1 math. A $6,800 demand backed by a credible filing threat forces them to weigh $6,800 against their own $3,000–$5,000 defense cost plus a day in court. Rational defendants settle somewhere in the middle — which is why the demand should ask for the full amount; it gives you room to land at 70–80% and still beat both litigation paths on expected value.

Document everything from this point forward. Every email, every text, every receipt goes in one folder. Small claims judges decide cases on paper in minutes — the party with the organized exhibit packet wins the credibility contest before anyone speaks. Photograph defective work, save the contract, export the text thread. This is the evidence work that no lawyer can do retroactively, which is why it matters whether you hire one or not.

## 7. The appeal and counterclaim traps

Two structural features of small claims catch first-timers:

**Appeals are asymmetric — and sometimes one-sided.** In many states, only the *defendant* can appeal a small claims judgment to a higher court for a brand-new trial (trial de novo). If you file as plaintiff and lose, that's often the end. If you win and the defendant appeals, you may find yourself in regular civil court, against a lawyer, with the relaxed small-claims rules gone. California follows this pattern. Know your state's appeal rule before you file, because it determines whether your "cheap" case has a trapdoor into an expensive one.

**Filing starts a clock you can't stop.** Once served, many defendants lawyer up and counterclaim — sometimes strategically, to push the total exposure above the small-claims limit and force the case into regular court, where their lawyer's hourly billing becomes your problem. The pre-filing consultation in Section 5 exists largely to answer one question: *what's the worst thing they can file back?* If the answer is ugly, you want to know before the clerk stamps your claim, not after.

**Collecting is a second project.** Winning the judgment and collecting the money are different tasks. Courts don't collect for you. Budget for post-judgment enforcement: bank levies ($100–$300 in fees per attempt), wage garnishment filings, and judgment liens. Against a cooperative defendant, payment arrives in weeks. Against a stubborn one, enforcement can cost another $500–$1,500 and several months. Factor a 10–20% collection haircut into the Section 1 formula when the defendant looks difficult.

## 8. The 60-second decision test

Answer these in order. Stop at the first "yes":

1. **Is the amount under your state's small-claims limit?** No → you need regular court and almost certainly a lawyer. Start with [attorneys in Texas](/attorneys/texas), [California](/attorneys/california), or [Florida](/attorneys/florida) to find counsel by practice area.
2. **Is it a simple money dispute with documents to prove it?** No (you need an order, not dollars, or you need expert testimony) → lawyer.
3. **Could the other side plausibly counterclaim above the limit?** Yes → get the $200–$400 pre-filing consultation first.
4. **Is the defendant collectible and in-state?** No → think hard before spending even the filing fee.
5. **All no?** File in small claims, send the demand letter first, and consider $500–$1,000 of unbundled coaching. Your expected value beats full representation by thousands.

The legal system prices certainty by the hour, and for small disputes that price exceeds the prize. Small claims court is the market correction — a forum where the economics work for the person who's owed $8,000 instead of the person billing $400 an hour. Use the math, respect the traps, and you'll spend hundreds to recover thousands instead of the other way around.
