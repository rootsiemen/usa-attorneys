If a drunk driver hit you, your case is not a normal car accident claim. The driver made a choice — to drink, then to drive — and the law treats that choice as a separate, punishable wrong. That one fact changes everything about what your case is worth: liability is rarely disputed, punitive damages enter the picture, and a bar or restaurant that overserved the driver can become a second defendant. The short answer: published settlement guides put the typical drunk-driving injury settlement between **$50,000 and $125,000**, with severe-injury cases regularly passing **$500,000** and wrongful-death cases reaching into the millions — and the cases at the top of those ranges almost always have punitive damages, a dram shop claim, or both stacked on top of the ordinary medical-bill math.

## The Short Answer: What Is a Drunk Driver Settlement Worth?

A drunk-driving settlement has three layers, and most victims only ever hear about the first one:

1. **Compensatory damages** — the same as any car crash: medical bills, lost wages, pain and suffering, property damage.
2. **Punitive damages** — extra money designed to punish the driver, available in drunk-driving cases in most states because the conduct was reckless, not merely careless.
3. **Dram shop liability** — a claim against the bar, restaurant, or store that served alcohol to a visibly intoxicated driver. Forty-three states have dram shop laws, and a second defendant often means a second insurance policy.

The scale of the problem explains why courts and insurers take these cases seriously. In 2023, **12,429 people were killed in alcohol-impaired driving crashes** in the United States — about **30% of all traffic fatalities**, according to NHTSA. A driver at a blood alcohol concentration of **0.15%** — not quite twice the 0.08% legal limit — is approximately **12 times more likely to crash** than a sober driver. Those numbers are why an adjuster who would fight a fender-bender for months often settles a drunk-driving claim faster, and for more.

## How Much Is a Drunk Driver Settlement? Amounts by Injury

Settlement ranges for drunk-driving crashes run higher than comparable sober-driver crashes at every injury level. The table below reflects ranges reported across published settlement guides — treat them as brackets, not promises; your medical bills, your state's caps, and the available insurance set the real ceiling.

| Injury | Typical settlement range | What pushes it up |
|---|---|---|
| Soft tissue / whiplash | $15,000 – $50,000 | ER visit, documented PT, missed work |
| Broken bones (arm, leg, ribs) | $50,000 – $150,000 | Surgery, hardware, months off work |
| Broken femur / pelvis | $100,000 – $400,000 | Multiple surgeries, mobility limits |
| Traumatic brain injury | $150,000 – $500,000+ | Cognitive deficits, permanent effects |
| Spinal cord injury | $300,000 – $1,500,000+ | Paralysis, lifetime care costs |
| Wrongful death | $500,000 – $3,000,000+ | Age, dependents, earning capacity |

The same injury in a sober-driver crash typically settles for less, because the multiplier is lower. Personal injury settlements are often built on a multiplier of medical bills — commonly around **2x** for a standard car accident. In drunk-driving cases, guides report multipliers of **3x to 5x or higher**, before punitive damages are even added. Worked out: $50,000 in medical bills at a 2x multiplier is a $100,000 settlement; at a 4x multiplier plus punitive damages, the same bills support **$250,000 to $400,000 or more**.

## Why Drunk Driving Cases Pay More: Three Multipliers

### 1. Liability is nearly impossible to dispute

A BAC of 0.08% or higher, an arrest record, and a DUI conviction make it nearly impossible for the insurance company to argue the drunk driver was not at fault. In most states, driving over the legal limit automatically establishes negligence. That removes the insurer's favorite tactic — arguing you were partly to blame — and it removes months of delay from the negotiation.

### 2. Punitive damages stack on top

Punitive damages are not compensation for your bills. They exist to punish the driver and deter the next one. Drunk driving is one of the most common situations where courts award them, and they can **double or triple** your total recovery. The strongest punitive cases share one or more of these facts: a very high BAC (0.15% or above), prior DUI convictions, driving on a suspended license, or fleeing the scene. Roughly **30 states cap punitive damages**, so the state where the crash happened matters — a case worth $400,000 in one state can be capped lower in another. Check the [DUI attorney](/dui-attorney) page for how these rules play out, and the [personal injury lawyer](/personal-injury-lawyer) page for the damages categories that sit underneath the punitive layer.

### 3. A second defendant means a second policy

Most drunk drivers carry only minimum coverage — **$25,000 to $50,000 per person** in most states. That ceiling is often lower than a single ER bill. A dram shop claim against the bar that overserved the driver opens a commercial liquor-liability policy, which is typically far larger. It also opens the employer's policy if the driver was drinking at a work event or driving for work. One defendant with a $25,000 policy is a small case; the same crash with a bar's commercial policy behind it is a different case entirely.

## Three Worked Examples: The Math, Step by Step

**Example 1 — Broken leg, moderate BAC.** Medical bills $38,000, lost wages $12,000, driver BAC 0.11%, no prior DUI. Compensatory damages at a 3x multiplier on medicals: $114,000, plus $12,000 lost wages, plus pain and suffering folded into the multiplier. Punitive damages modest — one-time offender, BAC just over the limit — add perhaps $25,000 to $50,000. Realistic settlement: **$150,000 to $200,000**.

**Example 2 — Traumatic brain injury, high BAC.** Medical bills $210,000, future care estimated at $180,000, driver BAC 0.19% with a prior DUI conviction. Compensatory damages: $390,000 in economic losses plus pain and suffering at a 4x multiplier on medicals. Punitive damages: strong — repeat offender, nearly two and a half times the limit — add $300,000 to $500,000. The bar that served him for six hours after he was visibly drunk adds a dram shop claim against a commercial policy. Realistic settlement: **$900,000 to $1,500,000**.

**Example 3 — Wrongful death, minimum policy driver.** A family loses a 34-year-old parent. The driver carries a $25,000 minimum policy and has no assets. The criminal case ends in a conviction. The civil case looks small — until the investigation finds the driver was overserved at a bar that kept serving after last call, and the driver's employer had sent him to a client dinner with an open bar. The dram shop claim and the employer claim turn a $25,000 policy-limits case into a **seven-figure settlement**. This is why the investigation matters more than the first offer: the first offer in a case like this is usually the driver's $25,000 policy limit, presented as if it were the whole case.

## Punitive Damages: The Extra Layer, State by State

Punitive damages are where drunk-driving cases separate from ordinary crashes — but they are also where state law bites hardest. The rules fall into three rough groups:

| State group | How punitive damages work |
|---|---|
| No cap, or DUI exemption | Some states put no cap on punitive damages at all; others exempt DUI cases from the cap that applies to ordinary cases |
| Capped by ratio | Many states cap punitive damages at a multiple of compensatory damages (commonly 2x or 3x) or a flat dollar ceiling |
| Capped or restricted | About 30 states cap the amount juries can award, and a few require a higher burden of proof for punitive awards |

Two practical consequences follow. First, the state where the crash happened can matter as much as the injury itself — the same broken leg with the same BAC can support a meaningfully larger recovery in a no-cap state than in a capped one. Second, punitive damages are almost never paid by the drunk driver's own auto policy; they are paid by the driver personally or, in dram shop cases, by the bar's commercial policy. If the driver has no assets and no dram shop defendant exists, a punitive award can be a paper victory. That is why a good investigation looks for the bar, the employer, and every policy before anyone talks numbers.

## Dram Shop Claims: Suing the Bar That Overserved the Driver

Forty-three states allow you to sue the business that served alcohol to the driver. The claim is not that the bar caused the crash — it is that the bar served someone who was visibly intoxicated, or underage, and that the service was a foreseeable step toward the crash. What makes a dram shop claim strong:

- **Receipts and timestamps** — credit card records showing how many drinks, and when. A tab that runs past last call is powerful evidence.
- **Witnesses** — other patrons or staff who saw the driver stumbling, slurring, or being cut off and then served anyway.
- **Surveillance video** — most bars keep 30 to 90 days of footage. It gets overwritten fast, which is why the preservation letter matters (see the checklist below).
- **The driver's own tab** — in several reported cases, the bar's own point-of-sale records were the best evidence in the case.

Dram shop law varies sharply by state. Texas, for example, allows dram shop claims but requires proof the driver was "obviously intoxicated to the extent that he presented a clear danger to himself and others" — a high bar that turns on witness testimony. Other states are more forgiving. If your crash happened in Texas, start with the [Texas attorneys directory](/attorneys/texas) and a lawyer who has actually filed a dram shop claim there, not one who is learning the statute on your case.

## Where the Money Comes From: Stacking Every Available Policy

A drunk-driving settlement is often assembled from four or five sources, not one. Miss a source and you leave money on the table:

1. **The drunk driver's liability policy** — often the minimum, $25,000 to $50,000 per person.
2. **Your own uninsured/underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage** — kicks in when the drunk driver has no insurance or not enough. This is frequently the largest available policy in the case, and many victims never think to claim it.
3. **Your collision and medical payments coverage** — pays your car repairs and immediate medical bills while the liability claim is pending.
4. **The bar's liquor-liability policy** — via a dram shop claim; commercial limits are typically $1 million.
5. **The employer's policy** — if the driver was on the job, at a work event, or driving a company vehicle.

One more source victims forget: **health insurance liens**. If your health insurer paid for treatment after the crash, it almost certainly has a right to be reimbursed from your settlement. A lawyer negotiates those liens down — often by 30% to 50% — and that negotiation is pure money back in your pocket. ## Criminal Case vs. Civil Claim: Two Tracks, One Crash

A drunk driver faces two separate cases, and victims who confuse them lose leverage. The **criminal case** is the state against the driver — DUI charges, possible jail time, license suspension. You are a witness, not a party, and you do not control it. The **civil claim** is you against the driver (and the bar, and the employer) for money. You control it, and it runs on a different clock.

Three things to know about how the tracks interact:

- **A criminal conviction helps the civil case enormously.** A guilty plea or conviction is powerful evidence of liability, and in some states it is binding — the insurer cannot relitigate fault.
- **The civil case does not have to wait for the criminal case.** You can file while the criminal case is pending, though some lawyers prefer to wait for the conviction first. Either way, the statute of limitations keeps running — typically **one to six years** depending on the state, and missing it ends the claim permanently.
- **The criminal case pays you nothing.** Fines go to the state. Restitution, where ordered, rarely covers more than a fraction of medical bills. Your compensation comes from the civil claim and the insurance stack, not the courtroom where the driver is sentenced.

If the crash was fatal, the family's claim is a wrongful death case on the civil track, with its own eligibility rules by state — who can file, and what damages are available. ## What to Do in the First 72 Hours After a Drunk Driving Crash

The steps that protect a drunk-driving claim are time-sensitive in a way ordinary crash steps are not — evidence like bar video and the driver's blood test result disappears or degrades within days.

- [ ] **Call 911 and get the police report number.** Tell the responding officer you suspect impairment. The officer's observations and the field sobriety test are the foundation of the liability case.
- [ ] **Get medical care the same day, even if you feel fine.** Adrenaline masks injuries; a same-day ER record ties your injuries to the crash. Gaps in treatment are the adjuster's favorite argument.
- [ ] **Photograph everything** — both cars, the road, skid marks, the driver's behavior if it is safe to do so.
- [ ] **Write down the bar or restaurant name** if you know where the driver was drinking, or where the crash happened near one. Tell your lawyer immediately — the preservation letter for surveillance video must go out within days.
- [ ] **Do not give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurer.** They are building a file against your claim from the first call.
- [ ] **Do not accept the first offer.** In drunk-driving cases the first offer is almost always the driver's minimum policy limit, presented as if it were the whole case. The real money is in the punitive layer, the dram shop claim, and your own UM/UIM coverage — none of which the first offer mentions.
- [ ] **Save every bill, pay stub, and receipt.** Medical bills, prescriptions, rides to appointments, missed work — the settlement multiplier runs on documented numbers.
- [ ] **Talk to a lawyer before the criminal case resolves.** A guilty plea is leverage, but only if your civil claim is positioned to use it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the average settlement for being hit by a drunk driver?

Published settlement guides report a typical range of **$50,000 to $125,000** for injury cases, with severe injury cases often exceeding $500,000. The range is wide because the total depends on injury severity, medical bills, lost wages, whether punitive damages apply, and whether a dram shop claim adds a commercial policy. A minor-injury case against a minimum-policy driver settles near the bottom; a brain injury with a repeat-offender driver and a bar defendant settles near the top.

### Can I sue a drunk driver even if they have no insurance?

Yes — and the claim often still pays. Your own uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage is the first place to look, and it frequently has higher limits than the drunk driver's policy. A dram shop claim against the bar can add a commercial policy. And if the driver was working, the employer's policy may apply. "No insurance" ends the claim only when every one of those doors is closed.

### How are punitive damages calculated in a DUI case?

There is no fixed formula. Juries weigh the driver's BAC, prior DUIs, whether the driver fled or drove on a suspended license, and how egregious the conduct was. As a rule of thumb, published guides report punitive damages doubling or tripling the compensatory total in strong cases — but roughly 30 states cap them, so the state where the crash happened sets the ceiling.

### Can I sue the bar that served the drunk driver?

In 43 states, yes, under dram shop laws. You must show the bar served alcohol to someone who was visibly intoxicated or underage, and that the service contributed to the crash. Receipts, witness testimony, and surveillance video are the key evidence — and the video is usually overwritten within 30 to 90 days, so the preservation letter has to go out fast.

### How long do I have to file a drunk driving injury claim?

Every state imposes a statute of limitations, typically **one to six years** after the crash. The criminal case does not pause that clock. File early: evidence degrades, witnesses forget, and bar video gets deleted while the criminal case is still working its way through court.

### Will the driver's criminal conviction help my civil case?

Almost always. A guilty plea or conviction is strong — sometimes binding — evidence that the driver was negligent, which removes the insurer's main defense. It also strengthens the punitive damages argument, because the court has already found the conduct criminal.

## The Bottom Line

A drunk-driving case is worth more than the same crash with a sober driver, and the difference is not a rounding error — it is a second and third layer of recovery that most victims never hear about. The compensatory damages pay your bills. The punitive damages punish the choice to drink and drive. The dram shop claim reaches the business that profited from the overservice. And your own UM/UIM coverage catches what the drunk driver's minimum policy cannot. The cases that settle at the top of the ranges in this article are not the ones with the worst injuries — they are the ones where every layer was investigated, documented, and claimed before the evidence went stale. If you were hit by a drunk driver, start with the checklist above, preserve the bar video now, and talk to a [DUI attorney](/dui-attorney) or [personal injury lawyer](/personal-injury-lawyer) who has actually filed punitive and dram shop claims — not one who will settle for the first policy-limits offer and call it a win.
