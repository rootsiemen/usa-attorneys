A fully loaded semi weighs 80,000 pounds. When it hits your car, the crash is not just bigger — it runs on a different clock. The trucking company can have a rapid-response team at the scene within hours, and the electronic evidence that proves what happened can be overwritten in weeks. That is why **what to do after a truck accident** is a question with a deadline attached to every answer. Roughly 500,000 crashes involving large trucks happen in the United States every year, and about 5,000 of them are fatal. The people who recover the most are rarely the ones with the worst injuries — they are the ones who did the right things in the first 72 hours.

This checklist runs in order: the first 10 minutes, the scene, the medical visit, the evidence that disappears, and the three days that follow. Print it, save it, and if you are reading this after a crash, start at the step that matches where you are right now.

## The first 10 minutes: safety, 911, and silence

Your first job is not your claim. It is staying alive and keeping anyone else alive.

1. **Get out of traffic if you can.** Turn on your hazard lights. If the car still moves, pull it off the travel lanes — a stopped car behind a jackknifed trailer is a second crash waiting to happen. Stay clear of the truck's blind spots and away from spilled cargo or fuel.
2. **Call 911.** Tell the dispatcher there are injuries if there are, and that a commercial truck is involved. That call creates the first official record of the crash, and it gets police to the scene. Ask the responding officer for the crash report number before you leave — that number is how your lawyer pulls the full report later.
3. **Accept the medical evaluation.** Let paramedics examine you even if you feel fine. Adrenaline masks pain for hours, and the injuries that cost the most in truck cases — internal bleeding, traumatic brain injury, herniated discs — often show no symptoms at the scene. Refusing treatment at the scene becomes a line in the insurer's file: "declined care, no visible injury."
4. **Say almost nothing about fault.** Exchange the information the law requires — name, insurance, license — and stop there. Do not apologize, do not guess what happened, and do not say "I'm okay" to anyone from the trucking company. Every one of those sentences resurfaces later, quoted back at you.

Federal rules require the truck driver to switch on hazard flashers immediately and set out warning triangles within 10 minutes (49 CFR 392.22). If the driver did not do that, note it — it is a small detail that tells a jury something real about how the carrier operates.

## What to photograph and record at the scene

A car crash has two vehicles. A truck crash has a company behind the truck, and the evidence that matters most is the evidence that identifies that company. Photograph everything you safely can, and collect the items on this list:

- **All vehicles and their positions** — wide shots showing the whole scene, then close shots of the damage on every side.
- **The truck's cab door** — the carrier name, USDOT number, and MC number are printed there. Photograph them; do not trust your memory.
- **Both license plates** — the tractor *and* the trailer. They often belong to different owners, and the trailer's owner can be a second liable party.
- **The trailer unit number and any cargo labels** — hazmat placards, refrigeration unit numbers, shipping logos.
- **Skid marks, debris field, road conditions, traffic signals, and weather** — the physical story of the crash before tow trucks erase it.
- **Your visible injuries** — photograph them now and again over the following days, because bruising and swelling change.
- **The driver** — name, CDL number, employer, and insurance information. Also ask for the name of the company that owns the trailer if it differs from the tractor's carrier.
- **Witnesses** — names and phone numbers. Independent witnesses are the single most persuasive evidence in a disputed-liability case, and they drive away.
- **The police officer** — name, badge number, agency, and the crash report number.

The difference between a truck case and a car case starts here. In a car crash, the other driver's insurance is the whole story. In a truck crash, the liable parties can include the driver, the carrier, the trailer owner, the cargo loader, and the maintenance company — and you only find them by recording the identifiers at the scene. Our comparison of [truck accident vs. car accident lawsuits](/blog/truck-accident-vs-car-accident-lawsuit-differences) breaks down exactly why the two cases are built so differently.

## The medical paper trail: why "I feel fine" costs cases

Go to the emergency room or urgent care the same day, even if you walked away from the crash. This is not optional, and it is not about being dramatic. It is about the two things that decide the value of a truck accident claim: the medical record and the gap.

- **The record links the injury to the crash.** A same-day ER visit creates a timestamped document that says "this injury happened in this crash." Wait a week, and the insurer's argument writes itself: something else must have caused it.
- **The gap is a discount.** Adjusters measure the days between the crash and the first treatment, and every day costs money. A 72-hour gap routinely becomes a 20–30% reduction in the settlement conversation — before anyone has even discussed fault.
- **Hidden injuries are the expensive ones.** A concussion, a slow internal bleed, a disc injury that only shows up on an MRI — these are the injuries that turn a $15,000 case into a $150,000 one, and they are the injuries people miss when they skip the doctor because they "feel fine."

Keep every appointment after the first visit, follow the discharge instructions exactly, and keep a simple symptom journal: pain levels, sleep, missed work, activities you could not do. Then keep every bill, receipt, and pharmacy record in one folder — medical records, itemized bills, out-of-pocket costs, repair estimates, rental car invoices, and proof of lost wages. That folder is the raw material of your settlement demand. ## The evidence that disappears: ELD, black box, and the spoliation letter

This is the part of a truck crash that has no equivalent in a car crash, and it is the part most victims never hear about until it is too late.

A commercial truck carries several recorders that a car does not. The **electronic logging device (ELD)** records the driver's hours of service — whether he was over his legal driving limit when he hit you. The **engine control module (ECM)**, the truck's "black box," records speed, braking, and throttle in the seconds before impact. The carrier holds driver qualification files, inspection and maintenance records, dispatch communications, and dash-cam footage. Any one of these can prove the crash was not an accident but a violation.

Here is the problem: federal rules require carriers to keep driver logs for only **six months** (49 CFR 395.8), and the black-box data can be overwritten in a matter of weeks — sometimes days — as the truck keeps running routes. Dash-cam footage is routinely wiped on a 30-day cycle. The carrier is under no obligation to preserve any of it unless someone tells them to, in writing.

That written notice is called a **spoliation letter** (also called a preservation letter). It is a formal demand that the carrier preserve every record touching the crash — ELD data, ECM downloads, driver logs, maintenance records, dispatch logs, dash-cam video, and the driver's qualification file. Once the carrier receives it, destroying that evidence becomes a legal problem for them instead of a convenience. Without it, the evidence simply ages out, and the case that could have been proven with a download becomes a case argued from memory.

This is the single most time-sensitive step in the whole checklist. It is also the step that is hardest to do alone, because the letter has to go to the right party — the carrier, not just the driver — and it has to name the records precisely. A [truck accident attorney](/truck-accident-attorney) sends this letter as a first-day task. If you are handling the first days yourself, the message is simple: get the letter out within the first week, and treat every day of delay as evidence quietly disappearing.

## The first 72 hours: hour-by-hour checklist

| When | You should do | What is happening on the trucking side |
|---|---|---|
| First hour | Safety, 911, medical evaluation, photos, driver and witness information | Driver must set out warning devices within 10 minutes; carrier's rapid-response team may be dispatched |
| Hours 2–12 | ER or urgent-care visit; start a claim file; photograph injuries again | Insurer opens a file; adjuster assigned; truck may be moved for inspection |
| Day 1 | Notify your own insurer (basics only); do not give a recorded statement to the trucking insurer | Carrier's insurer begins its own investigation; driver gives a statement to the company |
| Day 2 | Follow up on medical care; get the police report number; save every document | ELD and dispatch records still intact — this is the window to preserve them |
| Day 3 | Send (or have a lawyer send) the spoliation letter; keep a symptom journal; do not post about the crash | Dash-cam and black-box data still recoverable; after 30 days, much of it is routinely gone |

Notice what is not on the list: negotiating. Nothing in the first 72 hours should involve agreeing to a number, signing a release, or accepting a check. The trucking insurer's first offer typically arrives fast and low — it is priced for the victim who has not yet seen a doctor, not for the case the evidence will support. A quick settlement in week one is almost always a discount on the case you would have had in month six.

## What not to do after a truck accident

The mistakes after a truck crash cost as much as the crash itself. Most of them feel helpful in the moment.

- **Do not give a recorded statement to the trucking company's insurer.** You are not required to, and the recording exists to lock you into early, incomplete answers about injuries you do not yet fully understand. Give your own insurer the basics your policy requires, and nothing more to the other side.
- **Do not sign anything.** Medical authorizations, releases, settlement documents — the trucking insurer's paperwork is designed to close doors, not open them. A broad medical authorization lets them dig through years of your health history looking for a pre-existing condition to blame.
- **Do not post about the crash on social media.** A photo of you at a family barbecue, a check-in at the gym, a cheerful status update — all of it can be shown to a jury as proof you were not hurt. Assume the insurer is watching your accounts, because it is.
- **Do not throw anything away.** Keep the damaged car parts, the clothes you were wearing, the repair estimates, and every piece of paper from the crash. Physical evidence that looks like junk can decide a case.
- **Do not delay medical care.** Every day between the crash and your first appointment is a discount the insurer will claim. Go the same day.
- **Do not miss the filing deadline.** Every state has a statute of limitations, and truck cases have a second clock — the federal record-retention rules. The legal deadline is the one that kills the case; the evidence deadline is the one that quietly shrinks it.

The honest answer: sooner than you think, and it costs you nothing up front. ## The deadlines that decide your case

Two clocks run after a truck accident, and they run at very different speeds.

**The evidence clock — days to weeks.** ELD data, black-box downloads, dash-cam footage, and driver logs are preserved only if someone demands preservation. The spoliation letter stops this clock. Without it, the most powerful evidence in your case is gone before the insurance negotiation even starts.

**The legal clock — 1 to 3 years, depending on the state.** Most states give you two years from the date of the crash to file a lawsuit: Texas (2 years), California (2 years), Florida (2 years for negligence since the 2023 reform), Louisiana (2 years since July 2024). A few give three. Claims against a government entity — a city-owned truck, for instance — can require written notice within months, sometimes as little as 90 or 180 days. Miss the deadline and the case is over, no matter how strong the evidence was.

The 72-hour checklist exists because the first clock is the one nobody warns you about. Get safe, get treated, get the evidence recorded, get the preservation letter out — and do not sign anything or agree to anything until the medical picture is clear. The crash took seconds. The claim takes months. The first three days decide how much of it survives.
