A denied workers' comp claim feels like a door slamming shut. It is not. Roughly 7 to 10 percent of all workers' compensation claims are denied on the first try, according to National Council on Compensation Insurance data — and a large share of those denials get reversed, reduced, or settled once the worker appeals. The denial letter is the insurance company's first answer, not the system's final one. What decides whether you end up in the group that gets paid is almost never how badly you were hurt. It is how fast you move, what evidence you add, and whether you understand the math the insurer is doing on the other side of the table.

If your workers' comp claim was denied, here is the short version: read the denial letter today, calendar the appeal deadline in it, get your doctor to put the work connection in writing, and file the appeal before the clock runs out. Everything else in this guide is the detail behind those four moves — the timeline, the payout math, and the state deadlines that catch people who wait.

## The Short Answer: What Happens After a Denial

A denial means the insurance carrier decided, for now, not to pay your claim. In nearly every state you have the right to challenge that decision through your state's workers' compensation board or commission, usually by filing a dispute or hearing request within 30 to 90 days of the denial — though some states give as little as 15 days. The appeal path typically runs: reconsideration request to the insurer, formal filing with the state agency, mediation or an informal conference, then a hearing before a workers' comp judge. From filing to decision, expect 3 to 12 months. And the money is real: workers who appeal denied claims with a [workers' compensation attorney](/workers-compensation-attorney) win about 62 percent of the time, versus about 37 percent without one, according to figures published by workers' comp data sites. The single most common reason appeals fail is not weak injuries — it is missed deadlines.

## Why Was Your Claim Denied? The 8 Reasons That Decide Appeals

Every denial letter is supposed to state a specific reason, and that reason shapes your entire strategy. These are the eight the insurers use most, with the share of denials each one roughly accounts for:

**1. The insurer says the injury is not work-related.** This is the single biggest bucket — medical causation disputes account for roughly 23 percent of all denials. The fix is a written opinion from your treating doctor linking the injury directly to your job duties. A one-line note is not enough; you want a letter that names the task, the mechanism of injury, and why the condition follows from it.

**2. You reported the injury late.** Most states require notice to your employer within 30 days, and some give you far less. Late reporting is the easiest denial to issue and one of the hardest to beat without proof — a timestamped text, an email, or a witness who heard you report it the same day.

**3. You missed the filing deadline.** Separate from reporting, every state has a statute of limitations for filing the formal claim — usually one to two years from the date of injury. Miss it and the claim is dead, regardless of how strong the medical evidence is.

**4. No medical evidence on file.** The insurer reviewed the records it had and found no treatment, no diagnosis, or no work restriction. Gaps in treatment are read as proof you were not hurt.

**5. A pre-existing condition.** The carrier argues your back, knee, or shoulder problem existed before the job. The counter is the aggravation rule: in most states, a work incident that worsens a pre-existing condition is still compensable — but only if a doctor says so in writing.

**6. The injury happened off the clock.** Horseplay, commuting, or an injury during a lunch break off premises are classic denial grounds. The line is fact-specific, which is exactly why these denials get overturned at hearing more often than people expect.

**7. A failed drug or alcohol test.** Some states let the insurer deny on a positive post-injury test, but the carrier usually still has to show the substance caused the accident. A positive test alone is not always the end of the claim.

**8. The insurer disputes your employment status.** Independent contractor, temporary worker, or borrowed employee — if the carrier says you were not a covered employee, the whole claim stalls until status is decided. Misclassification fights are common in construction, trucking, and gig work.

Read your letter and match it to one of these eight. If the reason is vague — "insufficient evidence" with no detail — that vagueness is itself useful: request your complete claim file (see below) and make the insurer show its work.

## The Appeal Timeline: Month by Month After a Denial

Here is what the process actually looks like once you file. Timelines vary by state, but this is the shape of it in most of the country:

| Stage | When it happens | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Denial letter arrives | Day 0 | The clock starts. Note the date on the letter, not the day you opened it. |
| Reconsideration request | Days 1–14 | Send the adjuster the missing records, a doctor's letter, or witness statements and ask for reconsideration in writing. Some denials die right here. |
| Formal appeal filed | Within 15–90 days (state deadline) | File the petition, application for adjudication, or hearing request with your state agency. Filing late ends the claim. |
| Mediation / informal conference | Months 1–3 | A mediator or claims examiner tries to settle it. Many cases resolve here — the insurer would rather pay something than fund a hearing. |
| Discovery and medical exams | Months 2–5 | Both sides exchange records; the insurer may order an independent medical exam (IME). Attend it — refusing can sink your case. |
| Hearing before a workers' comp judge | Months 4–9 | Evidence, testimony, and a decision from an administrative law judge or commissioner. |
| Decision and further appeal | Months 6–12 | If you lose, most states allow an appeal to a review board, then to state court. Each level has its own short deadline — often 15 to 30 days. |

Total: **3 to 12 months** from filing to a decision is the honest range. The cases that drag past a year are usually the ones where medical evidence arrived late or a second IME was ordered. The cases that settle fastest are the ones where the worker filed quickly and handed the insurer a doctor's letter it could not ignore.

## What Do Appealed Claims Actually Pay? The Payout Math

This is the part the other guides skip, and it is the part that matters most when you are deciding whether an appeal is worth months of your life. The honest answer: appealed claims usually pay *more* than accepted ones, because the appeal itself changes the negotiation.

**The baseline math.** A workers' comp settlement is built from three numbers: your average weekly wage, your impairment rating, and the weeks of benefits your state assigns to the injured body part. A worker earning $900 a week with a 10 percent impairment rating on a back injury might see a lump-sum offer in the $25,000 to $45,000 range, depending on the state schedule. That is the math for a claim the insurer *accepted*. For a worked example of the full formula, see our [workers' compensation settlement payout guide](/blog/workers-compensation-settlement-payout-math).

**The denial discount — and the appeal premium.** When a claim is denied, the insurer's first settlement number, if it offers one at all, is usually a lowball "nuisance value" offer: $5,000 to $15,000 to make the case go away before a hearing. Workers who file the appeal and show up with new medical evidence routinely see that number multiply. The published win-rate gap tells the story: 62 percent of represented appeals succeed versus 37 percent unrepresented. But even among successful appeals, the *amount* gap matters — an appealed claim that settles at mediation commonly lands at 60 to 80 percent of what the same claim would have paid if accepted on day one, because the insurer prices in the months of delay and the risk it still wins. The exception is the case with a smoking-gun doctor's letter: when causation is nailed down in writing, settlements routinely come back to 90 percent or more of full value.

**Worked example — the $18,000 denial that became $52,000.** A warehouse worker earning $800 a week injures his lower back lifting a pallet. The insurer denies, claiming a pre-existing condition. His doctor writes that the lift aggravated a dormant disc problem — the aggravation rule applies. The insurer's pre-hearing offer: $18,000. At mediation, with the doctor's letter and an MRI showing a new herniation, the case settles for $52,000 — roughly 12 weeks of temporary benefits he should have received, plus a lump sum for a 12 percent impairment rating, minus nothing for the delay, because the insurer wanted to avoid a hearing it expected to lose. The difference between $18,000 and $52,000 was one letter and one filing.

**What an appeal costs you.** Almost nothing out of pocket. Workers' comp attorneys work on contingency — typically 10 to 20 percent of your benefits or settlement, capped by state law — and the consultation is free. If you lose, you generally owe no fee. That is why the 62-versus-37 gap exists: the lawyer's fee comes out of money you would not have recovered alone.

## State Deadlines: The Table That Decides Everything

Deadlines are the most time-sensitive part of this process, and they are the reason "I will deal with it next week" kills more claims than any medical dispute. Appeal windows after a denial vary sharply by state:

| State | Appeal deadline after denial | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Texas | 15 days | Benefit dispute — one of the shortest windows in the country |
| California | 20 days | For utilization-review denials; other disputes run longer, but do not guess |
| New York | 30 days | File with the Workers' Compensation Board |
| Florida | 30 days | Petition for benefits with the state |
| Illinois | 30 days | File with the Workers' Compensation Commission |
| Pennsylvania | 30 days | Appeal to a workers' comp judge |
| Ohio | 30 days | File with the Industrial Commission |
| Georgia | 30 days | Hearing request with the State Board |

These are typical figures, not legal guarantees — the exact deadline is printed in your denial letter, and it is the only number that counts for your case. If your state is not listed, call your state workers' compensation agency or a [workers' compensation attorney](/workers-compensation-attorney) the same day the letter arrives and confirm the number in writing. ## Evidence That Wins Appeals

If you take one thing from this article, take this list. Appeals are won on paper, not on sympathy:

- **The causation letter.** Your treating doctor, in writing, connecting the injury to a specific work task. This is the single highest-value document in any appeal.
- **Your complete claim file.** You have the right to see everything the insurer used: medical records reviewed, statements collected, adjuster notes, and any IME report. Request it in writing the day you get the denial — it often shows the insurer never saw your best evidence.
- **A second medical opinion.** If the insurer's IME doctor says you are fine and your doctor disagrees, a specialist's opinion breaks the tie. Judges weigh treating physicians heavily, but a specialist's report closes the argument.
- **Contemporaneous records.** The incident report, the text you sent your supervisor, the urgent-care visit the same evening. The closer the record is to the injury, the harder it is for the insurer to call it invented.
- **Witness statements.** A coworker who saw the lift, the fall, or the machine malfunction. Written, dated, and signed — not "I can get someone to say something."
- **Wage records.** Pay stubs proving your average weekly wage. Insurers understate this number whenever the file lets them, and every dollar of weekly wage is multiplied across your benefits.

## What To Do While Your Appeal Is Pending

A denial does not mean you stop treatment — in fact, stopping is the worst thing you can do, because the insurer will read the gap as recovery. Keep seeing your doctor and use your health insurance in the meantime; if the appeal succeeds, workers' comp reimburses those costs. Keep a simple log: every appointment, every call with the adjuster, every letter, with dates. Do not give a recorded statement to the insurer without advice, and do not sign a settlement release or a resignation form the adjuster slides across the table — some releases quietly waive your right to appeal at all. If the injury happened at work and you have not yet read the first-steps checklist, our [workplace injury legal checklist](/blog/what-to-do-after-workplace-injury-legal-checklist) covers the evidence you should have gathered from day one. And know this: in most states, your employer cannot legally fire or punish you for filing a claim or appealing a denial. Retaliation happens anyway — document it if it does, because it becomes its own claim.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long do I have to appeal a denied workers' comp claim?**
Usually 30 to 90 days from the denial, but some states give as little as 15 days (Texas) or 20 days (California utilization review). The exact deadline is in your denial letter — calendar it the day the letter arrives.

**Can I appeal a workers' comp denial without a lawyer?**
Yes, you can file the appeal yourself, and many workers do. But the published numbers are stark: represented appeals succeed about 62 percent of the time versus about 37 percent without a lawyer. Because the fee is contingency-based — typically 10 to 20 percent, paid only if you win — most workers lose nothing by getting representation.

**What percentage of denied workers' comp claims are overturned on appeal?**
There is no single national figure, but state-level data is encouraging: in Pennsylvania roughly 65 percent of appealed claims end favorably for the worker, and in California the figure is around 58 percent. The strongest predictor is not the injury — it is whether the worker adds new medical evidence and files on time.

**Will workers' comp pay my medical bills while I appeal?**
Not until the denial is overturned. In the meantime, use your health insurance and keep every receipt — if you win the appeal, workers' comp reimburses those costs. Never stop treatment because the claim was denied.

**What if my employer fires me after I appeal?**
Most states prohibit firing or punishing a worker for filing or appealing a workers' comp claim. If it happens, document everything — the timing alone is powerful evidence — and raise it with your attorney, because retaliation can support a separate claim.

**How much does it cost to appeal a denied claim?**
Filing the appeal itself is usually free or a nominal fee. A workers' comp lawyer works on contingency, so there is typically no upfront cost, and you pay nothing if the appeal fails. The real cost of appealing is time: 3 to 12 months from filing to decision.

A denied claim is a setback with a clock attached to it, not a verdict. The workers who get paid after a denial are not the ones with the worst injuries — they are the ones who read the letter the day it arrived, got the doctor's letter, and filed before the deadline. Do those three things, and the math moves to your side.
