A DUI arrest doesn't end when the handcuffs come off. It starts a countdown — and the timer that matters most isn't your court date, it's a deadline most first-timers never hear about: the DMV hearing window, which in some states is as short as **7 days**. Miss it and your license is suspended automatically, whether you're guilty or not.

The first 72 hours after a DUI arrest are the highest-leverage hours of your entire case. What you do (and refuse to do) in that window affects your bail, your driving privileges, your evidence, and the price your lawyer charges. This checklist walks you through it hour by hour.

## Hours 0–6: at the jail — booking, tests, and your one phone call

**1. Stay silent — politely.** You've already been arrested, so the roadside stage is over. From this point, say only what's needed to identify yourself and comply with booking. The booking process (fingerprints, photo, property inventory) is administrative, but anything you volunteer — "I only had two beers," "I'm sorry" — goes into the report. Officers write down exact quotes. You have the right to remain silent; use it like it matters, because it does.

**2. Understand the breathalyzer-vs-blood math before you answer.** After arrest, the officer will ask you to submit to a chemical test — usually a breathalyzer or blood draw. Refusing feels powerful, but it triggers **implied consent** penalties: in most states, a first-time refusal adds an automatic license suspension (often 1 year) on top of whatever the DUI case brings, and prosecutors can use the refusal against you at trial ("an innocent person would have blown").

That said, there are narrow situations where lawyers advise refusal — for example, if you've already blown over twice and the number is far above the limit. This is exactly the kind of judgment call a [DUI attorney](/dui-attorney) makes for you in the first phone call. If you haven't hired one yet, the safest general rule for a first offense is: comply with the chemical test, say nothing else.

**3. Make your phone call count.** You usually get one. Don't call a friend to vent — call a [DUI attorney](/dui-attorney) or someone who can contact one for you. Many DUI lawyers offer free 24-hour consultations precisely because the first night matters. If you can't reach one, call whoever can post bail and ask them to start calling lawyers in the morning.

**4. Know the bail numbers.** DUI bail for a first offense typically ranges from **$500 to $2,500** depending on the state and county, paid to a bondsman at a 10% fee (so $50–$250 out of pocket if you use a bondsman) or the full amount directly to the court, refundable if you show up. Aggravating factors — an accident, a child in the car, a very high BAC, or a prior DUI — can push bail to $5,000–$25,000 or result in no bail until arraignment. You won't be held forever: for a standard first DUI, most people are out within 4–12 hours once sober enough for release.

## Hours 6–24: the DMV clock starts — this is the deadline nobody warns you about

**5. Mark your DMV hearing deadline on day one.** A DUI triggers **two separate cases**, and most people only prepare for one:

- **The criminal case** — the court date on your citation, where fines, jail, and probation are decided.
- **The administrative (DMV) case** — where your driver's license is decided, run by the DMV or a state hearings office, completely independent of the court.

The DMV case has its own deadline, and it's brutally short. You must **request a hearing within a set number of days or lose your license by default**:

| State | Deadline to request DMV hearing |
|---|---|
| California | 10 days |
| Texas | 15 days |
| Florida | 10 days |
| New York | typically 15 days (varies by notice) |
| Illinois | varies — check your notice immediately |
| Arizona | 15 days |

Check the paperwork you were given at release — the deadline is printed on it. If you miss it, the suspension is automatic. This is the single most common and most avoidable mistake after a first DUI. A lawyer can request the hearing for you, and winning (or delaying) it keeps you driving while the criminal case plays out.

**6. Do not drive until you know your license status.** In many states, the officer confiscates your license at arrest and hands you a temporary permit (often pink or yellow) valid for 30 days — or until the DMV hearing. Driving on a suspended license is a separate criminal charge that turns a bad situation dramatically worse. Read the temporary permit. Know its expiration date. Set a calendar reminder.

**7. Photograph and write down everything — now.** Memory degrades fast. Before 24 hours pass, write a private timeline: where you were, what and when you ate and drank (with amounts), what the officer said and did at each step, whether field sobriety tests were done on a slope or in bad lighting, whether the breathalyzer mouthpiece looked new, and whether you have a medical condition (acid reflux, diabetes, dental work) that can inflate BAC readings. Store it somewhere safe — email it to yourself. This document is the raw material your lawyer builds a defense from.

## Hours 24–72: hire your lawyer — and understand what you're really buying

**8. Hire a DUI specialist, not a generalist.** Your cousin's real-estate lawyer is the wrong call. DUI defense is a technical specialty — breathalyzer calibration records, blood-draw chain of custody, field sobriety test protocols, DMV hearing procedure. Ask every lawyer you interview: *how many DUI cases did you handle last year?* and *how many DMV hearings?* If the answer is vague, keep looking. **9. Understand the fee math.** Most DUI lawyers charge a flat fee for a first offense, typically **$1,500–$5,000** depending on your market, plus trial fees of $2,500–$10,000 if the case goes to trial. Here's what that fee buys you that a public defender (free, but juggling 200+ cases) often can't give: immediate DMV hearing filing, evidence preservation requests before dashcam footage is overwritten (many departments delete it in 30–90 days), and negotiation time with the prosecutor for a reduced charge.

**10. Do the total-cost math — a DUI is a five-figure event.** The lawyer's fee is only the down payment. A realistic first-DUI budget in most states:

| Cost | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Attorney flat fee | $1,500–$5,000 |
| Court fines and penalties | $500–$2,000 |
| DMV reinstatement fees | $100–$500 |
| DUI education / alcohol program | $300–$1,500 |
| Ignition interlock device (IID), 6–12 months | $1,000–$2,500 |
| SR-22 insurance filing | $25–$50 filing, but premiums jump |
| Insurance premium increase (3–5 years) | $3,000–$10,000 total |

**The honest total: $7,000–$20,000+ for a first offense**, with insurance being the silent killer — a DUI can double or triple your premium for 3–5 years. This is why fighting the charge (or getting it reduced to reckless driving) pays for itself: the difference between a DUI conviction and a reduction is often $5,000–$10,000 in insurance alone.

## The two-track timeline: what the next 6–18 months actually look like

**11. Learn the sequence so nothing blindsides you.** Here's the typical order of events after the first 72 hours:

1. **Arraignment (2–6 weeks out):** You enter a plea — almost always "not guilty" at this stage, which preserves your options. Your lawyer appears for you in many states.
2. **DMV hearing (within 30–90 days):** Your lawyer challenges the suspension. Win rates vary, but even a loss here is often structured so you keep driving with restrictions.
3. **Discovery and pretrial motions (2–6 months):** Your lawyer gets the police report, breathalyzer maintenance logs, and video; files motions to suppress bad evidence. This is where cases are won — calibration gaps, improper stops, and mishandled blood samples surface here.
4. **Plea negotiations (ongoing):** Most DUI cases (roughly 85–90%) end in a plea — often to a reduced charge like reckless driving ("wet reckless") with lighter penalties.
5. **Trial (6–18 months out, if it gets there):** Rare, expensive, and sometimes worth it when the evidence is genuinely weak.

Notice: the criminal case is a marathon, but the DMV case is a sprint. That's why the 72-hour window matters — it covers the sprint.

## What NOT to do in the first 72 hours

**12. Don't post about it.** Not a joke tweet, not a "long night lol" story, not a cryptic Facebook post. Prosecutors and insurance companies check social media. Delete nothing either — deleting posts after arrest can look like destroying evidence. Just go quiet.

**13. Don't miss your arraignment.** Failure to appear triggers a bench warrant, and judges remember it at sentencing. If your lawyer is retained, they can often appear for you and waive your personal appearance — confirm this in writing.

**14. Don't assume a first offense is "no big deal."** Even a first DUI can mean: up to 6 months in jail (usually suspended), 3–5 years of probation, a suspended license for 6 months to a year, mandatory alcohol classes, community service, and a criminal record that shows up on background checks. "First offense" means lighter than a second — it doesn't mean light.

**15. Don't drive drunk again — obviously, but with math.** A second DUI within 10 years (the "lookback period" in most states) multiplies everything: mandatory jail time (often 10 days to 1 year), fines up to $5,000, license revocation for 1–2 years, and in some states a felony charge. The lookback clock starts at your arrest date. One bad night is a setback; two is a life-altering pattern in the eyes of the court.

## Building your defense in week one: the evidence checklist

**16. Preserve what your lawyer needs.** In the week after the arrest, gather: the citation and all DMV paperwork; receipts from the bar or restaurant (timestamps prove the drinking timeline); your phone's location history for that night; the names of anyone who saw you that evening; dashcam or security-camera footage if the stop was near a business; and medical records for any condition affecting BAC. Tell your lawyer about prescription medications too — some (like certain inhalers and diabetes medications) can cause false high readings.

**17. Ask your lawyer about the breathalyzer's paper trail.** Breathalyzers require regular calibration — typically every 30–90 days depending on the state. A machine overdue for calibration, an officer without current certification, or a test administered too soon after the stop (mouth alcohol contamination) can get the number thrown out. This is the most common technical defense in DUI cases, and it's why specialists matter: a generalist won't know to subpoena the maintenance log.

**18. Consider the blood-draw option carefully going forward.** If your state allows a choice at the station, know that blood tests are more accurate but take weeks for results — and the sample can be independently retested by your own expert. Breath tests are instant but vulnerable to the calibration and mouth-alcohol challenges above. There's no universally right answer; there's only the right answer for your facts, which is a lawyer's call.

## The bottom line

The first 72 hours after a DUI arrest are about three things: **protect your license** (file that DMV hearing request before the deadline), **protect your evidence** (write everything down, preserve receipts and footage), and **protect your future** (hire a DUI specialist, not whoever answers first). The criminal case will take months; the decisions that shape it take days.

If you're reading this within hours of an arrest, start at step 5 — the DMV deadline — then work backward. Everything else can wait until morning. That deadline can't.
